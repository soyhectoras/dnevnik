# HOARD

## Chapter One — The Priest's Smile

*Bakugan (Gen 3) fanfiction · dark · horror · mystery · primal*

---

The cold found me before my back foot had left the warm side of the world.

Behind me the seam still stood open in the air, one vertical stroke of gold, taller than a door and thinner than a blade, humming like a wet finger on the rim of a wineglass. Through it, for a second longer, hung the evening I had just left: the lot behind a laundromat, streetlamps blinking awake, a dryer vent breathing warm lint into the dusk. A mild evening. The kind that lets you keep your hands out of your pockets.

In front of me, Iceland put its whole wet hand over my face.

Sleet is what weather does when it can't commit. It came off the sea sideways, in fistfuls, half water and half grievance, and before I'd turned up my collar it had found the gap and gone looking for my spine. My jacket is cropped. It stops at the ribs. It was designed by someone who had never been north of anything, and possibly by someone who wished me harm. Within seconds every silver spike on my shoulders wore a little white cap of ice, a row of tiny Alps. *Brrr.* I didn't say it out loud. The face doesn't allow it.

People hear *I hate the cold* and think I mean it the way they would, like hating traffic or cilantro. I don't. Other people come with their own heat. They carry it inside like a pilot light; stand them on a glacier and they'll go on burning for hours out of sheer biological stubbornness. I have never once been warm from the inside. Warmth is something that happens to me — a radiator, a crowd, a mug held in both hands — and the cold knows it. It comes for me the way a bailiff comes for a man who's been living on credit: politely at first, and then for everything.

So I had perhaps a second and a half before Dan came through behind me, and I spent it on my face.

I set the smile the way you'd set a bone: one firm, practiced motion, and then you leave it alone. Mouth soft. Brow untroubled. Eyes doing the warm thing, the thing that tells a person *I have all the time in the world for you.* A priest's smile, the kind worn at bedsides and baptisms and church bake sales — benevolent, unhurried, sincere. Sincerity is mostly a matter of muscle, and I keep mine in excellent condition.

In that wind, though, it felt fired on, like the glaze on a china saint in an unheated chapel, and I could feel exactly where it would craze first: a hairline from the corner of my mouth up toward the eye, and then the whole kind surface lifting away in one piece to show whatever was underneath. Bone, presumably. What else would be under a face.

It held. It always holds. A face that cracks is a face that loses, and I hadn't come this far to lose to the weather. Strategy is strategy. It's the only commandment I keep, and I keep it better than most people keep their ten.

Dan Kouzo came through the seam the way he came through everything, chest first and mouth already open.

"Whoa. Whoa, whoa, *whoa*. Kage, where *are* we?"

"Iceland," I said.

"Iceland?! For real?" He turned a full circle, sneakers skidding on the moss. "Like *Iceland* Iceland? Vikings? Volcanoes?"

"There are volcanoes."

*There's one under your sneakers,* I didn't say. You don't tell a kid that in the first five minutes. You save it for when he needs a reason to stay.

Drago rode through on his shoulder with his wings flared for balance, and the instant the wind struck him they changed shape: not folding but mantling, half-raised and hooded forward over his body and the side of Dan's head, a small crimson tent pitched against the weather. Sleet hit his back and went up hissing as steam. He arrived wreathed in it, nine inches of red scale in a private fog, a coal dropped into a snowbank that had decided, on principle, not to go out.

Then he lifted his chin and tasted the air.

I always watch dragons do this. The head rose and stilled. The nostrils flared and closed and flared again, a long slow inhale that took the wind apart and sorted its pieces — salt, sulfur, wet wool, the rubber of Dan's soles, Dan. Me. On me the inhale caught for a fraction of a beat. His pupils drew down to slits as fine as the eye of a needle, and from under his breastbone came a rumble pitched below the wind, felt more than heard, like an engine idling two streets away.

Behind Dan, the seam drew itself shut from both ends toward the middle, and the last of the laundromat went out with it, warm lint and all.

"It's *freezing*," Dan announced, delighted, as though he'd discovered it personally. His teeth were chattering, and he grinned through the chatter like it was a drumroll. His red jacket was zipped to the chin and doing nothing whatsoever. "Dude. How'd you even *do* that? The gold door thing?"

"My card."

"Can *my* card do that?"

"Not yet."

*Not ever, most likely.* But *not yet* is a door, and kids walk through doors.

"Why here," said Drago.

He didn't make it a question. Drago laid his words down one at a time, flat, like a man setting out the tools he intends to use.

"Because nobody looks here," I said.

That was true. I'm generous with the truth when it's cheap, and that one was nearly free.

We walked, and the moss took our footprints and gave them back slowly, as if thinking it over.

I had been looking for them for a while. That's the polite way to put it. The accurate way is three weeks and two days of chasing a rumor through the city, and the rumor was *a loud kid with a red dragon*. Do you know how many loud kids there are? Loud isn't a distinguishing feature in children. Loud is the factory setting. I'd chased a red dragon that turned out to be a Pyrus with a skin condition and a grudge, and one spray-painted on a storm culvert by someone who had never seen a dragon and held strong opinions about wings. I had sat in a bus shelter at two in the morning while a boy of about twelve described the loud kid to me for forty minutes, and by the end I was fairly sure he'd been describing himself.

I hate searching. Searching is just waiting with your feet. Give me a closed door and a clock and I can be as patient as a trap; give me a whole city and no edges, and patience curdles into something I don't like the taste of.

Then this evening, behind the laundromat, there they were: an arena folding itself back into a parking lot, a Haos kid sitting in a puddle looking betrayed by physics, and a boy in a red jacket punching the air while a crimson dragon smoked on his shoulder like a censer. *There's a place I'd like to show you,* I'd said. *The Dragon Clan's home.* Dan said *sure* before I reached the end of the sentence. Drago said nothing at all, which in my experience is the louder of the two answers.

The lava field ran out from under us to the end of sight: a frozen boil of black rock that had come up out of the earth a thousand years ago in one long incandescent scream and then simply stopped, mid-scream, and let itself be covered. The moss had done the covering. It lay over everything, thick as a pelt, gray-green going silver where the sleet caught it, so deep you could sink a hand to the wrist and never touch stone. Every jag and blister and burst bubble of that old violence had been upholstered. It looked soft. It looked like the fur on something that had died standing up.

To the east the ground smoked. White plumes lifted from fissures in the rock and lay down flat in the wind, and the smell came across with them: sulfur, a match that strikes and strikes and never finishes striking. Beyond, the land fell away to a beach of black sand, less a beach than a hole where one should have been, and past it the Atlantic hauled up its gray shoulders and threw them down again with the dumb persistence of a thing that has never once been told no.

The light was going. Not setting; going, draining out of the day the way heat drains out of a room when someone leaves the door open. Back in the city it had been evening. Here it was some hour the clocks would have called afternoon and the sky declined to honor.

I looked up anyway. The clouds were low and pewter-colored and moving fast, whole continents of them dragging east over our heads, tearing apart, knitting back, never once arranging themselves the same way twice. It was an ugly sky, mean and wet and low-browed. It was also not the same sky again, and I find there's very little I won't forgive a sky, on those terms.

Two ravens sat on a fence post by the road, shoulder to shoulder in their black, like undertakers waiting for the family to finish. Drago's head turned to follow them — the whole head, smooth as a turret on a bearing — and the ravens turned theirs to follow him, and for three long steps the three of them regarded one another with the professional interest of creatures who all eat meat. Then one of the ravens said something. It sounded like a door with a bad hinge.

Farther on, a scatter of sheep the autumn roundup had missed stood in the moss with sleet piling white on their backs, black-faced, motionless, watching us pass with the flat devotion of the damned. Drago's nostrils worked. The tip of his tail, which lay along Dan's shoulder blade, twitched once — the only part of him, I was beginning to notice, that didn't bother lying.

We crossed a road, two empty lanes of wet asphalt with a yellow line down the middle like a single stitch holding the landscape shut. At the verge stood a sign, the only upright thing for a kilometer that anyone had put there on purpose: a white rectangle, a town name in letters that looked like ordinary letters after a bar fight, a number. I'm fond of road signs.

And far down the road, where it bent around a shoulder of lava, a gas station sign was burning.

It was a bead of blue and red no bigger than a fingernail, but it was *electric* — lit from inside, steady, absurd against all that dark — and my whole body tilted toward it like a plant toward a window. Somebody had taught lightning to sit still in a plastic box and spell out the price of diesel! I'm not over that. I don't intend to be. There'd be a fridge in there, humming, and I wanted it and everything in it with the clean, total greed of a child with his nose to the glass.

"Is there food there?" Dan had followed my eyes. "I'm *starving*."

"There's food where we're going."

*The sign was mine, though. I'd come back for the sign.*

"You've been asking about us," Drago said. "For weeks."

"I have."

"Why?"

"Because you two were the best I'd heard of," I said, "and I wanted to see for myself."

Dan lit up like I'd plugged him into the wall. Drago didn't light up at all.

"Flattery."

"Observation."

Both, obviously. Flattery is just observation with somewhere to be.

"And this clan," Drago said. "You lead it."

"I'm the one who comes to find people."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No," I agreed, and smiled at him, and let the smile carry the rest.

Faces are my specialty. It's why I'm the one out in the weather.

He kept watching me — not the way Dan did, eagerly, waiting for the next good thing, but the way a sentry watches a road. He held his nine inches like a rank: the head carried just so, the stillness of something trained to stand at attention long after anyone stopped calling it to. Pride, then. And under the pride, something that had broken once and been set badly and healed crooked. I didn't know what had broken. I didn't need to. You don't need the history of a limp to know which leg to kick.

"So it's all dragons?" Dan said. "Your whole clan?"

"Dragons belong with dragons." I said it gently, the way you'd say grace.

"That's *awesome*. Drago, you hear that? A whole clan of you!"

"I heard him," Drago said.

Dan wanted three things, and he wore them on the outside like the zipper pulls on his jacket. He wanted real brawls — not hit-and-run scraps behind a laundromat with one ear out for sirens, but brawls with stakes in them, with someone watching who mattered. He wanted to belong to something bigger than himself and Drago, which he would never say aloud, because saying it would feel like a betrayal. And he wanted, badly, for someone older to look at him and see a brawler instead of a kid.

I'm eighteen, which from where Dan stood counted as older. It's one of the few things about me that is exactly what it looks like.

I could give him all three. The first two I could even give him for free.

"One more question," Drago said.

"Ask."

"Where is your partner? Three weeks you walked the city asking after dragons, and nobody who told us about you mentioned one on your shoulder."

"He's at home," I said. "He'd rather introduce himself."

"Would he."

The rumble again, lower, and a thread of smoke unspooled from one of his nostrils and was torn away by the wind. I thought it was the most interesting thing I'd seen all day, and I'd seen Iceland.

The fire station squatted where the road gave up and became gravel: a block of concrete the color of a wet tooth, two stories at most, hunched against the wind as if it had been punched there and was still deciding whether to get up. Someone had painted it red once, with conviction. The weather had been taking the paint back ever since, in strips and scabs, so that the walls looked less weathered than flayed. Two bay doors faced the road, rusted shut, orange runs bleeding from their hinges down to the gravel. Above them, in letters that had gone the shade of old piano keys, a sign: SLÖKKVISTÖÐ.

I'd looked it up, the first time. Fire station; more literally, I'm told, *quenching-station*, a place where fires are put out. I enjoy that more every time, given what lives here.

Behind the building rose the hose tower, a narrow concrete column where they'd once hung the hoses to drain, windowless and dead, raised against the sky like a finger to the lips. On its top a weathervane squealed every time the wind changed its mind, a thin metal shriek like something small being stepped on. It had lost its N and its S, so it could tell you which way the wind was blowing but not where that left you.

"This is it?" Dan said.

He'd stopped walking, and for the first time since the seam his voice had something in it besides delight — not suspicion, he didn't have the equipment, but the flat small sound of a kid who'd been promised a castle and handed a garage.

"This is the door," I said. "Not the place."

I'd had the same reaction my first time. I'd had a better poker face about it.

Drago lifted off Dan's shoulder.

There was no wind-up. One instant he was crouched there, mantled; the next he hung in the air at the height of my eyes on short hard wingbeats that slapped the sleet to vapor. This close I could feel him — real heat, dry heat, going straight past my skin to the part underneath that is always cold. Small and furious and steaming, he was almost cute, the way a lit fuse is almost cute. I wanted to put both hands around him like a mug. He would have taken a finger. Possibly the hand.

"Dan trusts fast," he said.

"Then it's lucky he has you."

He looked at me for a long moment with his pupils thin as cuts. It is very hard to argue with someone who agrees with you. I recommend it.

"Hey," Dan said. "Drago. Come on. It's *fine*."

Drago went back to his shoulder, slowly, and folded his wings without taking his eyes off mine.

The side door was steel. Beside it hung a keypad bleached by summers and split by frosts, two of its buttons pried out to leave square sockets like pulled teeth, a single cut wire dangling underneath. A very convincing broken keypad. I've been told it really is broken. I've never asked who broke it.

I took out my card. Black, edged with gold, slim as a communion wafer and about as easy to explain, and warm. It's the only thing I own that is warm all by itself, and I carry it against my ribs the way people in old paintings carry relics. My fingers had stopped being fingers somewhere around the ravens; they came back to me one by one, gathering around that small borrowed heat like animals around a fire.

I touched it to the keypad. Nothing lit. Somewhere deep in the wall, something unlocked with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

I pulled the door, and the hinge screamed — a long, rising, rusted scream that went up into the hose tower and came back down changed. Drago's head snapped toward it so fast I never saw it move. Up on its tower the weathervane answered, and for a moment the two of them sang together in the dusk, and then there was only the wind.

Inside, it was colder than out. That shouldn't be possible, and in abandoned buildings it always is; empty rooms hoard cold the way churches hoard echoes. Concrete on every side, gray going to black where the doorway's light gave out. It smelled of old rubber and diesel. A row of iron hooks ran along one wall, all empty but one, from which a single yellow helmet hung by its strap like a head nobody had come back to collect. A length of hose lay coiled on the floor, flattened and split along its seams like a shed skin.

And at the far end, where there should have been another steel door, or a cinder-block wall, or nothing, there was a door of carved dark wood.

It didn't belong to the building. It didn't belong to the country. It barely belonged to the century. It stood twice my height in a frame of concrete that had plainly been poured around it afterward, as though the station had been built to hide it, and every inch of it was worked — scallops and coils and long overlapping ranks of carved scales, the grain gone black with smoke, polished pale where hands had pushed it. Along the bottom, between the wood and the sill, lay a seam of gold light, thin as a blade: the same gold that had slit the evening open for us on the far side of the sea.

"Whoa," Dan whispered. It was the first time all evening I'd heard him whisper.

On his shoulder Drago had gone still. Not watchful, not wary: still, the whole-body stillness of something that has stopped being a passenger, and the rumble in his chest had stopped with it.

I walked to the door and laid my palm flat on the wood. It was warm under my hand, warm right through, the way a hearthstone stays warm for hours after the fire, and I felt my face, for the first time since the seam, remember that it was a face. The door gave. Without a sound it began to swing inward, slow and heavy, and from the widening gap the first breath of heat rolled out to meet us, and found me first.

It wasn't a draft. A draft is a thin, apologetic thing, a rumor of warmth under a door. This was weather. It came out of the gap in one slow swell, dry and heavy and sweet with cedar, and went into me through every opening the sleet had found — collar, cuffs, the place where my jacket gives up at the ribs — and it didn't stop at the skin. It went past the meat and the bone to the spot in the middle of me that nothing has ever managed to heat, and sat down there, and stayed.

I'm not religious. I've studied it, the way you'd study any language that works on people. But I know what pilgrims look like at the end of the road, when they come through the cathedral doors on their knees into the heat of ten thousand candles and simply fold, and in that doorway, every time, I understand them completely. The back of my neck let go. My eyes stung. Some idiot animal part of me wanted to kneel on the threshold, press its forehead to the warm wood, and say *thank you* to nobody in particular, at length, with feeling.

Then my face came back.

It came back the way feeling comes back into a foot you've sat on, all at once and full of needles. My lips remembered they had edges. And the smile, which I'd been holding up by main force since the seam, stopped needing me. It softened at the joints. For the first time since the laundromat it was something I was wearing rather than something holding me together, and I could have taken both hands off it and it would have stayed.

I took my hands off it. Only for a moment. Nobody looks at the man holding the door.

I stepped aside, because a door like that is wasted on whoever opens it, and let them see.

Beyond the threshold, three shallow steps went down into black stone veined with gold. Not inlaid: veined, the way marble is veined, except that these veins moved. Liquid light crept through the rock in slow branching rivers, dividing and finding one another again, so that the whole floor seemed to be quietly thinking. The floor ran away from us toward a hall whose far end I have never seen, because there has never been enough light to see it by. The place was built by creatures who see perfectly well in the dark and keep lamps the way other people keep flowers, for show, and it has never learned what daylight is.

Lanterns hung at every height, glass globes caged in filigree, some on chains so long they vanished upward. In each, gold light turned over on itself, slow and thick, like honey being stirred. No wick, no flame, no smoke. Stand close to one and you could hear it: a low hum just under hearing, a hive somewhere inside a wall.

Arches marched away on both sides, every one cusped — scalloped along its inner curve into small rounded lobes, as if something with a neat, patient mouth had eaten its way up one side and down the other. Between them stood pillars of dark wood thick as old trees, carved in coils and leaves and ranks of overlapping scales, with gold pressed into the deepest cuts so that the carving seemed to glow from its wounds. Dark red silk hung in heavy swags from the galleries above, the color of the inside of a mouth.

Deeper in, jali screens filled the arches, stone lattice cut so fine it looked woven, and the lanterns behind them laid lace across the floor, shadow and light, like a cloth shaken out and let fall. I've crossed that floor more times than I've counted, which says something, because I count most things, and I have never found two screens cut to the same pattern. Whoever carved them never once repeated himself. I respect that more than I can comfortably say.

And over everything, incense, climbing in gray threads from bronze burners until somewhere far up it gave up climbing and spread into a slow private sky that never rained. Sandalwood, cedar, cinnamon, hot stone, iron; and under it all, steady as the pedal note of an organ, the thread of sulfur that tells you where the warmth is really coming from.

The fire station is two stories. I've paced it in the gravel: forty-one of my steps by twenty-six. This vestibule alone could have swallowed it, hose tower and all. Physics, I assume, has been informed. Physics has declined to comment.

Somebody, at some point in deep time, looked at a perfectly serviceable cave and said: *No. More gold.*

Dragons and their relationship with grandeur and luxury, huh.

I live here.

I have a room here, up a staircase I could find in the dark, which is convenient, since the dark is all there is. And I still don't believe it. Every time I come through that door, some part of me waits, very calmly, for a hand on my shoulder and a polite voice explaining that there's been a mistake with my booking.

Dan came down the first step and stopped on the second, as if the third might not hold him.

"Dude," he said. Then, softer: "*Dude.*" He turned in place with his head tipped back and his mouth open, the way kids look at fireworks and grown men look at the bill. The gold in the floor swirled around his wet sneaker prints and closed over them. "You *live* here?"

"I do," I said, as though it were nothing.

*It isn't nothing. It will never be nothing.*

At the door, Drago had stopped being a passenger. In here he looked like something that had walked into a stranger's house and recognized the furniture. The short crest of horn that swept back from his brow lowered, plate by plate, flat to his skull. The tip of his tail lashed once across Dan's back, hard enough that Dan said *ow* without looking around. Then Drago lifted his chin and scented.

Out on the moss, his inhale had caught on me. Here it caught on nothing. It went on long past where a breath should end, as if he were drinking the room and finding every piece of it exactly where he'd left it. When it stopped, the rumble came up out of his chest with a crack down the middle, like a bell with a flaw.

"The Gilded Maw," he said.

Dan stopped turning. "The gilded what?" He twisted to look at his partner, nearly nose to snout. "Drago? You know this place?"

Drago's eyes went down the line of arches one by one, the way an officer's eyes go down a rank, and I had the impression he was counting them, and already knew the number.

"Not this one," he said. "The one it was copied from. On Vestroia."

"You've *been* there?"

"I served there."

"Served? Like — in an army?"

"Like that."

That was all.

*Served.* Past tense, and a word with a uniform on. I knew whose house this was. I hadn't known it had a previous address, or that the previous address had once had Drago in it, standing very straight. I kept it off my face and filed it with the other things that might be worth something later.

"It's a long walk to anywhere in here," I said gently. "Let me show you around."

I took them left under the first great arch, and Drago's head turned left a full second before I did. At the next bend it happened again, his whole head swinging into a turn the corridor hadn't admitted to yet, and this time his tail came off Dan's shoulder blade, wound around Dan's collar, and pulled tight, like a bandage on something that had started to bleed. It cost him. He had no face of the right kind to hide that with, and the tail has never learned to keep a secret.

The sparring corner opens off that passage: a ring sunk three steps into the floor and filled with black sand, fine as ground pepper and warm — I took my boots off in it once when nobody was looking and stood there until my feet forgave me for everything. Around the rim stand posts of dark wood, every one of them scored, gouged white to the heartwood in parallel furrows, four at a time, at heights no human hand would bother with. Some of the furrows have gone gray and smooth. Some still smell of sap.

Two dragons were in the ring, small form, neither longer than my forearm: one the dull violet of a plum gone over, one the raw copper of new pipe. They didn't circle; circling is for animals that need time to think. They stood at opposite sides of the sand in perfect stillness, and then they were in the middle, and nobody saw the part between. They went at each other like knives thrown from opposite ends of a room — struck, glanced, spun off in sprays of black, struck again — and the only sounds were the clack of teeth closing on nothing and the hiss of sand coming down. The copper one came off a post with a noise like a hatchet sinking into a stump and left four new white lines behind.

"Oh, *man*." Dan grabbed the rail. "Drago, look at them go — Kage, can we go in?"

"Whenever you like," I said. "The sand's always warm."

*Whenever you like* was charity. The violet one was missing a strip of scale along its jaw roughly the shape of the copper one's mouth, and neither seemed to think that worth stopping for.

The gym is next door, if *door* is the word for an arch you could sail a ferry through. Chains hang out of the dark, each link as thick as my thigh, ending in hooks that could lift a truck by its axle. Under them sit the weights: blocks of black iron the size of small cars, each with one grip worn bright as a doorknob, and one with a dent in its corner unmistakably the shape of a heel. Along the back wall a cliff of raw basalt climbs out of sight, its holds not bolted on but bitten in.

And in among it all, like dollhouse furniture left in a cathedral, the human equipment: a rack of dumbbells graded like organ pipes, a heavy bag patched with silver tape, three treadmills in a row. I love the treadmills. A machine that lets you walk for an hour and arrive exactly where you started — people built that on purpose, and pay for the privilege. I've done my share of walking in circles. I just like to look at them.

A few of our brawlers were in. A girl about my age hung off one of the chains with her whole weight while her dragon, in avatar, hauled the other end up hand over hand and laughed at her.

And near the dumbbell rack, a boy of maybe sixteen sat on a bench with a towel around his neck, not training. His dragon stood behind him in avatar — tall, gray-green, horns swept flat along the skull — closer than a spotter would stand, closer than anyone needs to, one long hand resting on the back of the boy's neck, at the nape, where the hair was cut short. The boy was leaning back into it, eyes half shut, with the drowsy, loose-limbed contentment of someone sitting in the last sun of the year. Around his shoulders the air shivered faintly, a heat-shimmer clinging to him the way it clings above a road in August.

Dragon warmth, obviously. Stand close to one long enough and you start giving it off yourself, secondhand, like a stone left in a fire. I felt something about that which, in a less dignified person, would have been called envy.

*Some partners are clingy.*

I moved us along. Dan hadn't noticed; Dan had both arms around one of the iron weights like a long-lost uncle and was trying, with total commitment, to move it. It didn't move. It didn't notice him either.

The research lab is the only room in the palace lit from below. Down a short stair, the floor is all consoles — slabs of black glass set in carved lecterns, gold light moving under the glass in slow currents, a script I can't read, writing and unwriting itself. At the center, in a cradle of light, a bond card hung on its edge and turned while a beam fine as a hair ran along its rim like a needle reading a record. I put a hand to my ribs without meaning to, over my own.

Above the benches hung the glyphs: green for Health, blue for Speed, the deep red-gold of Strength, each the size of a dinner plate, unfolding their geometry and folding it away like flowers trained to open on a timer. Shelves of specimen jars lined the walls — a curl of shed horn in amber fluid, a tooth as long as my hand, and in the biggest jar something folded in on itself that I've never asked about and have privately decided is a wing. In glass-topped drawers, scales lay pinned in rows by color like a collector's butterflies.

Three human technicians in dark aprons worked the consoles. They didn't look up. They never look up. I once knocked a tray of glassware off a bench in here, and the nearest one stepped over the pieces without breaking stride, and I've been fond of him ever since.

Dan went off like a car alarm.

"Wait. *Wait.* Is that a B.A.M. Zone? You can *study* them? You can just *have* them, in a *room*?" He was already reaching for the red-gold glyph. "Can you *make* them?"

"We can read them," I said. "Making them is harder."

I caught his wrist an inch from the light. Gently; I'm always gentle. "I wouldn't."

"Why not?"

"It isn't finished."

*And I have no idea what it does, and I'd rather find out on someone I didn't spend three weeks looking for.*

Drago wasn't looking at the glyphs. He was looking at the drawers of scales, for a long time, and the lip on one side of his muzzle came slowly up off his teeth and stayed up.

Past the lab the passage bends right — Drago's head went first; I was watching for it now — and opens into a gallery with almost nothing in it, which in this palace is a statement. No silk, no screens, a single lantern. And at the far end, a door.

It made the carved door at the fire station look like something you'd keep brooms behind. Dark wood gone nearly black, banded with broad straps of gold worked in coils that slid under one another and surfaced where they had no business surfacing. No handle. No keyhole. Nowhere to touch a card. I've tried, more than once; it has shown me all the interest a mountain shows a moth.

And at its seams, the air moved. A long warm exhale came through the cracks and lifted the fringe off my forehead, smelling of loam and smoke and spice and something I couldn't name. Then it stopped. After a long while it drew back in, tugging at the hem of my jacket. Out. Rest. In. Rest. Slow and even, like something very large asleep on the other side.

Convection, I tell myself. Hot depths, cool gallery. Air does that. I stood in the exhale anyway and let it warm my face. It's the warmest spot in the palace that isn't on fire.

Drago's wings came up, mantled, hooded forward over the side of Dan's head the way they had against the sleet, though there was nothing here to shelter Dan from but warm air. He made no sound at all.

"Everything you've seen tonight," I said, "is about one percent of the MOD."

"One *percent*?"

"Give or take."

"What's in the rest?"

"The depths. They're closed to outsiders. Of course."

*Of course.* They're closed to me too; I didn't see why Dan needed to know that. Said in the right voice, *of course* lets you stand in front of any door in the world as if the key were in your other pocket.

Behind the gold-bound wood, the slow thing breathed out again, and the warmth of it went over all three of us like a hand.

The lounge is where I take everyone at the end, the way a good sermon ends on the part about forgiveness.

It's lower than the other rooms and smaller, which here means a modest church would fit. Divans run around the walls, upholstered in dark red silk gone soft as skin, their arms carved into dragons' heads with open mouths, cushions heaped on them in such drifts that sitting down is less an action than a negotiation. In the center a bronze brazier stood on three clawed feet, its coals breathing orange and black. Beside it, on a low table, someone had already laid out food and tea. I've never arrived here to find it not ready. I have never seen who readies it. I've decided not to need to know.

Dan saw the food and made a noise I can only call devout: flatbread blistered from the oven, smoked lamb sliced paper-thin, dates, figs, something lacquered in honey and spice that smelled as if the incense had decided to become dinner. *There's food where we're going*, I'd told him. I like keeping the promises that cost me nothing. It builds credit for the other kind.

He went face-first into the cushions and came up with flatbread in each hand.

I took the divan nearest the brazier — I'd sit in it if they let me — and poured. A gold pot with a spout like a long arched neck. Gold cups, thin as eggshell. And beside each cup, on its own square of silk, a gold spoon.

Here's the thing about the spoons.

I've held one. It's heavier than a spoon has any right to be — forty grams, call it, maybe forty-five — with that buttery, sullen weight I am almost completely sure means solid gold. A tiny dragon curls around the end of the handle, biting its own back. If it's gold, then one spoon is — I've done the arithmetic. On the walk back from the city, in the bath, at three in the morning, in two currencies. One spoon is a winter coat that actually reaches my hips. Six spoons is a car with heated seats. The whole table is a small warm island somewhere south, with my name on a sign at the jetty.

I'd be rich!!!

I've thought about the logistics of taking one. A spoon fits up a sleeve. A spoon fits in a boot. There's a gold-buyer in Reykjavík, near the harbor, with a sign in four languages and a man behind the counter whom nothing has surprised since about 1990. I've rehearsed it. *It was my grandmother's.* Whose grandmother? *A very old lady.* Where did she get it? *She had a lot of spoons.* No hallmark, naturally. He'd look at the dragon, and at me, and at the dragon, and I would smile, and I'm fairly confident about the smile.

And then I imagine the morning after.

Because dragons count their spoons. Dragons count everything. They don't keep things; they hoard them, which is keeping with teeth in it, and somewhere in this palace there is someone who knows exactly how many gold spoons there are, has always known, and would know within the hour that there was one fewer. I've never met whoever counts the spoons. I've never wanted to.

I stirred my tea with mine and laid it back on its silk, exactly square.

Dan had sunk so far into the cushions that only his knees and face showed, cheeks full, a gold cup cradled in both hands like a medal. "This," he said thickly, "is the best place I've *ever* been."

Drago didn't sit.

Dan patted the cushion beside him, and Drago looked at it the way you'd look at a bribe. The cup I'd poured him steamed, untouched. He went instead to the carved arm of Dan's divan, the dragon's head with its mouth open, and perched there with his talons hooked over its wooden teeth. He didn't fold his wings. He kept them half-mantled, the heat coming off him in a faint ripple that bent the lantern light. His tail hung straight down, perfectly still, which was worse than lashing.

He turned his whole head toward me — not the eyes; the head, the way dragons do once they've decided what they're looking at — and the brazier got into his eyes and stayed there, and his pupils thinned to slivers, and he drew breath.

"Every Bakugan in this MOD — are they all Dragon types?"

He didn't put it to the room. He put it to me. Dan had a gold cup in one fist and a honey cake in the other and was working through both as though the tray might be repossessed, and Drago had long since stopped pretending to look at anything but my face. He sat on the carved dragon's head at the end of the divan's arm, beside Dan's shoulder, all four feet gripping, his talons hooked over its wooden teeth and sunk into the grain, so that the old carving was acquiring new marks: pale crescents in the dark wood, one under each claw. His wings stood half-raised and hooded forward, the posture of a man who has risen halfway out of his chair and not yet decided whether he's leaving. In the lantern light he'd stopped looking red. He looked like the inside of a coal, the part you aren't supposed to touch.

From my divan the brazier was close enough to touch, its coals breathing orange through a skin of ash, and I could feel it on the left side of my face like an attentive friend. The tea was black and smelled of smoke with something sweet underneath, cinnamon perhaps, or something that had been cinnamon in a previous life. I held the cup by its body rather than its handle, so the heat could get at my palms. Gold conducts. It stung. I let it.

"Yes," I said. "This is the home of the Dragon Clan."

I said it the way you'd tell someone the name of the church they were already standing in: a courtesy, not news.

"Clan," Drago said.

He scented the word — chin lifting a fraction, nostrils working — as though *clan* had been carried into the room on somebody's clothes and he was trying to work out where they'd been.

"When brawling was outlawed," I said, "the Bakugan didn't stop being what they are. They stopped being allowed to be it in the open. And when you can't stand in the open, you go looking for someone to stand next to." I turned the cup a quarter-turn on my palm. "Bakugan of the same type started finding each other. Then staying. After a while it needed a name."

That's the version for guests. It has a beginning and an end and a modest moral, and nobody in it gets hurt.

Dan swallowed a mouthful of cake without, as far as I could tell, chewing it. "Wait. *Same type?* So there's more? Other clans?"

"Six, that I know of." I counted them off on my free hand, unhurried, like a catechism. "The Insect Clan. The Dino Clan. Aquatic. Mammal. Avian. And us. Each with a MOD of its own."

Surprise looks strange on a dragon. It can't go to the eyebrows; there aren't any. It goes to the pupils, which in Drago's case went from slits to coins in a single blink, and to the wings, which forgot for an instant to stay mantled and sagged half an inch before he caught them. Something had been tallied in his head and come out wrong.

"Each with its own," he said.

"I've never visited the others." I'd heard things, though, secondhand and improved in transit. The Insect Clan's MOD hums in the walls day and night, I'd been told — or else it doesn't, and the hum is in the people who've been. The Aquatic Clan keeps a front door you'd have to hold your breath to knock on. I discount a rumor in proportion to how good a story it makes, and those are very good stories. "They're different from us. They're different from each other. They don't agree about much. But every one of them believes the authorities were wrong to take brawling away. So none of them are doing as they're told."

"So it's like a rebellion," Dan said, and his eyes went bright, because he was at the age where *rebellion* is a word that comes with a cape.

"It's more like nobody listening."

A law is something one animal says to another while it's still holding the bigger stick. Somebody wrote *no brawling* on a sheet of paper, and signed it, and waited for creatures that can stand taller than office blocks to read it and comply. The Bakugan read it. Then they went on being the size of office blocks, only more quietly, and farther north.

Dan's pocket chirped. He dug out his phone, frowned at a red sliver of battery, and shoved it back. For the three seconds it was lit I couldn't look at anything else: a small cold rectangle of blue in a room built entirely of gold, and it knew what time it was in his city and exactly how much of itself was left. I'd have traded the tea for it. Possibly the divan.

Drago hadn't looked at the phone. Drago hadn't looked away from me since the door.

"Six," he said.

Smoke came out with the word, one gray thread from the corner of his mouth that hung in the warm air and declined to go anywhere.

"There were six planets, once. Six worlds. Six kinds." His voice had gone somewhere farther off than the room. "And a war—"

That was where it stopped. It didn't trail off; it stopped, clean, the way a road stops at a cliff. His jaw shut on the rest with a small click of tooth meeting tooth. The tip of his tail, which had found its way back to Dan's shoulder blade as though it kept an address there, pressed flat against the red jacket and stayed.

Dan twisted to look up at him. "Drago?"

"It was a long time ago."

He did not say who won.

I've heard a great many people tell their wars — listening is one of the things my face does best — and there are only two ways to do it. Winners lead with the ending: *we won*, first thing, and the rest is a drumroll. Losers lead with everything else — the odds, the weather, the traitor — and arrive at the ending last and loudest, so you'll understand it wasn't fair. Drago did neither. He named the war and stepped over the rest the way a soldier steps over something lying in the road: carefully, without looking, which is its own kind of looking.

I put that away in the place where I keep such things. It's a large place. It always has room.

I knew a dragon who would have finished that sentence. Who would have finished it, and then begun it again from the top, slower, with gestures.

"That's why the Clans make sense to me," I said, gently, into the hole Drago had left. "A Bakugan belongs with its own kind. Fire understands fire. Among dragons, a dragon never has to explain himself — not his heat, not his temper, not the way he looks at a door before he walks through it." I let my eyes rest on his claws in the carving for exactly one beat, and then moved them away, politely, as if I hadn't. "Everyone here already knows."

A rumble started in Drago's chest, so low I felt it through the divan before I heard it.

"And you," he said. "What kind are you?"

"Human," I said. "Last I checked."

I check fairly often.

"There's this crew back home, though," Dan said, through crumbs. "They've got, like, a Ventus and an Aquos and a Haos all on one team, and they're actually kind of—"

"I know the sort." I smiled at him with enormous tenderness. "They're brave. I don't doubt it for a second. But it's a joke, Dan. The kind you tell about people you like. Nobody on a team like that knows what the one beside them is *for*."

Did I believe it? I said it beautifully. I believe it the way I believe in my face: completely, for as long as it's on.

Dan chewed on that, along with the rest of the cake. Then he looked around the lounge — properly, as though the whole palace were a shop he'd been let into after closing: the cushions stitched with gold thread into scales, the jali screen beside the archway laying its lace of shadow on the carpet — and back at me.

"So are you, like, the boss here?"

"I'm the Clan's leader."

He nodded, satisfied. Kids like a straight answer, and they can't tell one from a precise one.

"Not its founder," I added.

I hadn't needed to say it. I said it the way you leave a door ajar in a house you'd like somebody to explore. The words went past Dan without touching the sides; his attention had already slid on toward the cakes.

They didn't get past Drago. His head made one small movement, a quarter-inch tilt, and his pupils drew down on me. He'd heard the shape of the space where a name should have been. I watched him decide not to ask for it, and I watched what the deciding cost him, and I liked him a little better for paying.

Some names are like the good china. You don't bring them out until the guests have agreed to stay for dinner.

So: dinner.

I set my cup down, one small clean click of gold on wood. Then I leaned forward with my elbows on my knees and my hands loosely laced and lowered my voice — not to a whisper, just below where it had been. A lowered voice is a secret, and a secret is a gift, and nobody refuses a gift in front of the person giving it.

"Can I tell you why I really brought you here?"

"To show off?" Dan grinned.

"A little." Give him that. A confession you can afford buys you the expensive ones later. "Mostly, I came to ask you to join us."

He stopped chewing.

"How often do you brawl, Dan? Really brawl." I let him open his mouth and went on before anything came out, gently, as though I already knew the answer and was sorry about it. "Behind a laundromat. In a parking lot. With one ear on the street the whole time, waiting for a siren."

I'd watched him this evening. Twice, mid-brawl, his eyes had flicked to the mouth of the alley. Kids think nobody sees them check the exits. Everybody sees. Only some of us take notes.

"Here, you'd brawl every day. Every day, and nobody comes."

His face did something — opened, a little, at the edges. Button one.

"And Drago." I didn't look at Drago when I said his name. I looked at Dan, because you never sell a kid on what he'll get; you sell him on what his partner deserves. "When did you last see him at his real size for longer than a few minutes? Before the arena folds up and somebody's mother calls the police?" I opened one hand toward the palace around us, all of it, the parts he'd seen and the parts he never would. "We have an arena where he can be what he is for as long as the brawl lasts. Nobody shrinks him back to nine inches because the neighbors are looking."

On the arm of the divan, the tip of Drago's tail lifted off Dan's back and came down again, once, hard enough that I heard it.

Button two. It's the biggest one there is, and it never wears out.

"And nobody here," I said, "is going to treat you like a kid."

I used the word on purpose, so he could hear it refused. It did its work on the way past. His shoulders came up and back, his chin lifted, and for one second he sat on that divan like somebody much older, called to a table and finding a chair with his name already on it.

Button three. I didn't lean on it. You never lean on the one that's working.

"When you win here," I said, "people see it. People who know what they're looking at. And when you lose, somebody helps you up, because next week you'll help them. That's what a Clan is." I sat back and unlaced my hands. "That's what we are."

*You* to *we*, between one sentence and the next, the way you change lanes on an empty road. Nobody hears it. Everyone feels it.

"Who's *we*?" Dan said. "Like — who else?"

Exactly the right question, and he'd found it all by himself. I could have applauded.

I tipped my head toward the far wall.

It ran the length of the lounge, dark wood in tall narrow panels, and set into it in neat ranks, eight rows deep, were plates of gold, each the size of a playing card, each engraved. Lantern light lay along the plates and made them look wet. Brazier ash had drifted over the years into a fine gray bloom on the wood between them, the way soot settles in a chapel where the candles are never allowed to go out.

"Everyone who trains here," I said.

Dan was off the divan before I'd finished, cup still in his hand, slopping. Drago went with him — one short hard wingbeat from the divan arm to his shoulder, landing heavy, claws set — and the two of them stood before the wall while Dan read the names out loud. He read them the way children read any wall with letters on it, as though it had been put up for him personally.

"Sólveig Arnardóttir. Marcus Bell. Hana Mori. Diego Ferrer. Tobias Lund — whoa, there's a *ton*. Priya Okafor. Jonah Reyes. Ines—" He moved down a row, tracing with one sticky finger an inch from the gold, not quite daring to touch, and then he stopped, because the next plate wasn't there.

In the fourth row, and again two rows down, the pattern broke. Where a plate should have been there was only bare wood, a rectangle dark and clean and sharp-cornered in the ash: a shadow in reverse, the one place on that wall the ash had never been allowed to touch.

"What happened to these two?"

"People quit," I said. "Brawling's illegal. Somebody's parents find out, somebody gets scared, somebody's family moves away." I shrugged with one shoulder, the gentlest shrug I own.

I like a pattern. I like it better when a pattern does something new. This wasn't new, only missing — two teeth gone from a smile — and the gaps bothered me for exactly as long as it took me to notice they bothered me, and then I put that away too. Someone would screw new plates into those spaces. That's what spaces are for.

"Look," I said. "There's room."

Dan looked at the clean rectangle in the fourth row as if he could already read his own name in it.

On his shoulder, Drago wasn't looking at the gaps at all. He was looking at me, and his nostrils were working, slow and steady — tasting the room the way he'd tasted the wind outside, sorting it, and getting the same answer twice.

"Every *day*," Dan said. It came out of him half shout, half laugh, at the volume he usually saved for the second before he called an ability. He turned on the spot and tea slopped over the rim onto the carpet, and he didn't notice, and neither did the carpet, which had plainly survived worse. "Drago! Did you hear him? Every day! A real arena! Full size! Nobody calling the cops, nobody—"

"Dan." Drago didn't raise his voice. He lowered it, which in a dragon is worse. "We should talk about this. In private."

"We can talk after—" Dan was already crossing the carpet toward me, cup abandoned on the low table, his right hand out and wiping itself on his jeans as it came — "we're *in*!"

Drago hissed.

It was water flung on a hot pan, short and violent, and it came with smoke — two threads from his nostrils this time and a third from between his teeth — and with it his tail came off Dan's back and lashed once across the red jacket, a whip-crack of scale on nylon. His wings snapped open in a single beat I felt on my face, hot and dry as the breath from an opened oven, and the nearest lantern swung on its chain and swept all our shadows up the wall and back down.

And Dan, who would have walked into a volcano for that dragon, said "*Drago*, come on," in exactly the voice you'd use on a dog that doesn't want its bath, and held his hand out to me anyway.

I took it.

I always notice the heat first. His hand was warm — dry and steady, generated on site, a small furnace that had been running without help since the day he was born, and he'd never once given it a thought. Then the stickiness: honey from the cakes, a fingertip he'd licked and missed. Then the size of it, smaller than mine, the bones under the skin still deciding what they meant to be. Then the grip, much too hard, the way somebody once told him a real man shakes hands. A pulse knocked in the heel of his palm, quick and light, a moth against a window. I held on exactly as long as a decent person holds a hand, and not a moment longer, though I wanted the heat. I always want the heat.

Contract signed. Witnessed by a dragon. No take-backs.

Every child knows that law. It's older than the one against brawling, and far better enforced.

Dan let go and grinned at me with cake on his teeth. I grinned back: the small, approved one, all warmth and no canine.

Then the palace breathed.

Every lantern in the lounge dimmed at once. Not a flicker; flickers are accidents, and this had a tempo. The gold sank in the glass, slowly and all together, down and down to the color of embers, until the room went brown and close and the brazier was suddenly the brightest thing in it. It held there at the bottom. Through the archway, as far down the corridor as I could see, every lantern had sunk with it.

Then, as slowly, they came back up.

I counted. I always count. Four heartbeats down, three held, four up — the same as the last time I'd seen it, and the time before that. It's the one repetition in this building I have never minded, because I know what it means. Somewhere in all this carved wood and gold, somebody had turned his attention toward the lounge, the way a big animal lying in the sun turns one ear toward a footstep without troubling to open its eyes.

Drago felt it. Whether he knew what it meant I couldn't tell, but his body did. His wings folded flat. His claws drew in. He lowered himself along Dan's collar until he was the shape of a red scarf with eyes, and those eyes went up, to the ceiling, to the nearest lantern, and stayed there, pupils thin as wire.

"Whoa," Dan said. "Did the lights just—"

"Then there's someone you should meet," I said. "Our founder."

I stood. I smoothed my jacket down over my ribs with both hands and felt the card under the leather, warm, keeping its counsel. Then I set the smile, gently, all the way to the eyes — the one that goes with long aisles and good news delivered quietly — and held out an open hand toward the archway, palm up, the way you'd show a congregation to its pews.

Dan went first. Of course he did. Drago rode him in silence.

I followed them out of the lounge and into the long corridor, and the heat met us at the threshold and went on climbing. Smoke hung in the air in slow blue layers, cedar and something underneath the cedar, and our footsteps ran ahead of us into the dark between the lanterns. Far down at the end of it, past more lanterns than even I have managed to count, the great doors stood waiting — so far off they looked small, which they were not — and still closed.

Nobody who had ever had to walk would have built that corridor.

It ran toward the heart of the palace for a distance I've never managed to count past four hundred, tall enough for wings, its black floor polished so deep that our reflections went along beneath us, upside down and slightly late, like the drowned keeping pace. Dragons don't walk; guests do, and the walking is the point: by the far end you've had plenty of time to consider who's waiting there.

The heat climbed as we went, as if the corridor were keeping score. Braziers stood in niches along both walls, iron cradles of coals that glowed from the inside out like honey kept at a simmer, and their smoke rose to lie under the ceiling in long gray strata, the sediment of a fire that has never once been allowed to go out. Cedar. Cinnamon. Hot iron. And under it all, faint as an old grudge, sulfur.

I loved it. Earlier that evening Iceland had been trying to take my face off; now every step put another degree into me, and I took each one the way a drunk takes a refill, and held out the glass. If I'm ever buried, I'd like it to be here, standing up, about two-thirds of the way along.

"It's like a *sauna*," Dan said, flapping the halves of his unzipped jacket, which helped about as much as fanning a bonfire.

"It gets warmer."

Dan's stomach weighed in, long and plaintive, and the stone carried it a surprising distance. "Is the founder guy gonna have food?"

"You could ask him."

*Please do. I'd like to see his face.*

The walls were carved, one frieze at eye height from end to end: Vestroian history told the way winners tell it — in high relief, in gold leaf, and at length. Dragons poured from a burning sky in ranks so orderly they looked woven. A planet of hives split like a seed pod. A sea boiled in its basin, and the carver had taken the trouble to give every fish in it an open mouth. Horned herds ran burning through a burning forest. Birds fell, hundreds of them, each feathered by some patient hand. Great scaled brutes knelt in chains with their foreheads in the dust. Six planets, six wars. The gold leaf was thickest on the fire.

Drago read it, the whole skull tracking the frieze panel by panel, the way a man reads a casualty list for a name he already knows is on it: not hoping, only checking how they spelled it. At the falling birds his tail tip, the honest part of him, clenched against Dan's shoulder blade, and Dan winced without knowing why.

Then we came to the last panel, beside the doors, and he stopped reading.

It was the largest carving in the corridor and the only one with nothing burning in it. Six planets hung in a ring against chiseled stars. Above them, wings spread wide enough to roof all six, stood a dragon with two heads. One head looked down at the planets. The other looked out of the wall at whoever stood in the corridor. The planets were evenly spaced and the size of plates. It looked less like a conquest than a table laid for one.

"Whoa." Dan tipped his head back. "Two heads! Drago, look — that's so sick. Who's that supposed to be?"

Drago didn't answer — not even Dan. He had gone quiet the way a struck bell goes quiet, the sound sinking back into the metal and staying there. I'd been collecting Drago's silences all evening. This one wasn't addressed to me. I kept it anyway.

The doors were taller than the inner door by roughly one more inner door: dark wood bound in gold, carved with two dragon heads in profile, jaws open, facing each other so that the seam ran straight down between their teeth. No handle. No knocker. I've never once touched them. You don't knock on a kiln.

Behind us the lanterns dimmed and brightened, one slow breath, and the smoke along the ceiling slid toward the doors, drawn in.

The jaws parted.

The doors swung inward without a sound, the carved heads drawing apart as if to bite, and the heat came out between them in one long exhalation — not the corridor's heat, which you could live in, but the heat of an open furnace, dry and total, which went past skin and muscle and settled in my bones as if it had been sent for. My face, which I'd set in the lounge, didn't twitch. I stepped through, and they followed, because I had.

The throne room is long. That's the first thing, always, and it's meant to be.

A double row of pillars ran away from us, dark stone carved so densely it seemed to move: each one a dragon climbing itself in coils to the vault, where cusped arches sprang from their jaws and met overhead in the smoke. Bronze braziers stood between the pillars, and their smoke gathered into an overcast of its own, lit gold from beneath, so that the hall kept its own weather, and the weather was always the hour before a storm. Behind the pillars, jali screens walled off the aisles and threw their patterns across the floor in long slanting sheets, stars inside hexagons inside stars, that slid over us as we walked and printed themselves on Dan's face, on Drago's scales, on mine.

Down the middle ran the runner, dark red, deep-piled, straight as a ruled line. I've always thought, and never said aloud to the owner, that walking up it is like walking up a tongue toward the back of something's throat.

On Dan's shoulder, Drago had stopped being a dragon and become a carving of one. Not coiled, not wary: stopped, like a clock someone has reached into and held with one finger. His wings stayed half open. Even the tail tip had nothing to say.

Then he made a sound.

I've heard dragons rumble and hiss and chuff, and once, from somewhere below the gym, heard something I've decided to believe was plumbing. I had never heard this. It came from very deep in him and was very small: a low note that climbed and caught and broke at the top, like a hinge on a door nobody has opened in a lifetime. There was no threat in it. It was recognition — the sound of a creature walking into a room it had been told no longer existed. It wasn't pleasant to hear, and I didn't miss a second of it.

"The Twin Crown Hall," Drago said.

"The what?" said Dan.

Drago didn't answer that either.

"Come," I said gently, and walked them up the runner.

The dais rose at the far end in nine steps of black stone veined with gold, and on top of it stood the throne: dark wood and gold, its back climbing in a carved gold surge — flames, or wings, or flames doing an impression of wings — over red velvet a shade darker than the runner. The armrests ended in gilded dragon heads, snarling, every tooth cut separately. On one rested a hand. On the other rested an elbow, and above it, on the backs of long fingers, as though the weight of his own head were a mild imposition, rested the jaw of the founder of the Dragon Clan.

Bakugan choose their form to suit their mood. Most evenings when I come to report, Nillious receives me at under ten inches, coiled on a cushion beneath an empty throne. At that size he and Drago would have been eye to eye.

Tonight: full avatar. Full court. Damn. They pick their looks to match the mood — and this wasn't a mood. It was a statement, addressed to the small red dragon on Dan's shoulder.

He was very tall; on his feet he would have had to think about doorways. He was built on a scale that made the throne, for the first time, look correctly sized. His skin was bronze and burnished and held the brazier light the way a bell holds the last of a note. His robe was black silk worked with gold dragons, open to the waist, caught at the hips by a dark sash knotted carelessly, and the body it didn't trouble to cover was muscled the way the pillars were carved: that deep, that deliberate. His hair fell over the velvet in long black lengths streaked with yellow-gold, as if the dark had been struck and the light showed through the cracks, and from it at the temples rose his horns, dark and jagged and swept back — a crown he had grown rather than been given.

And the face. An emperor's face, a tyrant's face, a face made to be stamped on gold and carried in the pockets of the conquered. Long jaw, high bones, a straight cruel nose, and a smirk at one corner of the mouth that let a fang show white. His eyes were half-lidded and red, the red of a coal seen through a keyhole, the pupils slit.

Behind him, folded high against the throne, his wings: black, the membranes running from yellow into violet, the colors of a bruise lit from inside. The air above him trembled like air over a road at noon. Heat came off him in a slow, steady pressure I could feel on my face from forty paces, and I leaned into it, God help me, like a cat toward a stove.

His tail poured from under the robe and down the steps — black, twice a man's length, ridged with yellow spikes like a row of teeth in a dark mouth — and its tip lay on the fifth step, moving.

Tick. A pause. Tick. The dry knock of a claw on stone, slow as a metronome set for a funeral march. I call it the Nillious weather report, privately; it's the only forecast in this palace I trust. Slow and even: fair, light winds, no executions anticipated. Faster: bring the laundry in. Faster than that, and it's too late to bring anything in.

Tick. Fair, for now.

On the wall behind the dais the braziers threw up his shadow, enormous across the hangings. The fire should have made it the shape of a man in a chair. It had two heads. One rested its jaw on its hand, as he did. The other was lifted and turned toward the doors, toward us, and as we came up the runner it followed us.

The Three Shadows stood at the foot of the dais like its last three steps.

At the emperor's left hand, Ornstein: slender beside the other two the way a blade is slender beside a club. Threads of blue static crawled the blade of his grounded spear and snapped now and then at the smoke, which flinched, and the air near him tasted like a battery on the tongue. (I've checked.) His red hair, gathered high, fell to the small of his back like the crest of a helmet he was above wearing. His face was a carved idol's, cold and finished, made for being knelt to and nothing else, with a sheen of scale at the temples like mica in granite and gold eyes whose pupils had drawn down to hairlines.

At the right hand, Smough, less a man than a landform: the size of a doorway that had decided to stand up and leave. Small blunt horns along the brow like the knuckles of a fist, and a grin — wide, brutal, permanent, with too many teeth in it — that had been on his face so long it seemed to have stopped consulting him. His hammer stood head-down beside him, the head the size of a chest freezer, his hand resting on the haft the way an old man rests his on a cane. I have seen him carry it the way I carry my phone.

And in the center, on the runner itself, so that the only road to the throne went through him: Artorias. A tattered blue cowl hid the upper half of his face; what showed was the pale of things that live under stones. The hilt of a greatsword stood up over his shoulder like a grave marker. The other two were still the way a held breath is still. Artorias was still the way things are still at the bottom of a lake. Once he lifted his head, and the eyes under the cowl were the eyes of something that had stood a long time in the dark between stars and been changed by the view.

And he was cold. In the hottest room I have ever stood in, a damp chill came off him like the breath from a cellar door, smelling of rain on stone: the only cold thing in the palace. It found the gap at my collar, the same gap the sleet had found, and I disliked him for it, specifically and personally, the way you'd dislike a draft that had learned your name.

*This isn't a clan,* I thought, not for the first time. *This is a bunch of Greek gods.* The one on the high seat; the thunder at his left hand; the hammer at his right; and the one from underneath standing nearest, the way the underneath always does. Somebody ought to tell them how on the nose it is. It won't be me.

I've met gods before. A different pantheon. They liked a staircase too.

And they were all beautiful — that was the other problem. Beautiful in an alarming, over-rendered way, as if the artist had been paid by the hour and the hours were long. The lighting was too good. The composition was too good. A red runner; an emperor at the top of it with his robe open and his chin on his hand; three bodyguards arranged like the cover of volume one. Had I wandered into an alien romance manhwa? A visual novel? Any moment now a text box would unfold along the bottom of the hall, with a menu: *[Bow.] [Smile.] [Compliment the horns.]*

Then something cold went down through me — not off Artorias; from much nearer, from the inside — one clean drop, like a stone let fall down a well, and no splash.

*Not going to think about it. Not yet.*

So I did what I always do in that room. I looked at the columns.

I have mastered not looking openly. When an emperor is lounging half-dressed on a chair made of teeth, you don't look at the emperor; you look at the architecture. I know those columns better than some of my own expressions, and I know my expressions to the millimeter. The second on the right has one scale carved upside down — a mason's joke, or a mason's last mistake. They are so *INTERESTING*. You would not believe how *INTERESTING* they are. To anyone watching I was a devout young man overcome by holy architecture, and not one soul in that hall had the faintest idea what went on in my head. That's what a head is for.

And the third on the left — counting from the dais, which is the direction everything in that room is counted from — has a scorch mark. It's old: a starburst of black at about shoulder height, where the stone was once so hot for so long that it forgot it was stone and ran like glass, the carved scales melted into one glossy blister. Every other inch of the hall is kept polished; that one has never been touched. I've never asked. I filed it, as I had at every audience — and this time, though I couldn't have said why, I filed it nearer the front.

Twelve paces from Artorias, I stopped. I always stop at twelve. Eleven is presumptuous; thirteen looks afraid. Dan stopped at my elbow and hunched into his open jacket, the cold off Artorias reaching him too.

I folded my hands, lowered my eyes a fraction as at an altar rail, and spoke in my priest's voice at its most perfect — the one for reading names off a memorial, the one that makes strangers lower theirs.

"Dan. Drago. The founder of the Dragon Clan — Nillious."

Then I stepped off the runner to the right, to stand by the third pillar on that side, directly across from the scorched one, near enough to Ornstein that his static stood up the hairs on the back of my neck. Against my ribs the card had gone warm as a palm laid flat. Across the runner, the black blister on the third pillar looked back.

Nillious didn't move. He hadn't since the doors opened — not a blink; only the tail, ticking. The stillness went on until Dan shifted his weight and the carpet sighed under his sneakers, and in that whole enormous hall it was the loudest thing.

Then his head was turned toward Drago. I didn't see it turn. There was no wind-up; there was before, and there was after. His nostrils flared, slow and wide, and the inhale pulled the smoke on the steps toward him in a visible current, and somewhere in it he found Drago, and the smirk deepened by the width of a fang. A thread of smoke slid from his lips. Ornstein did not flinch. Smough did not flinch. Artorias did not flinch. I've never seen anything else in this palace manage it. I do my flinching on the inside, where it doesn't count.

"General," said Nillious.

It wasn't loud. It was a voice that had been obeyed so long it had forgotten how to be raised, and it filled the hall the way heat does, from the floor up. And behind it, half a beat late, from somewhere that was not his mouth — lower, rougher, as if the other head were still speaking in a room we couldn't see — *General.*

"You've shrunk."

*—shrunk.*

Smough laughed, wet and booming, the sound of something heavy falling down a flight of stairs and enjoying every one of them.

"Don't, Smough." Nillious lifted his hand from the armrest and let it fall, an emperor bearing up under a great sorrow. "It's a tragedy. Look at him. I gave him legions. I gave him a sky to hold and a standard to hold it under. And now—" the red eyes slid across Dan without stopping, the way your eyes slide across a coat stand on the way to the door "—now he has found himself a *perch*."

The laugh came out of him with no warning, a great delighted bark that showed every fang he owned and blew smoke from his nostrils in two hard plumes; on the wall, the shadow threw both its heads back. Then it stopped — not tapered; stopped, as if a door had been shut on it.

"A perch," he said, quite flat.

*A perch,* the other voice agreed.

"Hey," said Dan. "I'm not a—"

"You were always so poor at remembering things, General," Nillious went on at exactly the same pitch, as if Dan were a pipe knocking in the walls. "Rank. Place. Whose sky it was."

Drago had come out of his stillness without seeming to move. Heat shimmered off his small body, and the shoulder of Dan's jacket had begun, faintly, to smoke. His tail had wound itself twice around Dan's collar.

"Nillious," Drago said. Only the name.

"*Emperor,*" Nillious said, almost kindly. "Say it. You used to say it so well."

Drago didn't say it.

Tick, went the tail on the fifth step. Tick. Tick. A little quicker now. I revised the forecast.

And then Dan caught up. I watched it cross his face like a lamp carried through a house at night, room by room — the name, the two heads in the corridor, whatever Drago had told him on some other night about a dragon who had ruled a world. He looked at Nillious; at Drago; last of all and hottest, at me.

"Wait. *Nillious?* Like — *the* Nillious? Drago told me about you!" His voice cracked upward, outrage and wonder fighting over the same breath. "Man, I should've *known* you'd be part of the Dragon Clan — you're a dragon! You're the emperor of all of Vestroia!" He rounded on me, red to the ears. "Kage, you never said — no. No way. Then the deal's off—"

Nillious lifted one finger.

He didn't sit up. He didn't look at Dan. He raised one long finger from the line of his jaw, its nail dark and hooked and filed to a point, and the hall went silent around it — the mutter of the braziers, the snap of static on Ornstein's spear, the wet rumble of Smough's breathing, the tick on the fifth step. Dan's sentence stopped where it stood, with the back half of it still in his mouth.

The finger stayed up, and the hall stayed quiet around it the way a meadow goes quiet under a hawk: nothing decided yet, every small thing in it simply doing the same arithmetic at once.

Smoke stood on the steps and did not drift. On the fifth step the tail tip lay where it had last struck and did not strike again, and that was new. I keep a whole almanac for that tail — fair, freshening, gale, too late for the laundry — and not one page of it covers *stopped*.

"Off," Nillious said.

He said it the way you'd lift a strange insect off your sleeve, between finger and thumb, to see what kind it was before you squeezed.

*—off,* said the other voice, half a beat behind and lower down, trying the word on for size and not caring for the fit.

His eyes never went to Dan. They went to Drago, so that Dan had to take what came next secondhand, like a servant paid through the steward.

"Tonight, in my house, a soft thing put out its hand. My Leader took it." The lifted finger turned, idly, toward the nearest lantern. "And my house felt it. Every lamp I own bowed. Did you notice? No. You were eating."

*Four down, three held, four up.* So that was what it had been, in the lounge. Not only an ear turned toward a footstep. A seal pressed into hot wax.

"In the house of the emperor, a hand given is a vow. A vow is a thing. I own it as I own the lamps, and the floor under your feet, and the air you are spending so freely on noise." His voice never rose. Nothing in him rose. "Your kind keeps a law for this, I'm told. I've heard children shout it in the streets." He let a thread of smoke out between his lips and watched it climb, fondly, like a pet. "No take-backs."

A playground law; I'd thought it myself in the lounge, as a joke. In that voice, with the other voice under it, it became the sort of thing cut over a gate in a dead language: nobody alive can read it, and everyone who walks under it understands.

Dan found the back half of his sentence and swallowed it, and found another. "That's not *fair*. I didn't know it was *you*—"

"Fair." Nillious seemed charmed, as if a dog had stood up on its hind legs and asked for the salt. "Smough. Did you hear? It said *fair*."

Smough's laugh came up out of him like a landslide that had been waiting all week for an excuse.

"Nobody hid me from you, mayfly. I have sat in this chair all evening. You never once asked what sat in it."

Dan rounded on me. "You *knew*."

"I told you he was our founder," I said.

It was true. That's the beauty of the truth: you can hide behind it in plain sight.

"*Founder!* You said *founder!* Not—" He flung an arm at the throne, the wings, the shadow on the wall, all of it. "—not *emperor of everything*!"

"Everything," Nillious repeated, pleased. "It's learning. My Leader tells the guests there are six clans. I've heard him. He counts them off on his fingers like a choirboy."

*I do not count like a choirboy. I count like someone paid per item.*

"Insect," said Nillious. "Dino. Aquatic. Mammal. Avian." Each name came out with a little smoke on it, like breath on a coin before the polish. "Five worlds. I broke them one at a time, and they knelt, and I let them keep their names, because a name is a collar you can see from a long way off. Then a hole opens in the sky and the whole table is shaken out onto this—" the finger flicked toward the doors, toward Iceland "—*damp* little world, and look. The dogs have found the gate open. They dig their own dens. They call themselves *clans*." The tail tip lifted off the fifth step and came down. Tick. "They've forgotten their collars. Collars don't forget."

I watched it go through Dan, slower than it had gone through me, and deeper. He'd had the frieze in front of him twenty minutes ago and hadn't known he was reading.

"The pictures," he said. "In the hallway. The birds, and the—" He swallowed. "You *conquered* them."

"I *finished* them," said Nillious. "Conquering is what you call it while they're still fighting."

I've walked that corridor more evenings than I'll admit, admired the gilding every time, and never once done the sum. Six planets on the last panel; six clans on my fingers in the lounge. I had recited a list of the conquered to a guest, warmly, teacup in hand, and called it a welcome. In my defense, it scanned beautifully.

"Well, I'm not your colony." Dan was red to the ears, both fists shut. "I'm not *anybody's*—"

"No," Nillious agreed, and for the first time his eyes came to rest on Dan — for about as long as it takes to read a price tag. "You aren't even that. A colony is worth the fire it costs. You are a mayfly. Your kind hatches in the morning and is dead by supper, and spends the whole afternoon telling everyone how it feels. Soft. Short. *Loud.* The stars turned a very long time without one of you under them and never once felt the lack."

Then, as though he'd bitten into something gone off: "And yet."

Tick, tick, went the tail. Freshening.

"And yet the universe has a sense of humor, and I don't share it." The hand on the armrest lifted and opened, slowly, and on the wall behind him the shadow's wings opened with it until they roofed the hangings from pillar to pillar. "I was the height of a mountain before your species learned to strike a spark. I am that height still. It is *mine*, by blood and by egg. And I may not stand at it, in my own body, unless one of you holds a scrap of card in his warm little hand and *permits* it."

*Permits* came out wreathed in smoke. The hand came down on the gilded head of the armrest and closed, and the carved teeth creaked under his palm like a ship's timbers.

"So. There is a use for your kind. I don't pretend otherwise; I'm not a liar, I'm an emperor. Warm tools." The smirk came back, long and slow, and showed its fang. "Some of you. Perhaps one in two."

Eh. At least it's honest.

Everybody uses everybody. Most of them make you sit through the speech about friendship first, and then ask you to split the bill. Nillious has never once called me anything but what I am to him, which is the hand that holds the card, and you would be amazed how restful that is. I respected it from a safe distance, while giving my full and reverent attention to the second pillar on the right, whose upside-down scale was looking especially upside-down tonight.

Which is how I knew, without looking, that he was looking at me.

Not the price-tag glance he'd given Dan. His eyes came to me and stopped, and stayed, one beat past where a glance ends and something else begins. His chin lifted a fraction. He drew one long, unhurried breath through the nose, and the smoke on the steps leaned toward him with it — and somewhere in it, among cedar and iron and the cellar-cold off Artorias, he found me, and held the breath there, and didn't let it go.

The stone went down the well again. Still no splash.

He was checking his property, that was all. A man pats his pocket to make sure the keys are still in it; I was the one in two. *Not going to think about it.* I didn't. I'm very good at not thinking about things. It's like holding your breath, except you can keep it up for years.

Then the breath went out of him in two long threads of smoke, and his attention swung back to Drago like a lamp on a chain.

"You talk," said Drago, "as if you still had something to be emperor of."

He hadn't been idle in his silence. The shoulder of Dan's jacket was smoking in earnest now, a thin brown curl going up from under each of his feet, and the air around his nine inches bent the way it bends over a grate.

Smough's grin got wider. I hadn't known there was more of it in stock.

"Your empire is on the other side of a hole in the sky, and the hole is shut. Between here and there is nothing but cold and dark for longer than light can be bothered to travel. You're not the emperor of Vestroia. You *were*." Drago's head came forward on his neck, low and level, the way a snake's does just before it makes up its mind. "Now you're the emperor of a fire station. In the sleet. Of a hall full of copies, and whatever exiles were lost enough to come when you called."

*Oh,* I thought, *that's good.* Emperor of a fire station. I'd be using that, silently, for the rest of my life.

"I am emperor," said Nillious, "wherever I stand. Set me down on a dead moon and it's an empire by morning. The stars do not get a *vote*."

*—vote,* said the voice under his voice.

"Former," said Drago.

"Present."

"*Former.*"

"Present, present, *present*—"

And for a moment, if you could have turned the sound down, it was two hatchlings in one nest, each shrieking that the nest was his because he'd cracked his shell in it first. I've heard better-argued disputes over a parking space behind a laundromat.

But you couldn't turn the sound down. You couldn't turn down anything.

The heat came off the dais in waves you could watch, and the pillars between us went soft at the edges and swam. Smoke poured from Nillious's nostrils and lay along the steps like a dog that knew its place. Drago's wings came up and hooded over Dan's head, and the jali patterns on the floor around them writhed like nets with something caught in them. Then Nillious's wings opened — not unfolded; *snapped*, the whole black span at once, with a crack like a sail taking a gale — and the wind of it laid every brazier flame in the hall flat. On the wall, the shadow had both its heads up now, though his own jaw still rested on his hand.

The tail had stopped reporting the weather. It was making it. It lashed, and the fifth step rang under it like an anvil, and a chip of black stone went skittering down the remaining four.

A few yards from me, Ornstein's spear began to crackle in earnest — blue static sheeting up the blade and spilling onto the floor, where it crawled toward my boots and stood the hair up along my arms. Across the runner Smough's hand had closed around the haft of his hammer, and he looked as if it were his birthday.

And Artorias didn't move. In all that heat he went on being cold, quietly and steadily, the way a well stays cold at the bottom of a burning village.

"*Former*," said Nillious, tasting it — and laughed. A high, warm, helpless laugh, the wrong laugh entirely: someone hearing a child's joke at a funeral. "Oh, General. Little ember. Shall I tell your mayfly how *former* happened? How you stood in my hall—" the laugh shut off as if a lid had come down on it "—in *my hall*, with my court watching and your own legions at your back, and said *no* to me."

The second voice didn't echo that. It snarled it.

"I was *fond* of you." Gentle again, wistful, a man turning over an old letter. "That's why I let you duel me. Do you remember? You were so sure. Ornstein — how long did it last?"

"Not long, Emperor."

"Not long," Nillious agreed. "And afterward I took your legions off you like a coat and gave them to a general of my own choosing — and do you know, I've forgotten his name." He sounded enchanted. "I have never once forgotten yours. Isn't that a kindness, deserter? Isn't that *love*?"

His hand clenched.

The gilded head under it split across the snout with a report like a gunshot, and one of its teeth — cut separately, like all the others — came away and fell. It went down the dais a step at a time, nine small bright notes. I counted every one. It came to rest against the toe of Artorias's boot, and Artorias didn't look down.

Drago had opened his mouth, firelight rising behind his teeth — and then he didn't answer. His head had turned. Not toward Nillious. Toward the left-hand row of pillars, past Smough's shoulder, going down them the way his eyes had gone down the arches in the vestibule — one, two — and stopping on the third.

I was already looking at it. I'd been looking at it all evening, in the sense that I'd been looking at everything else.

The black starburst at shoulder height. The scales run together into one glossy blister. The single inch of that hall that nobody has ever been allowed to polish.

Every bit of heat went out of Drago at once. Not cooled: withdrawn, pulled back inside him like a hand snatched from a stove. The smoke stopped. The tail around Dan's collar went slack. When he spoke, it wasn't a hiss. It was barely anything.

"You kept it."

Nillious smiled at him, and the smile was almost tender.

"I keep everything I win."

*Everything,* said the second voice, half a beat late and very close.

Somewhere under the conversation, the floor went.

Because this hall is a copy. Drago had said so in the vestibule — *not this one; the one it was copied from.* Every pillar raised new, on a new world, in stone that had never seen Vestroia. Which meant that once the third pillar on the left was finished, and polished, and perfect, someone had stood in front of it with the shape of an old fire in his head and burned it back in, to the inch, across a wormhole, because the original had been lost with everything else, and losing it had not been acceptable.

I'd stood across from that mark at every audience and taken it for damage. It was a trophy, re-cast.

I'd known dragons hoard. I hadn't known they framed their victories and hung them where the loser would one day have to stand and look.

Then the wrath was gone. There was no transition; with him there never is. One moment the hall was a furnace with its door wide open, and the next his wings had folded down behind the throne as softly as a closed book, and his voice had gone to honey — dark honey, the kind left too long in the comb, with smoke at the back of the sweetness.

He took his jaw off his hand and leaned forward, elbows on his knees, fingers loosely laced, and lowered his voice to just below where it had been.

I knew that move; I'd used it on Dan in the lounge. It's a very good move. It's much less charming from the outside.

"Ember," he said. "Listen to us. Two dragons of the old command, hissing over a burnt pillar at the bottom of the universe, while a planet of soft things looks on and learns that dragons can be divided. Why do we keep reopening an old wound, you and I, when we could let it heal?" His hand turned over, palm up, open, a gift. "Come home. There are five clans on this world with no emperor over them, and they've forgotten whom they belong to. Rule them with me. Not as my general — as my equal. Almost. Two dragons. We'd have them kneeling by spring."

For one second the hall waited, and I waited with it, and I'll admit I didn't know which way he'd go.

"No," said Drago.

Only that. Then, because a dragon can't leave a thing at one word when he means it: "I said it to you once. I'll say it as often as you ask."

"Yeah," said Dan, a beat late, fists still shut, because he hadn't followed all of it but he had followed Drago. "What he said."

Nillious looked at them both. Then he sat back, slowly, and the honey drained out of his face, and something brighter and worse came in.

"Ah," he said. "I see. You're afraid."

Drago went rigid from horns to tail.

"Not of me. You were never clever enough to be afraid of me." The finger again, lazy, toward the third pillar on the left. "Of *that*. Of doing it twice. Last time it was your legions who watched you kneel. This time it would only be one small loud boy—" the red eyes didn't go to Dan; they didn't need to "—and you can't bear it. You'd sooner walk out into the sleet than find out, in front of him, that you are exactly what you were."

The rumble that came out of Drago didn't sound like it could fit inside anything nine inches long. Fire came up into his mouth again, and this time it stayed there, blue at the root, and his pupils had swallowed his eyes, black as drilled holes.

"Brawl me," he said.

A different silence from the first: a hall full of creatures who had all, at once, stopped pretending they weren't hungry.

"Brawl me," said Drago. "If we win, the deal's void and we walk out."

And Nillious lit.

It went through him like a struck match through paper — the pupils blooming until the red was only a rim, the wings coming half up again, not in wrath now but the way a cat's whole body gathers when the string finally moves. Smoke went up from him in a column. The tail rang off the fifth step like a bell. On the wall the two-headed shadow opened both its jaws at once.

"And when you lose," he said, low and delighted, tasting each word like a wine he'd laid down long ago for exactly this night, "you stay. You kneel in my hall and call it home."

"*Deal!*" said Dan.

He said it before Drago could. Of course he did. That made three times in one evening; I'd been counting. Drago turned and looked at him — the whole head — and for a second I thought Drago might bite him. Then he turned back to the throne and said, flat as a blade laid down on a table, "Agreed."

"*Accepted,*" breathed Nillious, and this time the second voice said it with him, both at once, and he laughed so long and so joyfully that Smough joined in out of sheer good manners.

And something in me sat up.

I'd been working all evening. Talking is labor; I'm good at it the way a man is good at a job he's held too long. But a brawl has edges. A field, rules, a winner, an end. No two of them have ever gone the same way, and I would know. Three weeks of searching, one long evening of being lovely, and at last the thing I'm actually good at!

I wanted to win. Not for Nillious, whatever he believed. Not for the Clan. For the clean, mean pleasure of watching a good opponent find out a beat too late that good was not going to be enough. I'm never sorry about that part.

My face did nothing. I kept it grave and a little regretful, a priest asked to witness something he would rather not, and behind it I was grinning with every tooth I own.

"Kage," said Nillious.

Only my name, and under it, half a beat behind, *Kage*.

I stepped away from the third pillar and back onto the runner, level with Dan, a few paces of red carpet between us, and as I went I slid two fingers inside my jacket and drew out the card. It came out already warm and grew warmer in my hand — not the steady, borrowed heat I carry against my ribs, but rising, quickening, like a coal when someone kneels and blows on it. Drago lifted off Dan's shoulder on short hard wingbeats and hung at his ear, and Dan dug out his own card and held it up in both hands, white-knuckled, grinning, scared, every inch a brawler.

Under the runner, the floor answered.

A crack opened across the black stone between us — one line, quick as a thought, the red pile of the carpet parting along it like a lip — and light came up out of it, red going to gold, and behind the light came the heat. Not the palace's heat. Not cedar and lamps and the courtesy of a hearth. This was rawer: sulfur and scorched rock and the breath of a mountain opening its throat, and it rolled up over my boots and through my clothes and broke against my face, and every cold thing in me turned toward it at once, like a congregation when the doors at the back swing open.

The floor came apart along its veins of gold.

Every vein in the throne room opened at once. Threads became fingers, fingers became gutters, and the light in them stopped being gold and sank through amber into a red so deep it was nearly a sound. Between the veins the black stone broke into plates that tipped, each on its own slow hinge, like the pages of a hymnal riffled by a draft, and up through the gaps came heat with no lid on it.

The runner went next. It didn't fall; it ran. The whole red length of it slumped and darkened and began to move, and within a dozen yards it was no longer carpet but a river of molten rock rolling between banks of black, skinning over and splitting and healing as though it couldn't make up its mind to be solid. I've always privately compared that runner to a tongue. I take it back. Tongues are cooler.

The pillars went up. Something above the smoke took hold of their capitals and pulled, and the carved dragons climbing them smeared into long glassy flutes, and when the pulling stopped they were spires of obsidian, black as the inside of a shut eye, flaked down their faces in shell-shaped scoops. The braziers sank and became vents. The jali screens burned off last, their lattice flaring once on the ground as lines of light, and gone. And the overcast of incense that always hung beneath the vault swelled and reddened and climbed until there was no vault left to lie against, and then it was a sky, low and rust-colored and lit from below, rolling over on itself like a sea seen from the bottom.

The sulfur that runs under every other smell in the palace came up to the front and took over the service.

My plate dropped a yard and caught, hard enough to click my teeth together, and settled as a terrace of basalt thirty paces across, with a channel of magma down one side and, on the other, a drop I didn't spend time on. Less the name of a band than a complaint you'd describe to a pharmacist in a low voice. Dan yelled it like a war cry. I'll give him that.

"He *burned* you!"

"*Us*," the left head snarled, smoke boiling between its teeth.

"*You*," said the right, delighted. "Look at your *face*."

I'd suspected for some time that Nillious didn't like anybody. I hadn't realized it extended to himself.

It wasn't fencing. It was two furnaces trying to eat each other. Drago got his jaws around the root of the right neck and hung on, wrenching; the scorched head came down over his shoulder and sank its teeth into the base of his wing. The right head swung clear and found me.

"*Run*, mayfly."

I was already running.

I love this. I'll say it once, plainly, since nobody can overhear: I love this. The heat off the river, the ash in my mouth, the ground going like a drum under my boots; the clean, total simplicity of a thing that one side wins. I want to win the way I want warmth: not as a preference, but as a condition of going on. And my face did what it always does. It looked like a young man walking briskly to evening prayers, mildly concerned about being late. I didn't need a mirror. I'd set it.

There are two kinds of black in an arena like that, and you learn to read them or you learn something worse. There was basalt, rough and matte, pitted and blistered like the crust of burned bread, ugly and honest and solid all the way down. And there were the smooth places, skins of cooled lava stretched over vents, with a dull, oily sheen like the film on a puddle in a gas station lot. The spike had gone through one of those. Twenty strides on, a chunk of fallen spire bounced onto another, and it caved like the lid of a pie, and fire came up. A third swallowed a falling scale whole.

One is an event. Two is a rhyme. Three is a rule. I'm very good at noticing when something comes round again. I've had practice.

I kept to the matte.

I put that somewhere I'd be able to find it again.

Health.

Across the river, Dan saw it. I watched him see it, his whole face flung open like a window. Then I watched him see the river.

"Aw, come *on!*"

None of it stayed. It never does. It isn't for me; I'm only the wire it travels by.

Green fire broke over Nillious from the crowns of both heads to the forks of his tail. The black star on the left cheek knit shut, new scale sliding over the burn the way ice skins a pond. Drago.

"Good," said the left head, to me, without looking.

"Late," said the right.

Everyone's a critic. Most of them only have the one mouth to do it with.

The next name came up in gold.

Scissor Tail: the band the drummer starts after he's thrown out of Shuriken Spikes. "*SCISSOR TAIL!*"

Nillious turned.

Drago saw it coming. He got his wings half up. It made no difference.

"*DRAGO!*"

Dan was already moving: off his terrace, down a slope of loose scree on his heels and his hands, across the basalt in a stumbling sprint, falling, up again with his palms scraped raw, shouting the name over and over as if it were a rope he could throw. He didn't look at the ground once. Kids never do.

I didn't watch him. I'd seen him run. A spire. A seam. A shelf of basalt far off to the north where the ash wasn't settling quite the way it settled everywhere else.

The wreck of the spire was still settling when Drago came up through it.

Obsidian doesn't crumble the way honest stone does; it argues. People get up in installments, consulting each joint. Dragons get up the way a fire does when somebody opens the door: all at once, and bigger than you'd budgeted for.

"DRAGO!"

So Dan, who has never in his life searched for anything methodically, found the Speed zone by falling over it.

Drago left.

Two heads against one. It wasn't fair; I have never understood the appeal of fair. One head tracked where Drago was, and the other watched where he was going to be. Drago went between them.

He struck at the fork where the two necks rise from one pair of shoulders, and the sound reached me a heartbeat late and landed in my sternum: two great weights dropped on the same drumskin. Fire against shadow. It doesn't sound like physics; it looked exactly like it. Drago's fire was the kind you know, red going to white at the heart, and for one second it lit the whole underside of Nillious, so that I saw the architecture of him the way you see the bones of your own hand over a flashlight. "Faster," said the laughing head. "You were always the faster one, General."

"Then keep up."

I knew that color. I'd caught Dan's wrist an inch from it in the lab.

"—*DRAGO!*"

I'd been counting on that too.

"Kage—"

Name a thing and you have to feed it; feed it, and one day you look around and find you're keeping it. By the next stride it had dropped away behind me on the rim, and I didn't go back for it.

The zone lay in the top of its rock the way a coal lies in a cupped hand, a red-gold circle as wide as a millstone, the color the inside of a crucible turns a moment before the pour. I put my palm on it at a dead run.

He had been the size of a high-rise. He added floors. The yellow spikes along his spine lit from the root, one after another down his whole length, like the lamps along a pier coming on at dusk. His wings cracked open so hard the smoke tore from one horizon to the other. And the two heads turned.

Together.

"*TWIN MAW ECLIPSE!*"

Of all the rejected band names, it was the one I'd have paid to see live, and I gave it the voice reserved for the last page of Revelation.

Both heads breathed.

A black disc in the middle of the sky, crowned in gold fire.

In the middle of the black, very small, something red was still burning.

Drago. Every coal he had left, flaring against the inside of the ring. Pushing. I watched it flare. I watched it push. I watched it go out.

Winning tastes like sugar.

It filled my mouth. My face didn't move. The spires didn't topple; they drew themselves in, the glass going matte, the jagged edges softening into coils and scales, and they were pillars, two long rows of them, and always had been. The magma thinned to threads and sank into the black floor and went on moving there, gold. The ash forgot how to fall, turned to incense, and remembered how to rise. The red sky came down and folded itself into the overcast under the vault. The basin lifted like a cloth drawn off a table, and the chute closed under Dan like a hand, and set him down.

On his knees. On the runner. Both hands cupped in front of him, held up the way you'd hold them out for water.

Drago dropped the last few feet into them as if the arena had aimed him there.

Dan didn't move. His palms were raw where the ledge had skinned them; one knee of his jeans had torn through; the ash in his hair had turned him gray, a little old man of a boy. In the bowl of his hands Drago lay curled on his side, wings crumpled half open, smoking — not burning, smoking, the way a hearth smokes after it's been doused — and under the smoke his ribs moved. Dragons are hard to put out. It's the whole point of them.

Up on the dais, Nillious sat on his throne exactly as he had sat before: jaw on the backs of his fingers, robe open, hair poured over the velvet, not a strand of it out of place. Walk in now and you'd swear he had never moved, never had a head scorched or a throat raked, and you'd be wrong in a way you could never prove. Only the smoke gave him away: one slow gray curl rising from the corner of his smile, past the fang, past a horn, up into the dark. The last of the eclipse, going home.

Ornstein stood at his left hand with static crawling his spear. Smough stood at his right, wearing the grin that has never needed a reason. Artorias stood at the foot of the dais, cowled and motionless, and the damp came off him and found the sweat at the back of my neck, and for once I hardly minded. I was full of sugar.

My palm was flat against a pillar on the right side of the hall, as if the zone were still under it. The stone was warm. I left my hand where it was.

Nobody spoke.

The braziers muttered to themselves. Somewhere down the hall a coal settled in its cradle with a small dry click. Dan looked at what he was holding, and his mouth opened, and for once in his life, nothing came out of it.

From his cupped palms the smoke off Drago went straight up, a thin gray line drawn from his hands into the dark, unbent, because there was not a breath in that whole hall strong enough to move it.

For a while nothing in the throne room moved but the ash.

The arena had gone back to wherever arenas go when they're done with you, but it had left a little weather behind: the last gray flakes still sifting out of the smoke under the vault and going out a hand's breadth above the runner, as if each had remembered at the last moment that it had no business here. My boots were gray to the ankle with the dust of a volcano that no longer existed. I slid the card back inside my jacket. Against my ribs it was hot — not warm; hot, a coal sewn into the lining.

Dan knelt in the middle of the runner where the hall had set him down: ash in his hair, a graze along one cheekbone, one knee of his jeans split open and grinning. He held his hands cupped in front of his chest the way you'd carry a bird that has flown into a window, and in them lay Drago.

Nine inches again, and smoking — fine gray threads from his nostrils, from the seams between his scales, as though a fire still burned in there with nobody left to tend it. His wings were folded wrong, crumpled like an umbrella shut in a hurry, and he hadn't fixed them. That, more than anything, told me how bad it was. Dan's palms had gone an angry red. He wasn't letting go.

Up on the dais the emperor smoked, slow and blue, the way a pipe smokes after a very good dinner, as if eleven stories of black scale had been a fever the rest of us passed from hand to hand.

The Three Shadows stood where they always stand. Nobody spoke. In a room that size, silence isn't an absence. It's a tenant, and it pays no rent.

"Dan."

It was barely a voice. It was the sound a coal makes when you turn it over.

"Yeah. Yeah, I'm here, buddy. I got you."

Drago lifted his head out of Dan's palms. It took him two tries. Then he did a thing I wouldn't have bet on, not before those witnesses: he tipped his chin back and bared his throat to the boy — the small pale scales of it, the soft underside of the furnace.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I failed you."

I've watched a good many things paid for in that room. That was the most expensive.

"*What?* No." Dan's voice cracked straight down the middle. "Don't *do* that. You didn't fail anything — *I* failed *you*. I went straight across, I didn't even look, I just *ran*, and I fell in a stupid *hole*, and you were up there all by yourself—" He ran out of breath before he ran out of sentence. His thumb went back and forth along Drago's spine, though it must have been burning him. "That's on me."

He hadn't looked. I had, very carefully, and then let him have the straight line all to himself, because it was shorter and he was in a hurry and I was raised to be generous. Confession is a sacrament, and I was never ordained.

"You both failed," said Nillious.

He didn't raise his voice. It came down the nine steps at its leisure, like a man descending a staircase in his slippers, and behind it, half a beat late, from wherever the other head keeps itself when it isn't wanted: *—failed.*

Smough laughed. It went off in him like a rockfall into a flooded quarry: one great wet boom, then a long, glad slosh of echoes off every pillar in the hall.

"Listen to them, Smough." Nillious let his free hand drift off the armrest, palm up, offering us the scene on a tray. "Squabbling over the failure as though it were an heirloom." The smirk lengthened until a fang showed. "Allow me. I am known for my judgments. You failed *together*. It's the first thing you've ever done as equals."

*—equals.*

His head turned left, and Ornstein's gold eyes met it as cleanly as a key finds its lock. "He apologized. Did you hear? Millions of years, Ornstein. Did I ever once have that word from him?"

"No, my lord."

"No. I had his fire on my pillar. I had his back as he went out of my sky. And the mayfly gets *sorry*." Smoke slid from his nostrils, unhurried. "Shall I tell you how you lost, little ember? Not the zones. Not your soft thing's legs, though they are sad, short legs. You lost because when it fell, you *looked*. In the middle of my fire, with both my heads in front of you, you turned to see whether the warm thing was still breathing. I put the eclipse exactly where your attention had been." He smiled. "You always did look at the wrong thing."

Drago's lip came up off his teeth. The rumble under his breastbone was thin and cracked, a draft through a broken flue, and it was all he had left, and he spent it anyway, trying to get his feet under him in Dan's hands.

"Drago, don't—"

"*DESERTER.*"

It went off like a cannon in a cathedral. Every brazier flung its flames a foot higher; the other voice landed on the same syllable half a beat behind and made it a chord. Dan flinched so hard he nearly dropped what he was holding. On the fifth step the tail tip came down once, sharp as a shutter slammed in a gale.

Then, between one breath and the next, Nillious was examining his nails.

"You'll kneel," he said mildly. "Not tonight. You're lying down already. It wouldn't count."

Smough laughed until his hammer shifted on the stone. Ornstein didn't — I'm not convinced he has the parts — but a thread of static ran up his spear and snapped at the smoke, which from Ornstein is a standing ovation. Artorias said nothing, in that way of his that makes silence feel damp. His cowl turned one degree toward the pair on the runner, and the cold came off him and settled over them like mist on a low field. Pity, or weather. I cared for neither.

On the fifth step the tail had gone back to its tick: slow, fat, contented. High pressure, settled in for the week.

My turn, then. I set the smile — the long-aisle one, for christenings and wreaths — stepped forward two paces and no farther, and looked down at Dan and the small wrecked dragon in his hands with all the tenderness I keep in stock, which is a great deal, since I never use any myself.

"You can challenge us anytime," I said. "Both of you. Whenever you're ready, the arena will be here." I left a beat, the space you leave for the amen. "And welcome to the Dragon Clan."

Dan looked up. His eyes were wet and furious, and for one second he was a kid all the way down; then his jaw set and he was something else. "We will," he said thickly. "And we're gonna *win*."

"I hope so."

I meant that. It's rare enough that I noticed.

Drago said nothing. He watched me over the rim of Dan's fingers with his pupils drawn down to scratches, and went on watching, and I'd honestly have preferred to be bitten.

So: benediction given, congregation dismissed, coffee and cake in the church hall. In any respectable religion, that's when everybody stands up.

"*Welcome,*" Nillious said, enchanted.

Oh, no.

"Yes. Welcome home, General." He took his chin off his hand. He *sat up*, and somewhere inside me my soul quietly sat down on the floor. "Your old quarters are gone, of course. We'll find you a shelf."

He was just getting started. On ordinary evenings, when he receives me nine inches long on his cushion under the empty throne, I'm in and out in under a minute; small, he has the attention span of a cat. Full size, with a court, he has the attention span of geology.

And I wanted to go *home* — up my staircase, to a room that was absurdly mine, where a bag of paprika chips waited, pinned shut with a clothespin bought for that exact purpose, and a girl in a yellow raincoat had been standing on a bridge in the rain since Tuesday, waiting for me to decide whether to tell her the truth. I wanted it so much my molars ached.

My face, I'd like the record to show, was a lake at dawn: hands open, eyes soft, a young man lost in the mysteries of victory. Behind my sternum a dog had heard keys in the door and was hurling itself at the wood.

Nillious talked. He discussed the shelf — its height, its light, its neighbors: a jawbone I won't describe and a very fine bowl. He asked whether the eclipse had been beautiful, and Ornstein said, "It was," and Smough boomed, "*Beautiful,*" and Nillious said nothing, which meant *again*, and they said it again. He spoke of the Strength zone as though it had come to him the way tribute comes; I had crossed four hundred meters of broken basalt to fetch it. Credit is for people who can't print their own. He said *General* fourteen more times. I counted.

He was making it last. He was eating Drago the way you eat a Tópas pastille: no crunching, just letting it sit on the tongue and give itself up one sweet degree at a time.

*Crunch it,* I prayed. *Crunch it, my lord, and let me go.*

In a gap between two of the emperor's sentences, Dan's stomach growled the length of the hall, and Nillious paused, faintly appalled, as if the carpet had passed a remark. The pause was so beautiful I nearly wept. Then he went on.

And then, at last, he leaned back. He sank into the velvet as heat sinks into a stone, all at once and completely, and on the fifth step the tail slowed to the tick of a clock in an empty house.

"Artorias. Take the General and his warm thing to the guest wing. Have them fed. Soft things wilt."

Artorias didn't answer; he doesn't. He walked down the runner, and the lanterns he passed dimmed a little in their glass, and he stopped beside Dan and waited. Dan got up without using his hands — kept them cupped and stood up around them — looked at me once more, got the smile, and went. As they passed the third pillar on the left, Drago turned his head toward it. Only that. Then the cold closed around them, and they went away down the long hall: a tall blue shape, a boy, and a small red light going out by degrees in the boy's hands.

Nillious lifted two fingers from the armrest and flicked them toward the doors: the gesture you'd use to send back soup.

That was me.

I bowed. I turned. I walked down that runner at precisely the pace of a man with nowhere to be, which is the hardest pace there is. The doors drew their jaws shut behind me. I made it forty steps along the corridor at a walk, out of respect for the frieze.

Then I ran.

My way home runs through the balcony gallery. Its arches stand open to the night, scalloped down their inner curves, and the pillars between them climb in carved bands, leaf on lotus on leaf, each with a glass lantern burning at its foot. More lanterns line the rail like votives left by a congregation that has gone home. The parapet is stone lattice, and the moonlight comes through it and lies on the flagstones in a scatter of bright flecks, as though someone had walked the gallery ahead of me with a hole in his purse. I always slow down there. It looks like money.

Beyond the arches: dark treetops, a long still water with a few far lights on it, and over them the moon, enormous and white, hung in the third arch as if placed there for effect.

The moon here is always full.

I've counted. Seventy-three nights I've walked this gallery, and every one of them the same flawless coin, not a sliver gone. And there shouldn't be a moon at all. I'm inside a fire station. I've stood in the gravel and looked at its roof.

A thing that comes round exactly the same every night ought to bother me more than most. It bothers me for precisely the length of the gallery. Then I shrug. If anyone was ever going to take a moon and keep it full on a shelf, it would be this family.

I took my staircase two steps at a time, in the dark, without needing to look.

Nobody locks anything here; nobody needs to. I stopped on the threshold. Habit. No one had come about the booking. Another night, then.

It's too much. The bed stands under a canopy of teal silk so fine the lanterns shine through it, its curtains spilling onto the floor in loose blue drifts, like water that has been told it can stay. Teal divans heaped with brocade. A carpet of deep blue worked all over in gold. Pierced gold lanterns scattering small stars across the walls. Little round tables, each with its own lantern on its own tray, as though every table had been issued a candle in case it was afraid of the dark. And across the far wall, three tall cusped arches, each hung with a sheer curtain that never quite settles, because the air comes up from below and lifts it.

Over all of it, like a picnic on an altar, my things. The room was built for a prince to recline in while someone fanned him; I've made it the bedroom of a teenager who won the lottery and hasn't told his parents. Cans in a row on a table with carved dragon feet. Twelve power banks along a divan like rounds in a bandolier, because there isn't a socket in the whole palace — dragons never needed to invent the plug — so once a week I carry them through to Reykjavík and charge them at a hot dog stand whose owner thinks I'm a student.

I went to the balcony first. I always do. The heat met me at the arches, dry and spiced, coming up from underneath. Far underneath — so far that the first time I looked I sat down on the floor without meaning to — lay the depths.

Walls go down there: towers, round and colossal, shouldered together like a stand of stone trees, climbing out of a gulf of warm haze, every one crowded with windows, thousands, all lit, as if someone had opened every door of an advent calendar at once. Between two of the greatest stands a gate, an arch the palace's front door could walk through without ducking, and through it pours an avenue of light going away into the deep. Awnings. Cooking smoke. Crowds in a slow glittering current, too far down to be anyone. And among them the beasts — huge, gray, draped in red and gold, swaying through the gate under their loads, each step about as long as a sentence. From up here they look like elephants. I'm almost sure they aren't.

I've never been down. The depths are closed to outsiders, and on that one subject, it turns out, so am I. I leaned on the rail and looked anyway, mouth slightly open, a kid at an aquarium. I live above *that*. Nobody ever asked me for a deposit.

Then I went to see about the chips.

Alone, I let the smile go. It came off all at once, like a wet shirt, and underneath, my face did what it liked for the first time since the laundromat. What it liked was to grin — the real one, the one nobody gets — and I felt my lip catch on a canine.

Every dragon in this house has a hoard. Mine is in a cedar chest built for ceremonial robes.

Nóa Síríus milk chocolate in its blue paper, stacked like bullion, which in every way that matters it is. Draumur — it means *dream*; it's licorice in a chocolate coat, exactly as specific as I'd expect an Icelandic dream to be. Þristur, also chocolate and licorice, because this country found one good idea and has refused, with admirable discipline, to be distracted from it. Tópas. Ópal, the blue kind, which tastes like cough medicine and which Icelanders eat for pleasure, and so, now, do I. Haribo bears in colors no bear has ever been. Paprika chips, loved here with the fervor you'd expect a country this dark to save for the sun. Energy drinks, which I drink warm. Everyone says they're vile warm. Everyone says a great many things.

I cracked a can, unclipped the chips, and put a whole fistful in my mouth, and the taste went off on my tongue like a struck match — salt, smoke, sweet red pepper — and I shut my eyes.

This planet is *cool*!

There is so much *in* it! They put air in chip bags on purpose, a little pillow of nothing so the chips won't break — somebody engineered that, for *crisps*! They print the day everything runs out right on the wrapper, and nobody finds it sad; they just buy another. Every house keeps lightning in its walls behind a little plastic plate, and you press a switch and it *comes*! The food is so *good*, and everything is so *new*, and it stays new — next year there'll be a new phone and a new flavor of chip and a new song everybody hates.

Back there, the same dawn came up over the same white stairs every morning, and the same gods died on schedule, and everyone already knew how it ended, because it had ended that way the last time, and the time before.

Breaking that Amphoreus cage was totally worth it.

I lay back on the carpet with paprika on my fingers and the card warm on my chest and laughed once at the ceiling. Far behind me, in a hall full of smoke, they had the gentle young leader of the Dragon Clan — the soft voice, the honest eyes, the priest's smile, the one who comes to find people. They'd watched him all evening. They'll watch him tomorrow.

*And no one suspects him~*

I wedged myself into the divan cushions with the chips and the Haribo and woke the console. Its clock said 23:41; in a palace that has never seen a morning, it's the only thing that knows the time. The game is called *Rain over Kestrel Academy*: a transfer student, a boarding school where everyone is beautiful and hiding something. I find it very relaxing. It's nothing like my life.

The girl in the yellow raincoat was still on her bridge. At the bottom of the screen: *[Tell her the truth.]* *[Say nothing.]* I'd had days to think about it. I picked the second, obviously. She smiled as if I'd said something kind, and the music turned into a slow piano thing.

It was somewhere in the piano that I heard it.

Breathing, on the other side of my door. Not a person's — too slow, too deep, too much air in it: a long, patient draw, like a forge bellows worked by someone with all night to spare; a pause you could have balanced a coin on; then the release, warm enough that the fringe of the carpet by the door lifted and lay down again. Under the door, the strip of lantern light from the corridor had gone dark in the middle, the way it does when somebody is standing there.

I sat with a chip halfway to my mouth.

Then the strip of light was whole again. I never heard anything walk away. The corridor was lit, and the piano went on.

It's a big house. Dragons walk their own halls at night; so would I. And somebody has to count the spoons.

I ate the chip.

On the screen the rain thinned, and the bridge went white, and a small box unfolded in the middle of the light with one word in it.

*Continue?*

It always asks, as though there were some chance I'd say no. I pressed yes.

Far below, something exhaled. It came up out of the depths and over the balcony rail in one long warm breath, sweet with spice and smoke, and all three curtains lifted at once and stood out into the room, filled, and slowly let themselves down; and I was already reaching for the Haribo, getting my thumb into the seam and pulling too hard, so the bag split along its whole length and bears went everywhere, red and green and that clear one nobody can identify, bouncing off the silk and the gold, and I was down on my knees after them with one arm under the divan to the shoulder, feeling around in the warm dark for the green one, while above me a morning I hadn't seen yet faded up over a town I'd never been to, and the first line of the next scene began to type itself across the bottom of the screen, letter by letter.
