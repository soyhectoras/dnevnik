# Chapter Two: The Sly Messiah

The throne room was sweating.

It did that when the emperor was angry. Kage had never found a better word for it. The heat didn't rise so much as *thicken*. It sank into the carpets and soaked the velvet and pooled at the foot of the dais like something spilled, slow and dark and syrupy. The braziers burned a low, clotted red. The smoke under the vaulting didn't drift; it hung there, heavy as wet silk, and every breath Kage took tasted of resin and hot iron and something underneath them both, something animal and furious, like walking into a cave and realising the cave is breathing.

The gold leaf on the columns was beading. Actual beads, tiny bright drops gathering in the flutes, as if the room were too hot even for gold.

*Lovely,* Kage thought. *Very atmospheric. Ten out of ten. I would like to die now, please, in my sleep, which I have not had.*

He sat at his table and sorted paper.

The table was his. It stood at the foot of the dais, on the left, a little below and a little behind where the light fell. It was low and black and lacquered, carved along its edges with coiling dragons whose eyes were chips of garnet, and it was, as far as Kage knew, the only piece of furniture in the entire throne room that had been built to human scale. The emperor had ordered it made in his second month, after watching Kage sit cross-legged on the bottom step with a laptop balanced on his knees for nine hours straight. He hadn't said anything about it. One morning it was simply there, with a cushion of dark red silk in front of it, and a brass lamp, and a cup of tea already steaming.

Kage had stared at that table for a very long time. It had felt like being given a desk. It had also felt, in some small, cold place at the back of his skull, like being given a *basket*.

He stacked the arena photographs. He squared their edges. He put them down.

*Do not yawn.*

The fog in his head was thick enough to lean on. He'd slept, by his own generous estimate, two hours. Perhaps two and a half, if he counted the forty minutes between three and four in the morning when he had been technically unconscious but also being *rearranged*, which in his opinion should not count as sleep but as freight.

It had started the moment they came back through the portal. The emperor had come home from that closed little arena, from the Dragonoid's face and the Dragonoid's words and the Dragonoid's boy standing there with his friends, in a rage so black that the corridors had gone dim around him as he passed. He had not said anything. That was always worse. He had walked to the chambers with Kage under one arm like a parcel, put him in the nest, and then *prowled*.

All night. Up and down. Kage had lain in the pillows with his eyes shut and listened to the claws on the floor, the low continuous growl that came and went like surf, the sudden ripping hiss of silk as something in the dark took its feelings out on a cushion. Every forty minutes, like a sentry changing the guard, the emperor had come back to the nest, dropped into it with the full weight of a collapsing building, hauled Kage against his chest, buried his face in Kage's hair, breathed him in for a long, long time, growled at the door as if the door had personally insulted him, and then got up and started prowling again.

At four in the morning he had come back, found Kage three centimetres further away than where he'd left him, made a noise of deep and wounded offence, and dragged him back by the ankle. With his tail.

*Three centimetres,* Kage thought, shuffling the Ventri brawl analytics into chronological order. *I rolled over. In my sleep. Like a mammal. And I was retrieved like a stolen sheep.*

So: fog. Thick, warm, sticky fog behind his eyes. The heat in the room wasn't helping. It lay on his shoulders like a fur blanket and crept under his collar, and it kept trying to fold him gently forward over the table, the way you fold a letter, so that his forehead could rest on the lacquer and his eyes could close and—

*Do not.*

*Yawn.*

The yawn came anyway. He felt it coming the way you feel a sneeze, from somewhere low behind the breastbone, a slow tidal pull at the hinge of his jaw. He clamped his molars together. He breathed through his nose. He felt his nostrils flare and his eyes water and the muscles at the corners of his jaw creak like old rope, and he did not open his mouth, and he did not open his mouth, and the yawn rose up behind his teeth and pushed against them and finally, sulkily, went back down.

His eyes were wet. He blinked them dry and squared the photographs again.

Because here was the thing. Among dragons, an open jaw in the presence of a superior was not tiredness. It was not boredom. It was *teeth*. A wide, slow gape, showing the whole mouth, the back of the throat, the full equipment, was the oldest gesture there was, and it said, very clearly and in every language that had ever been spoken with fangs: *I am not afraid of you. Come and try.*

Artorias had explained this to him once, gravely, in his first week, after Kage had yawned during a council and the entire throne room had gone silent and every dragon in it had turned its head towards him at the same moment, like a field of sunflowers turning towards a very small, very stupid sun.

The emperor had found it funny. That time. He had looked down at Kage from the throne with his chin on his fist and an expression of slow, deep, terrible delight, and said, *Was that a challenge, little strategist?* and Kage had said, *No, Your Radiance, it was Tuesday,* and the emperor had laughed — actually laughed, a sound like a landslide — and nobody had died.

Today was not that time.

Today the tolerance level of the emperor of all Vestroia for the human species had dropped from its usual setting, which was *perhaps one in two of them has a use*, to a flat, clean, gleaming zero. Kage could feel it on his skin. It wasn't about him. He knew it wasn't about him. It was about a boy in a red jacket and three of his friends and one smug red dragon who had stood in a closed arena and said the word *mistake* in front of witnesses.

But zero was zero. And a puddle was a puddle. And Kage had seen what was left on the wall of the east corridor after the last courtier who had tested the emperor's patience on a bad day, and he had no wish whatsoever to become a decorative feature of the east corridor, glistening.

So he did not yawn. He sorted paper. He was a very quiet, very still, very small human, sitting at a very small table, in the shadow of the dais, being furniture.

He was good at being furniture. It was one of his best skills.

* * *

"*Again,*" said the emperor.

The word came down from the throne like a block of hot stone dropped from a height.

The scout on the carpet flinched. He was young, for a dragon — Kage put him at a few centuries — in a human avatar that was all long limbs and nervous copper scales scattered over the cheekbones like freckles, two short, neat horns, and a tail that was currently trying very hard to wrap itself around his own leg and hide. His name was Garrow. He worked in the outer scouting network. Kage had signed his travel expenses twice, and both times there had been a line item for *rabbits, live* that nobody had ever explained.

"They have—" Garrow swallowed. "They have formed a clan, Radiance."

"I heard you the first time."

"You said *again*, Radiance—"

"I wanted to hear it *again*." The emperor's voice did not rise. It did something worse; it went *down*, into a register that Kage felt in the soles of his feet and the fillings of his teeth, a register where the words stopped being words and started being weather. "I wanted to hear you say it slowly. So that I could taste it."

Nillious was not lounging today.

On a good day the emperor lay across his throne like a big cat in a sunbeam — one leg stretched long, one arm draped over the gold dragon's head, the black robe falling open, a lazy, molten sprawl that made the throne look like an expensive cushion. Today he sat upright. Forward. Both elbows on his knees, both hands loosely clasped between them, the long dark claws laced together, so that the gold dragon heads on either side of him seemed to crouch at his shoulders like a pair of hunting dogs. His hair poured down over his shoulders and back, black and heavy and threaded with gold, and the light from the braziers ran along the gold streaks in it like lava along a seam. The horns rose from it, ridged and dark and swept back, branching into their broken crown.

And his eyes.

Kage had looked once, when they came in, and then had found something very important to do with a stapler.

The eyes were gold today. Not amber, not honey. Pure, hard, molten gold, the colour of metal in a crucible right before it's poured, and the pupils in them were not slits. They were *lines*. Two thin black cuts, so narrow they were barely there, and when the emperor turned his head they did not move with it. They stayed on what they were looking at, the way a hawk's head stays still while its body sways on a branch.

Right now they were looking at Garrow, and Garrow was dying of it, a little, in real time.

"A clan," said Garrow, slowly, obediently. "The Dragonoid and his human. The human girl, Mia. The other two. Juno. Griff. Their Bakugan — Ventri, Trox—" He faltered. "All types, Radiance. Mixed. A clan of — of no type. They're calling it—" His throat worked. "*The Misfit Clan.*"

The silence after it was long enough to grow things in.

Then, from the steps of the dais, someone giggled.

It was a high, soft, delighted sound, and it came out of a body the size of a wardrobe. Smough was sitting on the third step with his knees spread and his elbows on them, and in one enormous hand he was holding a bone. It had been, quite recently, part of a leg; Kage could see that much and had decided not to see the rest. Smough's avatar was built like a cathedral bell — vast, round, heavy, solid all the way through — with a mop of pale gold curls on top and a round rosy face beneath them, so pink and cheerful and dimpled that he looked like a painting of a cherub that had been left out in the rain and grown to the size of a door. His eyes were small and bright and blue. His teeth were very white. There were a great many of them.

"*Misfits,*" Smough said, and giggled again, and crunched the end off the bone. "Oh, that's sweet. That's so sweet. They named themselves after what they are."

"It's not sweet," snapped Ornstein. "It's an *insult*."

Ornstein did not sit. Ornstein never sat; Kage was beginning to suspect he'd been built without the necessary joints. He stood at the emperor's left hand, a step above Smough and a world apart from him — lean and quick and golden, as narrow as Smough was wide. His hair was a spiked gold mane that stood up off his skull like a lion's ruff, and his eyes were amber, and his tail was scaled in bright, hard, polished gold and never, ever still. When he was angry the air around him smelled of ozone, the clean, sharp, frightening smell of the moment before lightning, and right now the whole left side of the dais smelled like a thunderstorm.

"It's a *banner*," Ornstein said. He bared his teeth at Garrow as though Garrow had named the clan personally. "They're waving it in our faces. *Look at us, we don't kneel, we don't need a type, we don't need an emperor*—"

"You don't need to translate," said Smough comfortably. "We all heard."

"Some of us," Ornstein hissed, "are *thinking*."

"Some of us are eating."

"Some of us are eating *all the time*—"

"*Enough.*"

It was not loud. But the gold beads on the nearest column trembled and ran, and the smoke under the vaults flinched, and Ornstein closed his mouth so fast his teeth clicked. Smough stopped chewing. His small blue eyes went up to the throne, and stayed there, bright and attentive, the cheerful face suddenly very still, the way a dog's face is still when it has heard its name said in a certain tone.

The third one had not moved at all.

Artorias stood at the foot of the second column on the right, in the shadow just past the brazier light, as he always did, as though he had been placed there by a sculptor and asked to keep an eye on things. Tall. Lean in a dangerous, unflashy way — the build of a wolf at the end of a long winter, all reach and endurance. Blue-black hair, straight and heavy, falling past his shoulders. Deep blue eyes that missed nothing and offered nothing. His left arm hung at his side, motionless, bound from shoulder to fingertips in strips of black cloth like a mummy's, and Kage had never once seen it move. Nobody spoke about the arm. Kage had asked Ornstein, once, very early on, back when he still asked things, and Ornstein had looked at him with flat amber contempt and said, *The dark took it,* and walked away, and that had been the whole of the conversation.

Artorias was watching the emperor. Artorias was always watching the emperor. But every so often — Kage had noticed this, and filed it, and not thought about it — the deep blue eyes moved, without the head turning, to the small black table at the foot of the dais. And rested there. And moved away.

*The three killing machines,* Kage thought, sorting the Juno file from the Griff file. *Assembled. All present. Full set. Collect them all.*

He had other names for them in his head, but those were for days when he'd slept.

"Where," said the emperor, "are they now?"

Garrow's tail had made it all the way around his own ankle and was gripping it like a child's hand. "We — I — the arena was closed, Radiance. A private portal. When they left it, I followed the human girl's signature to—" He stopped. He could see, as Kage could see, where the end of the sentence went. "I lost the trail."

"You lost," said Nillious, "the trail."

"The portal folded, Radiance. It was — it was very clean work. Someone knew how to—"

The emperor stood up.

Kage did not look up. He kept his eyes on the Griff file, on a blurry photograph of a boy in a sports jacket mid-shout, and he felt the movement more than saw it — felt the heat of the room *lurch*, the way heat lurches in a kiln when the door is opened, felt the shadow come down the steps over his table and his hands and his papers like a wing.

Nillious did not hurry down the seven steps of the dais. He never hurried. Each step was a slow, deliberate fall of weight, the black robe swinging open around his legs, the tail sliding after him over the gold-edged stone with a whisper like a blade being drawn very slowly from a very long sheath. Smough leaned aside to let him pass, still holding the bone, beaming up at him like a child watching fireworks. The smell of him rolled ahead of him — smoke and spice and hot metal and that deep, animal musk that Kage's body had long ago learned to recognise before his mind did — and the air went thick with it, so thick Kage had to make himself breathe slowly through his nose, so thick it seemed to coat the back of his throat like honey.

The emperor stopped in front of Garrow.

He was a head and a half taller. In the avatar, he was a head and a half taller; in the truth, he was eleven storeys taller, and the avatar did very little to hide that. Garrow went down onto one knee without being told. Then both knees. Then his forehead nearly to the carpet.

"Radiance—"

"*Up.*"

Garrow came up onto his knees, shaking.

Nillious took him by the horn.

It was a casual movement. Almost gentle. He reached down and closed his long dark hand around the left horn — the short, neat, copper-dusted horn — the way you might take hold of a doorknob, and tilted Garrow's head back, and looked down into his face from very close, his own face lowered, his hair falling forward and curtaining the two of them so that for a moment there was nothing in Garrow's world but those gold eyes with the black lines through them.

"A clean portal," the emperor said softly. "Someone knew how. And so you lost my enemy's trail on the first day of his treason, and came home, and knelt on my carpet, and told me about *clean work*." His head tilted. Too far. The neck bending at an angle that made something in Kage's spine curl up and hide. "Do you know what I hear, Garrow, when you say that?"

"Radiance, I—"

"I hear a dragon," said Nillious, "who would rather admire a human's trick than be ashamed of his own failure."

And then he twisted his wrist.

The sound was not loud. It was a dry, deep, splintering *crack*, like a green branch snapped across a knee, and it went through the hall and came back from thirty-six columns as a chorus of small cracks, fainter and fainter. Garrow didn't scream. Kage would think about that later — would think about it, and slide off it, and think about it again at three in the morning with an arm across his chest. Garrow didn't scream. He made a sound like all the air going out of a bellows, and his whole body went rigid from the shoulders down, and his tail let go of his ankle and lashed once against the carpet, hard enough to tear it.

The emperor held the horn up in the brazier light and looked at it with mild, absent interest, as one might look at a key.

Where it had been, on Garrow's head, there was a jagged stump, and from the stump ran something that was not blood. It was thick and slow and dark, dark gold shading to black, and it moved down Garrow's temple like honey down the side of a jar, and it smelled of iron and burnt sugar, and where it dripped onto the carpet the threads smoked.

"Thank you, Radiance," Garrow whispered.

*He's thanking him,* Kage thought, with great clarity, with his hands squaring a stack of paper. *He's thanking him. He's going to go back to the barracks and sit down and someone is going to bring him a cup of something and he will say, the emperor was merciful today.*

*And the thing is,* said some other, older, quieter part of him, *the emperor was.*

His hands were perfectly steady. He noticed this. He was rather proud of it. Some people would have had shaking hands, after a thing like that. Kage didn't. Kage's hands were calm and clean and dry and sorting paper, and that was good, because shaking hands were a weakness, and weakness was something you did not show in this room, and he did not, at all, at any point, wonder why his hands *should* have been shaking, or why they were not.

The heat at the side of his neck pulsed, slow and warm. He thought about staplers.

"Smough," said the emperor, without looking round.

"Mm?" Smough's face lit up like a lamp.

Nillious tossed him the horn.

Smough caught it one-handed, beaming. He turned it over in his huge fingers. He sniffed it, long and appreciative, eyes half-closed, the way a man sniffs a good cigar. "Oh, young," he said fondly. "Young and *nervous*. You can always tell with the nervous ones. Sour at the root." He put the bone he'd been gnawing down on the step beside him with great care, like a man setting aside an appetiser, and then, with an expression of deep and simple happiness, put the end of the horn in his mouth and bit.

The crunch was very loud.

Kage looked at the Juno file. Juno, the file said, was fifteen and had a twelve-point lead in her school's regional science fair and had once been cautioned by police for brawling in a public fountain. Very interesting. Very interesting file.

"Go," said the emperor to Garrow. "Find them. Bring me the trail, or don't come back. If you don't come back, I'll send Smough to find out why."

Smough waved at Garrow cheerfully with the horn.

Garrow went. He went on his feet, which was a kind of miracle, holding his hand to the side of his head, the dark gold running out between his fingers, and the doors at the far end of the hall opened for him and closed behind him without a sound, and the hall was quiet again, apart from the steady, thoughtful crunching coming from the third step.

The emperor stood where he was. He did not go back to the throne.

He stood in the middle of the blood-coloured carpet with his back to the dais and his hands hanging loose at his sides, and the smoke rose off his skin in thick, slow ropes, and the air around him rippled like the air above a road in August. His tail moved behind him, a long heavy black arc on the floor, the gold-edged scales catching the light. Back. Forth. Back. Each sweep a little harder than the last.

"They will regret it," he said.

He wasn't speaking to anyone. Or he was speaking to the hall, to the columns, to the smoke, to the whole empire. His voice was very quiet and very full, the way a river is quiet and full just before it floods.

"The Dragonoid stood in my house. Drank from my cups. Lost in my arena, under my eyes, and knelt — *he knelt* — and took my clan's name into his mouth. And then he took it out again, in front of those *creatures*, and called it a *mistake*." The tail cracked once against the floor like a whip. A brazier on its iron tripod three columns away rocked, and spilled a small avalanche of coals, and nobody moved to pick them up. "He made me a promise and he broke it in public. And now he plays at kingship. With *strays*."

"Shall I go and get him?" Smough asked, through a mouthful of horn. "I'd go. I'd be ever so quick."

"No."

"I'd bring back *most* of him—"

"*No.*"

Ornstein's tail was lashing. "Radiance. Give me the Dragonoid. Give me one day. I'll put him on his knees in front of his own *Misfits* and make him say it again — make him say *mistake* — and this time he'll mean it about himself—"

"You'll do nothing," said the emperor, "until I say what will be done."

He turned. Slowly. The robe swinging. And the gold eyes went across the hall, across the three of them, and Kage felt them pass over his little table like a hand passing over a candle flame, close enough to feel the heat, not close enough to burn.

"They will regret it," Nillious said again, almost tenderly. "All of them. The Dragonoid, and his boy, and his strays. I will make them *crawl* back to my door, and when they are lying on the threshold, I will let them beg to come in. And then I will decide." A slow smile. Too many teeth. "I want to see the Dragonoid's face when he understands that I am deciding."

It was, Kage thought, very much in character. It was also a promise, and the emperor always kept his promises, and Kage would have bet the entire black card that somewhere in the human world at this exact moment Dan Kouzo was eating a sandwich with no idea that a dragon the size of a tower block had just personally decided to make him crawl.

*Poor sandwich,* he thought. *Poor kid. Poor me, who has to schedule it.*

He put the Juno file on top of the Griff file and reached for the Mia file.

* * *

The emperor went back up the steps. He didn't sit. He paced the top of the dais instead, from one gold dragon head to the other, the tail dragging behind him, and every time he turned at the end of a pass the black robe swung out and the heat of him swung out with it and rolled down the steps like a wave and broke over Kage's table and soaked through his shirt.

Kage was beginning to sweat. A thin line of it at his hairline, under the blue fall of his fringe. A trickle at the back of his neck, under his collar, sliding slow and warm down his spine. He did not wipe it. Wiping it would be moving. Moving would be noticed.

"The Dragonoid," the emperor said, "I can predict."

He said it to the top of the dais, to the empty throne, to the smoke.

"I have known him since before he was hatched. I know what he wants. I know how he moves. I know what he will do when he is cornered and what he will do when he is proud and what he will do when he is afraid, and he is always one of the three." Pass. Turn. The heat broke over Kage's shoulders. "He is a Bakugan. A dragon. Fire, pride, honour, stupidity. I could draw you a map of the inside of his skull. I *have*."

Ornstein made a low sound of agreement. Smough, licking his fingers, nodded wisely.

"But these—" said Nillious.

And his lip curled, and something came into his voice that Kage had heard only a handful of times — not contempt. Contempt was easy for him, contempt was his resting state. This was something else. Something almost like *disgust*, and under the disgust, very faint, something almost like unease.

"These *things*."

Pass. Turn.

"The boy Dan. The girl Mia. The other two. Juno. Griff." He said the names as if he were spitting out seeds. "They do not make sense. They stopped a brawl, Ornstein. In the middle. For a *dog*."

"A dog, Radiance," Ornstein agreed, with deep and righteous loathing.

"A dog came into their arena, and wanted to know if they had *snacks*, and they *stopped*." The emperor's tail lashed. "And the Dragonoid let them. The Dragonoid, who once fought me for three days over a mountain, stood in the middle of a brawl and let a small, wet, hairy animal end it. And then they all laughed. I *heard* them laugh. I have Garrow's recording." His lip pulled back off his teeth. "They want nothing. They want *everything*. They break the law and then they cry about the law. They have no territory and no type and no blood and no sense, and they lie in front of each other and their faces go red and they *tell the truth anyway*. They are soft. They are stupid. They are short-lived and weak and they—" He stopped pacing. His voice dropped. "And they *move*. In ways I do not see coming."

That, Kage thought, was the closest thing to an admission of weakness he had ever heard come out of that mouth. He made a careful note of it. In his head. In very small letters. In a locked drawer.

He had the Mia file open in front of him now.

It was mostly photographs. Toma's analytics team had pulled stills from Garrow's recording and printed them, and they were laid out across the lacquer in a neat grid, and Kage had been looking at them for a while now. Not at Mia, particularly. At the background. At the corners. At what was standing behind whom.

There was one photograph — near the end of the sequence, after the Misfits had found out, after Drago had said his piece — where the four humans were standing in a loose half-circle in the closed arena, and Drago was standing behind them. Not in front. Not beside Dan. *Behind them*. A little back. A little to the side. Head lowered. Wings — no, he was in avatar, no wings — *shoulders* drawn in, making himself smaller, letting the light fall on the humans and not on him. His face, in the still, was turned toward Dan. And the expression on it was —

Kage tilted his head. He picked up the photograph and brought it closer to the lamp.

The expression on it was *humble*.

Drago. Humble. Drago, who had looked at the emperor of all Vestroia in this very throne room and said *you're emperor of nothing.* Humble, in the back of a photograph, at the exact moment his human was telling a crowd of his friends that he had made a terrible mistake by trusting the Dragon Clan.

*Huh,* thought Kage.

He put the photo down. He picked up the next one. Drago again, in the back. Dan in front, red-faced, earnest, hands open. The girl, Mia, looking at Dan with her chin up, in a way that suggested she was about to be the one to say something important. And Drago behind them all, quiet, his head bowed, his eyes on his boy, his whole body arranged like a — like a —

Like a painting.

Like a very particular kind of painting. The kind that hung in very particular kinds of buildings.

He picked up the third photograph.

"*Huh,*" he said, quietly, to his nose. "That's strange."

He didn't mean to say it aloud. That was the fog. That was two hours of sleep and a room that was sweating and a dragon horn being eaten twenty feet away. It just came out, low, absent, the way you talk to yourself when you're doing a crossword. His brows were drawn down. His lips were pursed. His face had on it — he would realise this later, much too late — the precise, focused, faraway expression of a man who has just smelled smoke in a building where nothing should be burning.

"How *strange*," he murmured again, to the photograph. "Why would he—"

The pacing had stopped.

Kage noticed that about a second and a half after it happened, which was about a second and a half too late.

The silence came down on him from above like a lid on a jar. The crunching on the third step had stopped too. The ozone smell from the left of the dais had sharpened, very suddenly, into something that prickled on the skin. And the heat that had been rolling down the steps in waves with each of the emperor's turns had stopped rolling and was simply *there*, pouring down over him and around him, close and heavy and terribly, terribly attentive, like a hand that had been hovering an inch above his head and was now, very slowly, coming to rest.

Kage looked up.

He did it slowly. He did it the way you look up in the middle of a forest when you have heard a branch creak above you in a place where there should be no branches.

The emperor of all Vestroia was standing at the top of the dais, perfectly still, with his head tilted down and to the side at that awful, too-far angle, looking at him. His hair hung forward. The black lines of his pupils had widened, just a fraction — just enough. And the corner of his mouth had begun, very slowly, to rise.

Ornstein was looking at him, golden and narrow and offended, as if a piece of furniture had spoken.

Smough was looking at him, round and pink and interested, the half-eaten horn held forgotten in one hand.

And at the foot of the second column on the right, Artorias had turned his head — actually turned it, for the first time that morning — and the deep blue eyes rested on Kage with an expression of grave, attentive, unhurried interest. Like a wolf who has heard a rabbit say something clever.

"Something," said the emperor softly, "is *strange*, little strategist?"

*No,* screamed every cell in Kage's body. *No no no no no no no. Why. Why now. Why* today. *Why today, of all the days in the calendar, the day of the horn, the day of zero tolerance, the day I have had two hours of sleep and a stolen sheep's worth of dignity, why today did the emperor and all three of his personal killing machines decide to look at* me—

"You know something." The emperor came down one step. The heat came with him. "You're making a face. I know that face." Another step. Smoke curled from his lips, slow, unhurried. "That is the face you make at the ledgers when somebody has been stealing."

*Ah,* Kage thought. *Yes. That's fair. I do make that face.*

"You were born one of these strange creatures, too," said Nillious. Another step. He was halfway down the dais now, and his voice had gone low and smooth and almost warm, and the warmth of it went down the back of Kage's neck and pooled at the base of his spine, and stayed there. "So. Tell me what is strange, Kage. Tell me what you see that I don't."

It was the name that did it.

The emperor didn't use his name very often. *Little strategist*, mostly. *Little one*. Sometimes, in the nest, at three in the morning, a word in the old tongue that Kage had never asked the meaning of and never would. But *Kage* — said like that, in the middle of the throne room, slow and deliberate, with the gold eyes holding him — went into him like a hook into a fish's mouth. He felt it catch. He felt it pull.

The mark on his neck flared hot.

And Kage, eighteen years old, strategist of the Dragon Clan, who had once talked his way out of a police station, a debt collector's car, and a very small boat, sat at his very small table under the gaze of four very large dragons, and opened his mouth, and heard himself say, in a voice that was perfectly calm and only slightly hoarse:

"It's Drago, Your Radiance." He held up the photograph. "He's acting like a human."


The hiss came from four directions at once.

It was not a human sound. Humans hiss through their teeth, a thin little whistle of annoyance. This came from *deeper*: from the chest, from the throat, from somewhere behind the breastbone where a dragon keeps its fire, a long, dry, rattling exhale like steam escaping a cracked boiler. Ornstein's lips peeled back off his teeth. Smough's round pink face didn't change at all, but the sound came out of him anyway, low and continuous, like a kettle left on a stove in an empty house. Even Artorias — grave, silent Artorias — let a breath out through his nose that had an edge on it.

And the emperor.

The emperor's hiss was not loud. It didn't need to be. It came down the dais in a single long breath that Kage felt on his face, hot and dry and smelling of struck flint, and every flame in every brazier in the throne room leaned toward the sound and then sprang back as though it had been slapped.

*Acting like a human.* In this room. About a dragon. To a dragon.

*Oh,* Kage thought, from somewhere very far away, *I've done it. I've finally done it. That was an insult. In dragon, that was a huge insult. That was "your mother was a gecko." Goodbye, everyone. Goodbye, black card. Tell the lamb with apricots I loved it.*

"Careful," said Nillious.

One word. Soft. And it was not a warning so much as a *weather report*, and the weather was: *the next sentence will decide whether you are a strategist or a stain.*

Kage did the only thing he could think of.

He kept sorting paper.

He put the photograph down — gently — on the lacquer in front of him, and he picked up the next one, and he squared it with the first, and he did not look up, and he spoke in the same calm, slightly hoarse, faintly puzzled voice he had been using before, the voice of a man thinking aloud over a crossword, as though four dragons had not just hissed at him and the air was not full of the smell of a lightning strike.

"I mean," he said, "it's strange. It's very strange. Because he's a dragon, Your Radiance. You said so yourself. Fire, pride, honour. When he's cornered, he fights. When he's insulted, he challenges. That's what he did here, in this room." He laid down another photograph. "But that's not what he did in the closed arena."

The hissing hadn't stopped. It had, however, changed pitch.

"Look where he's standing."

He didn't hold the photograph up. He didn't dare make that much movement. He simply turned it on the table, a quarter-turn, so that it faced the dais, and tapped it once with a finger.

"He's in the back. Behind the humans. Not beside Dan — *behind* him. Head down. Shoulders in. He's making himself small." Tap. "A dragon, in front of the people he wants to impress, making himself *small*. When has the Dragonoid ever, in his whole life, made himself small?"

Silence. Then Ornstein, through his teeth: "He was ashamed."

"Maybe," said Kage, laying down another. "But look at this one. He's still in the back. Still small. And the human girl, Mia — she's in front, and she's about to speak. And look at her face. That's the face of someone who has decided she's going to *lead*. And look at his." Tap. "That's not shame. That's a dragon watching his trap close and trying very, very hard not to smile."

The hissing stopped.

It didn't trail off. It simply stopped, all four at once, like a tap being turned. And in its place, very slowly, a different kind of quiet came down over the throne room. Not the hostile quiet of before. Something heavier. Something with its head tilted.

Kage felt it on the back of his neck like breath.

*Don't look up,* he told himself. *Don't look up, don't look up, if you look up you'll see four faces paying attention to you and you'll lose your nerve and your train of thought and possibly your bladder. Keep sorting. You are a man sorting paper. You are a filing cabinet with opinions.*

"He went to the closed arena," Kage went on, "and he let himself be caught. He let Dan's friends find out. He let me walk in and tell them we'd recruited Dan. And then he stood in front of all of them — in front of *you*, Your Radiance — and said the word *mistake*." He stacked the Mia photographs. "That's not something that happened to him. That's something he *did*. On purpose. He confessed. In public. In front of his own people and his enemy at the same time."

"Why," said Artorias.

It was the first word he had said all morning. His voice was low and rough, like a blade being drawn over a whetstone, and it did not carry, and it did not need to. Kage felt it land on his table like a hand set down on a chessboard.

*Oh, good,* Kage thought. *The quiet one's interested. The quiet one is always the worst one to interest.*

"Because," he said, "a confession is a *story*, Sir Artorias. Humans love it. It's — the most human story there is. The good one who went astray. Who was tempted into the house of—" he chose the next word very, very carefully "—of a *great power*, and who saw what it was, and who walked back out and told everyone, *I was wrong, I'm sorry, I'm with you now*. And then he stands in the back and lets the humans lead and doesn't take the credit and looks at them like *that*." He tapped the humble face again. "He's not playing a dragon. He's playing a—"

He stopped.

The word was right there. It had been forming in the back of his head since the third photograph, rising up through the fog like a body through dark water, and now it broke the surface and floated there, pale and obvious, and he looked at it, and it was so perfect and so ridiculous that his mouth said it before his good sense could catch it.

"—messiah."

Another silence.

"A *what*," said Ornstein.

"A sly one," Kage said, mostly to himself, and he could hear the shape of it now, could feel the whole picture sliding together under his hands like a jigsaw. "A very sly messiah. That's — oh, that's clever. That's actually really clever, the scaly bastard—" He stopped. He heard what he'd said. He looked at the paper in his hands with great concentration. "—the Dragonoid," he amended, "is being very clever."

There was a small, delighted sound from the third step. It might have been Smough choking on horn. It might have been Smough laughing. With Smough it was frequently both.

"What," said the emperor, "is a messiah."

He said it slowly, testing each syllable, the way he said human words he hadn't eaten before. And he had come down another step while Kage wasn't looking. Kage knew this because the heat had changed — because it wasn't rolling down the dais anymore but standing over him, close, like the heat that comes off a horse in winter — and because when he finally, finally looked up from the table, the emperor was sitting on the bottom step of the dais.

Sitting. On the *step*. Two feet from the edge of Kage's table, one knee drawn up, one long arm draped across it, the claws hanging loose, the black robe pooled around him on the stone like spilled ink. His head was tilted. His hair fell over one shoulder in a black-and-gold river that reached the floor. His eyes were on Kage's face, and the pupils had widened from lines to slits, and the gold around them had gone — not soft, it never went soft — but *bright*, the brightness of something leaning in to look more closely at a thing it has not yet decided whether to eat or to keep.

Kage had never seen the emperor sit on the step before.

He felt his own heart do something unprofessional.

"It's—" He wet his lips. That was a mistake; the emperor's eyes dropped to his mouth and stayed there for one long, hot, liquid second before they came back up, and Kage felt the back of his neck go warm and then his ears and then, God help him, his face. He looked down at the paper. "It's a human word, Your Radiance. Very old. From one of our oldest stories. It means — the one who is sent. To save everyone." He put a photograph on a pile. "Usually he's poor. Usually he comes from nowhere. A small village, a bad family, a stable, the back of a fire station. He has no army, no gold, no throne. All he has is a story, and the story goes: *the powerful are wrong, and I have come to tell you so, and if you follow me, you will be free*."

"And people follow that," said Ornstein. His voice was flat with disbelief. "A weakling with nothing, who *says things*."

"People follow that more than anything else in the world," said Kage.

"*Why?*"

"Because he's weak." Kage looked up, briefly, at Ornstein's narrow golden face, and then back down at his papers. "That's the whole trick. That's the thing you have to understand about my — about humans. A dragon follows the strongest. That makes sense; I understand it; you can *see* strength. But humans—" he shrugged, sorting "—humans are mostly weak. Most of us, most of the time, are the small one in the story. So when someone small stands up to someone enormous, every human watching thinks: *that's me*. That's *me* standing there. And they love him for it. And the more the enormous thing hurts him—" he laid down a photograph of Dan, red-faced and earnest "—the more they love him."

"That," said the emperor, "is absurd."

"Yes, Your Radiance."

"It is a *sickness*."

"Very possibly, Your Radiance."

"You are telling me that if I break the Dragonoid in half, his little clan will grow *stronger*."

Kage put down a file.

"I'm telling you," he said carefully, "that it's happened before."

And then — because the emperor was sitting on the step and looking at him like *that*, and because the fog had burned off his brain and left something sharp and bright and reckless underneath, and because he was eighteen and had had two hours of sleep and a strategist who has just seen the whole shape of the board is a very difficult thing to shut up — he told them.

* * *

He told them about Rome.

He kept his hands busy while he did it. He'd learned that early: dragons watched hands. If your hands were still, they watched your face, and if they watched your face for long enough, something in your face would eventually show them what they wanted. So he sorted. Arena analytics into one pile. Brawler profiles into another. Garrow's expense claims — *rabbits, live* — into a third, to be dealt with later, with prejudice.

"There was an empire," he said, "in the human world. A long time ago. The biggest one we'd ever had, at that point. It had roads from one end of the world to the other, and an army that had never lost a war that mattered, and an emperor who was—" he caught himself "—who *called himself* a god. And at the very edge of it, in a dusty little province nobody cared about, there was a carpenter's son."

"A *carpenter*," said Ornstein, with the same intonation another person might have used for *a slug*.

"A carpenter's son. With twelve friends. Fishermen, mostly. A tax collector. Nobody important." He stacked. "And the carpenter's son went around saying: the poor will inherit the earth. The last will be first. The powerful have it all wrong. And people listened. Not many. A few thousand, in a backwater. And the empire noticed him and did the obvious thing."

"Killed him," said Smough, with great satisfaction.

"Killed him. The way they killed thieves. Publicly. Slowly. On a hill, where everyone could see." Kage put a file down. "Problem solved, yes? Three hundred years later, the emperor of that same empire — the biggest empire in the world — was kneeling in front of the carpenter's son's god. The whole empire was. Every road. Every legion. All of it." He lined up the edges of the pile. "The empire didn't kill the story. The empire *finished* it. They gave it the one thing it was missing. An ending where the good one dies because the great power was cruel. After that, the story didn't need him anymore. It ran on its own."

The silence in the throne room had a different texture now.

It wasn't hostile. It wasn't even skeptical. It was the silence of four predators who had, without quite realising it, sat down.

Smough had put the horn down.

"The empire knelt," said Artorias softly.

"The empire knelt," Kage agreed. "To a dead carpenter."

He told them about Spartacus. A slave. A gladiator. Somebody's property, made to fight for the amusement of the crowd. Who one day decided not to, and broke out, and gathered seventy thousand other slaves behind him, and humiliated the greatest army in the world for two whole years. And when the empire finally caught him and his people, they crucified six thousand of them along the side of the main road — one every forty metres, for two hundred kilometres — so that everyone who travelled that road for months afterward would see.

"Good," said Ornstein. "That's how it's *done*."

"Two thousand years later," said Kage, "we still make films about Spartacus. Nobody remembers the general's name."

Ornstein's tail lashed once.

He told them about a girl. A peasant. Seventeen. Couldn't read. Who walked up to a king and said a voice from heaven had told her to lead his army, and was laughed at, and *led his army*, and won. And who was captured by the other side and tried, and burned alive in a public square.

"And they made her a saint," Kage said. "Holy. They built statues. Five hundred years later, people are still naming their daughters after her. The judges who burned her—" he shrugged "—are a footnote. Mostly a footnote about how badly they messed up."

"So you shouldn't eat them," said Smough slowly. He sounded genuinely distressed. As if Kage had told him that the sun had been cancelled.

"You shouldn't eat them *in public*," Kage said, before he could stop himself.

Smough's whole round face lit up again like a lamp. "Oh," he said happily. "*Oh*. That's all right, then."

*Kage,* said a voice inside his skull that sounded very much like his own, only older and more tired, *what are you doing. What are you* doing. *You are giving tactical advice to a cannibal.*

He sorted faster.

"There's a word for it," he said. "When the empire kills the small one and makes him bigger. *Martyr*. A martyr is someone whose death is a weapon. You don't kill a martyr; you *load* one. Every drop of blood is ink. Every story that gets told about it is another recruit. It's the oldest trap in human history, and empires walk into it over and over and over, because from the inside, it always looks like the obvious move." He looked up, just once, at the emperor. "The strong thing. The *dragon* thing."

The emperor looked back at him.

He hadn't moved. He was still sitting on the bottom step, still with one knee drawn up and that long arm draped across it and his head tilted at its too-far angle. But his eyes had changed again, and Kage could not have said how. They were very bright and very still and very *close*, much closer than two feet, it felt like, the gold of them so intense and so focused that it was almost a physical pressure, a heat on Kage's face, like sitting too near an open oven. His tail had come round from behind him while Kage was talking, and lay along the floor at the foot of the table, and the tip of it was moving in a slow, lazy, absorbed curl. Back. Forth. Like a cat's, when the cat is watching something through a window that it has not yet decided to kill.

"Go on," said Nillious.

His voice had gone soft. Soft and thick and dark, like smoke from a fire of wet wood. Kage felt it go down his back.

*Go on,* he thought. *Sure. Sure, I'll go on. I'll just keep explaining the history of my entire species to the dragon equivalent of a nuclear warhead. That's fine. That's a normal Tuesday thing to do. That's what they pay me the black card for.*

He went on.

* * *

"The Dragonoid," Kage said, "knows exactly what he's doing."

He'd moved on to the brawl analytics now. Toma's printouts — energy readings, B.A.M. Zone activations, the timeline of the closed-arena match between Drago and Ventri, which had ended in nothing at all. No winner. No loser. Interrupted by a dog.

"He's building the story. All of it. Look at the pieces." He set them down on the lacquer one at a time, as if he were laying out a hand of cards. "Piece one: the name. *Misfits*. That's not an insult, Lord Ornstein. That's a sermon. It's saying: *we're the ones nobody wanted. The ones without a type. The ones who don't fit in the great houses.* Every brawler in every clan who's ever been passed over, or lost a brawl and been sneered at, or been told they don't belong — they hear *Misfit Clan* and they think, *that's me*. It's a door. And it's open."

Ornstein said nothing. His mouth had gone very thin.

"Piece two: the betrayal. He'll tell it everywhere. But not the way you think. He won't say *we betrayed the Dragon Clan*. He'll say *we escaped it*. *We were in the dragon's house, and we saw what it was, and we got out.*" Kage laid down the second card. "That's a much better story. That's a story with a monster in it. And everyone loves a story about getting out of the monster's house."

He did not look at the emperor when he said *monster*. He did not need to. He could feel the heat spike against his cheek like a slap and then — strangely, slowly — subside.

*He likes it,* Kage realised, with a small cold drop in his stomach. *He likes being the monster. Of course he does. That's going to be a problem.*

"Piece three," he went on quickly. "He keeps Dan clean. You saw it. Dan's always in front, Dan's always honest, Dan's always the one who says sorry, Dan's always the one with his hands open. The Dragonoid's in the back, letting the humans be the heroes. That's not modesty. That's *staging*. Dan is the face. Drago is the—" he hunted for the word "—the *priest*. The one who explains what it all means."

"And piece four?" said Artorias.

Kage hesitated.

He looked at the last printout in his hand. It was a still from Garrow's recording: the emperor himself, in the closed arena, standing over the Misfits with his robe open and his eyes blazing, mid-word, his lips back from his teeth. It was a magnificent image. It was a terrifying image.

It was, Kage thought, exactly the image Drago wanted.

"Piece four," he said quietly, "is us."

He put the printout down on the table, face up, facing the dais.

"He needs us to strike. Hard. In public. Preferably at the boy. A messiah needs a hill to die on, and somebody has to build the hill. He's going to go around to the other clans — not to their leaders, Your Radiance; to the unhappy ones, the second-best, the brawlers who are tired of kneeling — and he's going to tell them, *you don't have to kneel*. And every time we answer that with a horn snapped off or a brawler broken, he'll hold it up and say: *see?*" He tapped the printout once, very lightly, beside the emperor's open mouth. "He's written you a part, Your Radiance. He's written you the part of the empire. The part with the hill and the nails. And the day Lord Smough brings back *most* of Dan Kouzo—"

"I'd bring back quite a lot," said Smough.

"—the Misfit Clan *wins*," said Kage. "Not the brawl. The story. And the story is what humans fight for."

He put the last printout on the last pile. He squared it. He folded his hands on top of it.

The throne room was silent.

Not hissing. Not breathing. Silent, the way the sea is silent in that long, sucking instant before the wave comes back.

And Kage realised, very slowly, the way you realise you have been standing in the road for a while and the sound you've been hearing is a truck, that he had just told the wrathful, murderous, eternally offended emperor of the dragons — to his face, in his own throne room, in front of his three personal killing machines — that he was playing a part in his enemy's story.

The heat beside him moved.

The emperor uncoiled from the step. He didn't stand. He leaned *forward* — slowly, smoothly, impossibly far, the long body extending in a way that no human body could extend, the knee unfolding, the arm sliding from it — until his face was level with Kage's face across the edge of the little lacquered table, and the black-gold curtain of his hair fell forward over the papers, over Kage's folded hands, heavy as warm water. Smoke curled from his lips. It touched Kage's mouth. It tasted of cedar and burnt sugar and *him*.

"You think," said Nillious, "that I am being *played*."

His voice was very quiet. Kage could feel every word on his skin.

"I think—" His throat had gone dry. He swallowed. The emperor's eyes followed the movement down his throat and came back up. "I think he's *trying*, Your Radiance."

"And I am walking into it."

"I think you were *invited* to walk into it."

"By the *Dragonoid*."

"By the Dragonoid."

The gold eyes held him.

And then — slowly, slowly, starting at one corner of that wide dark mouth — the emperor smiled.

It was not a nice smile. It was not even a pleased smile. It was the smile of something enormous that has been sleeping on a hoard for a thousand years and has just felt, in the dark, a single coin being lifted from the pile — and is *delighted*, utterly delighted, because nobody has dared to steal from it in a very long time, and it had begun to think that nobody ever would.

"How," the emperor breathed, "*interesting*."

Kage's stomach dropped through the floor of the throne room and kept going.

* * *

He tried to salvage it.

"It's — it's still strange, though," he said, too fast. "That's the thing. That's what I meant. It's *strange*. Because he's a Bakugan. This isn't a dragon's game. You don't play it; you've never needed to. Where would the Dragonoid even *learn* this?" He was talking to the papers now, his voice dropping back into the low, absent, crossword-mutter, because that was safer, because if he was just thinking aloud then maybe nobody was really listening. "He must have read something. Human library. A bookshop. Or—" a thought struck him and, God help him, he almost laughed "—Dan's homework. Dan had a history essay due this term, I saw it in his bag, it was something like *Heroes of Resistance, six hundred words*, he probably got a C—"

"A *what*," said Ornstein.

"A grade. A bad one. Never mind." He shuffled. "But he can't have read much. That's what's strange. He's got the *shape* of the thing. The confession, the name, the clean face, the open door. He's got all the first chapters. But he doesn't seem to know how the story usually *ends*." He frowned at the photograph of Drago's humble face. "Because there's a counter for every single piece of it. Human history's full of them. Empires that *didn't* walk into the trap. You can co-opt the story — Rome did that, in the end; they took the carpenter's god and made it the *empire's* god, and suddenly the messiah was on their side of the wall. You can make it ridiculous — a messiah can survive a sword, but he can't survive being a *joke*; nobody dies for a punchline. You can split the disciples—"

"Disciples?" said Smough.

"Followers. The twelve friends. There's always one who's tired. Who wanted something different. Who could be—" Kage waggled a hand "—*persuaded*. You find that one."

He was aware, distantly, that the emperor's eyes had gone from bright to *burning*.

He kept going, because stopping now felt like a worse idea than continuing. "Or. Or the cleanest one. The one I'd do." He put a single sheet of paper in the middle of the table, all by itself, and laid his palm flat on it. "You don't touch him at all."

"Explain," said Artorias.

"There was a governor," Kage said. "In the carpenter's story. A Roman. The man who actually had the power to kill him or let him go. And he knew — he *knew* — that whatever he did, it would stick to him. So he brought the carpenter out in front of the crowd, the carpenter's own people, the ones who already hated him for their own reasons, and said, *you decide*. And then he called for a basin of water and washed his hands. In front of everyone. *Not my doing. Not my blood.*" Kage tapped the lone paper. "It didn't work for him, as it happens. Bad writers. But the idea's sound. If you want the Misfits broken without building them a hill, you don't break them. You let someone else do it. Someone with their own grudge, their own reasons, and no story attaching to you at all."

"Who," said the emperor.

It was barely a word. It was a breath with a shape.

"There's a rumour," said Kage, "that the human leader of the Aquatic Clan has the worst relationship with the Misfits of anyone in the underground. Something about a brawl in a flooded car park. And a video. Of the Aquatic leader falling into their own water arena in front of their own clan. Which the Misfit girl, Juno, may or may not have posted. And which may or may not have two million views." He let himself smile, very slightly, at the paper. "If the Misfits get crushed by the Aquatic Clan, it's not a martyrdom. It's a *grudge match*. Humans don't build churches over grudge matches. They build comment sections."

"Comment sections," Smough repeated, savouring it like a new flavour.

"And meanwhile," Kage said, "the Dragon Clan has clean hands. And the Dragonoid has no hill. And his sly little messiah story—" he spread his fingers on the paper "—has no ending. Just a lot of very wet children."

He stopped.

He stopped because he had looked up.

And all four of them were looking at him.

Not glancing. *Looking*. Ornstein, golden and narrow and rigid at the top of the dais, his amber eyes gone wide and fixed and strange, the ozone smell coming off him in sharp prickling waves, an expression on his face that hovered between outrage and something horribly close to *hunger*. Smough on the third step, his round pink chin propped on both his huge fists, gazing at Kage with the rapt, adoring attention of a child at a puppet show. Artorias, at his column, utterly motionless — but his head was up, and his blue eyes were very dark, and there was something in them that Kage had not seen there before and could not name. Respect, maybe. Or the thing that comes right before respect, in wolves, when they are deciding whether you are prey.

And the emperor.

The emperor was leaning over the edge of Kage's little black table with his chin propped on one fist and his hair spilling across the papers, his face so close that Kage could see the faint scaled texture at his temples where the avatar thinned, could see the gold flecks inside the gold of his eyes, could feel each slow breath on his own lips — and the emperor was smiling at him the way a dragon smiles at a mountain of treasure that it has only just realised is bottomless.

*Oh no,* thought Kage.

He looked back down at his papers. He looked at the photograph of Drago, humble in the back. He looked at the lone sheet under his palm. He thought about Rome and Spartacus and the girl in the square and the governor with his basin, and every single one of the ten thousand ways human beings had ever invented to break each other without lifting a sword, and every single one of them was in the libraries, and the libraries were *open*, and the emperor of all Vestroia could read.

*Oh, no.*

He had just introduced a wrathful, murderous, possessive, thousand-year-old dragon emperor — a being who conquered *worlds* for a *hobby* — to the concept of human psychological warfare.

He had given a nuclear weapon a library card.

*Damn,* Kage thought, with feeling. *Damn, damn, damn. Well done. Gold star. Excellent strategy. Truly. The Dragonoid read half a book and got a messiah. I read him three paragraphs and got—*

"More," said the emperor.

* * *

"Come here."

Kage was already moving before he'd decided to. That was how it was, with that voice. His knees unfolded from the silk cushion; his hands left the papers; he was on his feet, and the emperor was rising too — slowly, all of him, uncoiling up off the bottom step of the dais like smoke rising off a fire — and then the emperor was walking backwards up the steps, without looking, without needing to, and Kage was following, and the heat closed around him like a fist closing very gently around a bird.

The emperor sat down on the throne.

He did not sprawl. He sat back, deep, his shoulders against the red velvet and the gilded fan of flame rising behind his head like a halo made of knives, and he spread his knees, and he took Kage by the wrist and drew him in, up the last step, onto the top of the dais, between them.

*Between his knees,* Kage's brain reported, in a thin, high, disbelieving voice. *I am standing between the emperor's knees. On the dais. In the throne room. During a council. In front of Ornstein.*

The black robe was open. The emperor's chest was a wall of heat a foot from his face — dark bronze and burnished, the muscle under it moving slow and dense with each breath, the gold dragons embroidered on the robe's edges glinting as they rose and fell. The smell of him was everywhere. Smoke and spice and hot metal. Kage's face was burning. His ears were burning. He could feel his own pulse in his lips.

A hand came up and slid into his hair.

The claws went in first — not hard, just the points, light as the tines of a comb, through the blue at his temple and back along his skull, down through the darker violet at his nape — and then the whole palm followed, broad and hot, fever-hot, cupping the back of his head. It tilted his head back. Gently. Inexorably. Until he was looking up, and there was nothing to look at except the emperor's face looking down.

"You see the shape of things," Nillious murmured.

His thumb moved along the line of Kage's jaw. Slowly. Like a man testing the edge of a blade he is pleased with.

"I have conquered eleven worlds. I have never once needed to understand them. I took them, and they were mine, and their understanding was their own business." The thumb reached the corner of Kage's mouth and stopped there. "But this one is strange. It moves in ways I do not see coming. And you—" his eyes went dark gold, thick, molten "—*you* see them. You were sitting at your little table sorting your little papers, while I paced and Ornstein spat and Smough ate, and you *saw*." The thumb pressed, very slightly, against the edge of Kage's lower lip. "Mine sees what I don't."

*Mine.*

He didn't say it like a word. He said it like a door locking.

Kage's knees did something structurally unsound. He locked them. He locked them so hard his thighs ached.

The emperor bent his head.

He bent it down and to the side, slow, the curtain of black-and-gold hair falling around both of them and closing them in, and the heat of him came down with it, and his face went past Kage's face, past his cheek — breath scorching along his cheekbone — and into the curve of his neck. Under his ear. On the left. Where the collar of his shirt sat over the mark.

He breathed in.

Long. Deep. The way he had breathed in the scent of the throne room on that first day back, the way he breathed in Kage's hair in the nest at night — a slow, enormous, greedy inhale that seemed to go on and on, that seemed to pull at the skin itself, at the warmth under it, at the pulse jumping there. Kage felt the edge of a tooth. Just the edge. Just resting against the side of his throat above the collar, sharp and smooth and very still, not pressing, not breaking anything, just *there*, a small hard fact on his skin.

The mark under the collar went off like a struck bell.

Heat poured out of it. Down into his chest. Down his spine. Out along his arms to his fingertips, which curled, helplessly, into the black silk of the emperor's robe at his ribs, because they had to hold onto something, because the floor of the dais had gone soft and far away under his boots. His breath went out of him in a sound that he would deny making until the end of his life. It was a small sound. It was a very small sound.

The emperor heard it.

Of course the emperor heard it. The emperor could hear a coin drop in a vault on another continent. Kage felt the rumble start in that great chest a foot from his face — low and slow and deep, a sound like a mountain settling, like a purr made by something that ate purring things — and felt the mouth against his throat *curve*.

*I am a strategist,* Kage thought desperately. *I am a strategist. I run the budget. I designed the training regime. I negotiated with three MODs. I have a black card. I am* eighteen years old *and I am standing in a throne room with my face in a dragon's chest making* noises *and Ornstein is* RIGHT THERE—

From the left side of the dais, very quietly, almost under his breath, Ornstein said: "The pet talks a great deal today."

The tail moved.

Kage didn't see it. Nobody saw it. There was a sound like a whip — a single dry *crack* that split the air — and then Ornstein was on one knee on the step, his golden head snapped to the side, one hand pressed to his face. Between his fingers something dark gold welled up and ran. A long, clean cut, from the corner of his mouth to his ear, opening in a slow red-gold line along the sharp beautiful cheekbone.

The emperor had not lifted his face from Kage's neck.

"Say it again," he murmured, against Kage's skin. To Ornstein. His lips moved on the mark as he said it, and Kage felt every syllable, and the floor tilted. "Say it again, Ornstein. I want to be sure I heard."

Ornstein's tail had gone flat and still against the stone. The ozone smell had vanished as if blown out. "Forgive me, Radiance."

"Not to me."

A pause. Very long. Kage could hear the gold blood dripping, one slow drop at a time, onto the black stone of the step.

"Forgive me," said Ornstein, through his teeth, to the floor, "*strategist*."

*Oh, great,* Kage thought, faint and far away, with his fingers still clenched in the emperor's robe and the emperor's tooth still resting on his throat. *Wonderful. Now he's going to hate me until the heat death of the universe. He's going to stand at my funeral and smile. I've made an enemy of a lightning bolt with a haircut.*

On the third step, Smough giggled softly. He didn't stop for a while.

At his column, Artorias did not laugh. He didn't move at all. But when Kage — dizzy, flushed, mortified to the marrow, the side of his face pressed against a chest like a furnace — dared to slide his eyes sideways for half a second, Artorias was watching him. Not the emperor. *Him*. And the look in those deep blue eyes was not hunger and was not contempt and was not interest.

It was something very like pity.

Kage looked away from it fast. Much too fast. As though it had burned him.

The emperor's mouth left his throat. Slowly. The tooth last of all, drawing away with a light, unhurried scrape that left a line of heat on his skin. The head came up. The hair swung back. And the thumb that had been resting at the corner of his mouth moved, deliberately, and pressed against the centre of his lower lip, and held it there, and the gold eyes looked down at his mouth as though it were a thing that belonged to them and had done something unexpectedly clever.

"This mouth," said Nillious, "said something worth hearing today."

Kage couldn't speak. There was a thumb on his lip.

"Tonight," the emperor said, "it will say more. All of it. The carpenter. The slave on the road. The burning girl. The governor and his little basin." He smiled, slow and dark and enormously pleased. "I want to hear every way your soft, stupid, clever little species has ever found to break itself."

The thumb moved away. Kage drew a breath like a man surfacing.

"Your Radiance," he managed, and his voice came out rough and much too low, "they're — some of them are quite *long*—"

"Then," said the emperor of all Vestroia, "we will not sleep."

*No,* thought Kage, with total, cosmic despair. *No. No, no, no. Please. Not again. I'm begging you. I will tell you every story ever written. I will read you the entire encyclopaedia. Just let me lie down for one night in a horizontal position with my eyes closed and no dragon rearranging me by the ankle—*

"Ornstein," said the emperor, and his hand slid back into Kage's hair as he said it, idly, the way another man might rest his hand on the head of a favourite hound, "you'll go to the Aquatic Clan."

Ornstein's head came up. The cut on his face was already beginning to close at the edges, the gold blood drying dark. His amber eyes flicked once to Kage, and Kage felt the look land on him like a slap. Then they went back to the emperor. "Radiance."

"You'll take a letter. In my name." The claws combed slowly through the blue. "My strategist will write it."

*Oh, will I,* thought Kage. *Will I really. How lovely. A personal letter to the Aquatic Clan leader, who is a teenager I have met once and who keeps a pet stingray, from the emperor of the dragons, written by me, in his voice, delivered by a man who now wants me dead. What could possibly—*

"You'll be polite," the emperor went on. "You'll be generous. You'll offer whatever my strategist tells you to offer. And you will not, under any circumstances, *touch* anyone." A pause. "We are washing our hands."

He said it with relish. He said it the way you'd say a word in a new language that you'd just learned and liked the taste of. *We are washing our hands*.

Kage closed his eyes for a second.

*A library card,* he thought. *I gave him a library card.*

"Smough."

"Radiance?" Smough sat up, beaming.

"You'll stay hungry."

Smough's face fell like a soufflé.

"Artorias." The emperor's eyes went to the column. "When my strategist has finished his letter, you'll walk him to my chambers. He's tired." The hand in Kage's hair tightened, very slightly, at the root, and tilted his head back again so that the emperor could look down into his face, and the look was not kind, and was not cruel, and was something worse than both: it was *fond*. "He's been trying not to yawn at me all morning."

Kage's blood turned to ice water and then, immediately, to steam.

"I—" he said. "I didn't—"

"I know," said Nillious, and the smile went wider, and showed far too many teeth. "You didn't. You were very brave about it." His thumb brushed once along Kage's cheekbone, where the skin was burning. "I was watching you."

*Of course you were,* thought Kage, very faintly. *Of course you were. You're always watching me. That's the whole problem. That's been the whole problem for a year, and I didn't notice, because I was busy being* clever—

He thought about that for a moment. He thought it with great clarity.

And then he thought about the letter to the Aquatic Clan instead, and what kind of opening line would sound generous without sounding weak, and whether *esteemed* was too much, and the thought about watching sank away under the letter like a stone sinking into warm, dark, honey-thick water, without a ripple, without a sound, and was gone.

The emperor let go of his hair.

"Go and write," he said. "And then go and sleep. For a little while." The smile again. Lazy. Dark. Royal. "You'll need it."


The letter took him an hour and a half.

It would have taken him twenty minutes on a normal day, but on a normal day Ornstein would not have been standing three feet behind his cushion with his arms folded and a freshly closed cut across his face, radiating the smell of a lightning strike and a pure, crystalline, personal hatred that Kage could feel on the back of his neck like a sunburn.

*Esteemed Leader of the Aquatic Clan,* he wrote, and crossed it out, because *esteemed* was too much.

*To the Leader of the Aquatic Clan,* he wrote, and crossed it out, because that was too little, and the emperor did not write *too little*.

*His Radiance Nillious, Emperor of the Dragons and of all Vestroia, greets the Leader of the Aquatic Clan, and has heard with interest of certain recent events involving a flooded car park—*

He crossed that out very hard.

Behind him, Ornstein breathed.

*He's not going to kill me,* Kage told himself, dipping his pen. *He's not allowed. The emperor would turn him into a lampshade. A lampshade that smells of ozone. That's a comforting thought. Hold on to it.*

He finished the letter. It was very good. It was generous without being weak and warm without being soft, and it mentioned, in passing, in a single elegant subordinate clause, that the Dragon Clan had *no particular interest in the affairs of a certain small clan of mixed type, and would view any action taken against it as a purely private matter between neighbours*. Which was, translated out of diplomat, *please go and drown them, we promise not to watch*.

He sanded it, folded it, and sealed it with the emperor's seal — a coiled dragon in black wax, which he kept in the second drawer of his table and was the only person in the palace permitted to touch. Then he turned on his cushion and held it out.

Ornstein looked at the letter. Then at Kage. Then at the letter.

He took it the way you'd take a dead rat from a cat — between two long golden fingers, at the very corner, as far from his body as his arm would reach. And as he took it, he leaned down. Just slightly. Just enough that his face came within a foot of Kage's, the half-healed cut gleaming dark gold along his cheekbone, and the amber eyes held Kage's eyes, and the ozone smell rose off him so strong that the fine hairs on Kage's arms stood up.

"Clever little pet," said Ornstein, very softly. Barely above a breath.

Then he straightened, and turned, and walked out of the throne room with the letter held out in front of him like a torch, and his golden tail lashed once against the doorframe on the way out, and left a scorch mark.

Kage sat very still on his cushion and looked at the scorch mark.

*He said it quietly,* he thought. *He said it very, very quietly. Under the emperor's hearing. Which means he thinks there's such a thing as under the emperor's hearing. Which means he's an idiot. Which is* great *news, honestly, because idiots are predictable, and I would love for something in my life to be predictable—*

"He will not touch you," said a voice behind him. "He knows better."

Kage did not jump. He would be proud of that later. He did, however, feel his heart leap up into his throat and try to climb out through his mouth.

Artorias was standing beside the table.

He had not walked there. Kage had been sitting with his back to the column; he would have heard footsteps on the stone. There had been no footsteps. There had been the column, and the brazier, and a shadow beside the brazier that had looked like a shadow — and now there was a tall, grave man in dark clothes with blue-black hair falling past his shoulders and one arm bound in black cloth hanging motionless at his side, looking down at Kage with those deep, quiet, blue eyes.

"The emperor said I'm to walk you," said Artorias. "Are you finished?"

"I'm finished," said Kage. He stood up. His knees were stiff. "Thank you, Sir Artorias."

Artorias inclined his head a fraction of an inch, and turned, and walked, and Kage followed.

* * *

The way to the emperor's wing was long, and Artorias did not fill it with talk.

That was fine. Kage didn't want talk. He wanted a horizontal surface and eight hours and possibly a medically induced coma. He walked half a step behind the tall, silent figure through the dim gold corridors, past the lamps of pierced brass throwing their lace of shadow on the walls, past the carved columns with their coiling dragons, past arch after arch opening onto skies that were not Iceland's — a red desert under two moons, a black sea full of slow green lights, a mountain range so high that its peaks were above the clouds and the clouds were full of lightning — and the heat of the palace lay over everything like a blanket that had just come out of a dryer, and the fog in his head began, very slowly, to come back.

He yawned.

He did it without thinking, a huge, jaw-cracking, eye-watering yawn, the yawn of a man who has been holding a yawn behind his teeth for four hours and has finally been released into the wild. He was halfway through it before he remembered. He snapped his mouth shut so fast his teeth clicked.

Artorias did not turn around.

"I am not the emperor," he said. "You may show me your teeth."

Kage felt his ears go hot.

"I wasn't—"

"I know." The voice was low and rough and very even. "You have small teeth. It would not be much of a challenge."

*Was that a joke?* Kage stared at the back of the blue-black head. *Was that — did the quiet one just make a joke? About my teeth? Is that allowed? Is he allowed to do that? Should I laugh? If I laugh, will he take it as a challenge? Do I have to fight him now? With my small teeth?*

He did not laugh. He walked.

They went down a long gallery where the moonlight fell through carved lattice and lay across the floor in a net of black and silver, and Artorias's boots moved through the pattern without a sound, and his bound arm hung at his side, and Kage looked at it.

He didn't mean to. It was just there. It was at eye level, more or less, swinging very slightly with each step, wrapped from shoulder to fingertips in strips of black cloth so tight and so many that it looked less like an arm than like something that had been *packaged*. Something that had been put away. And the black cloth was — Kage squinted — the black cloth was *moving*. Very slightly. Not with the swing of the walk. On its own. A slow, faint, oily shift under the surface, like something breathing under a sheet.

"You want to ask about the arm," said Artorias.

"I would *never*—"

"Everyone wants to ask. The emperor asked, when I came back." He did not slow. "He was the only one who was answered."

They walked through another slice of moonlight.

"I went somewhere I was told not to go," said Artorias. "For someone I should not have followed. A long time ago. In Vestroia, there were places where the dark was not an absence of light. It was a *thing*. It was alive, and it was hungry, and it took what you brought into it." The bound arm swung. "I brought the arm in. The dark kept it. As a fee."

Kage didn't know what to say to that. He said nothing. Sometimes that was the cleverest thing.

"I tell you," said Artorias, "so that you will understand when I say this." He stopped.

He stopped so suddenly that Kage nearly walked into his back, and caught himself, and stood there in a rectangle of moonlight with his heart thudding, and Artorias turned around.

He was very tall. In the moonlight his face was all planes and shadows, long and grave and weathered in a way that avatars usually weren't, as though he had chosen to wear his age instead of hiding it. The deep blue eyes looked down at Kage, and they were not cold. That was the thing. They were not cold at all.

"You should not have told him," said Artorias.

Kage opened his mouth.

"He asked," he said. "In front of everyone. I couldn't—"

"I know. You could not have refused. That is not what I mean." The blue eyes searched his face. "You should not have told him *well*."

The moonlight lay between them like a sheet of cold water.

"He keeps the things that please him," said Artorias quietly. "You have seen how close. The chambers. The nest. The tail at your ankles in the throne room, in front of the Dragonoid, in front of all of us. That is close." A pause. "There is a closer than that, strategist."

Something under Kage's collar went warm.

"I don't—" he began.

"The venom moves slowly in those he wants slowly," said Artorias. "And quickly in those he wants quickly. Today you made him want you *quickly*." The bound arm hung motionless. Under the black cloth, something shifted. "I have walked into the dark once, for someone I should not have followed. I know what it looks like, from the inside, when you have already gone too far to see the way back. I am telling you what it looks like from the outside."

Kage stood in the moonlight and looked up at him.

There was a thought. He could feel it. It was very large and very cold and it was rising up through him from somewhere deep, somewhere underneath everything, the way the black shape of a whale rises under a small boat — slow and enormous and inevitable, and it was going to break the surface, it was going to break it *right now*, and when it did he was going to understand something about the warmth under his collar, and the nest, and the year, and the way his hands had not shaken this morning when Garrow's horn came off, and—

—and the moonlight on the floor was really very beautiful. Like lace. He'd thought that before, hadn't he? Lace. And Artorias was being very kind, in his strange grave way, which was nice, which was unexpected, and Kage should probably say something polite back, something about the arm, about how he was sorry about the arm. That would be the decent thing.

The whale sank back down into the black water without a ripple.

"I'm sorry," said Kage, "about your arm."

Artorias looked at him for a long, long moment.

Then he closed his eyes, briefly, the way a man closes his eyes when he has been told a piece of news he expected and hoped not to hear.

"Yes," he said. "So am I."

He turned around and walked on, and Kage followed him through the lace of moonlight to the emperor's wing, and did not think about it again.

* * *

The chambers were empty.

It was so rare that Kage stood in the doorway for a full minute after Artorias had bowed and closed the great black doors behind him, just looking. Listening. The braziers had been banked low, to a slow deep orange, and the lamps were dimmed, and the whole vast room lay around him in warm, close, honey-coloured dark. Dark wood, worked with gold. Dark red silk on the walls. Carved screens throwing their patterns of shadow. The heavy, resinous, smoky smell that lived in this wing and nowhere else — cedar and myrrh and hot stone and *him* — lay over everything like a second, invisible layer of silk, and Kage breathed it in without meaning to, and felt his shoulders drop two inches.

And at the far end, under the great carved canopy, the nest.

It was a sea. A dark, heaped, tumbled sea of cushions and pillows and blankets and furs — crimson, black, gold, the deep wine-red of old blood, the bronze of autumn leaves — piled so high and so deep that Kage had once lost a shoe in it for three days. Half the pillows were scored with long pale gashes where claws had raked through them in sleep. Feathers lay drifted in the hollows like snow in a ditch. Last night's casualties had been cleared away by someone Kage had never once seen, and replaced, and the new ones were already plumped and waiting, smooth and whole and innocent, with no idea what was coming to them.

*Rest in peace,* Kage told them silently. *You were very brave.*

He went to the bathing room first.

It wasn't a bathroom. It was a cavern. A pool of black stone sunk into the floor, wide enough to swim in, fed by a mineral spring that came up from somewhere deep in the island's bones and filled the whole room with steam and the faint egg-and-flint smell of the volcano. The emperor bathed in it like a crocodile, submerged to the eyes for hours at a time. Kage had used it exactly once and come out the colour of a boiled prawn. Ever since, he'd used the shower — the single, small, perfectly ordinary human shower that he had requested in his third week, and that had appeared overnight in the corner of the cavern, tiled in black marble, with gold taps shaped like dragon heads, which spat the water out of their mouths. He tried not to think about the taps.

He stood under the water until his skin stopped feeling like Iceland and started feeling like a person. He washed the throne room out of his hair. The smoke, the resin, the iron smell of the scout's horn. The heat of a hand. He washed it all, and he stood there afterward with his forehead against the cool black marble and his eyes closed and the water drumming on his shoulders, and he thought, with great and simple longing: *sweatpants*.

His sweatpants. The grey ones. The soft, baggy, shameful grey ones with the hole in the left knee and the drawstring he had chewed in a moment of stress during his first budget meeting. And the T-shirt. The faded black T-shirt, three sizes too big, with a cartoon cat on the front sitting in a cardboard box and the words *IF I FITS, I SITS*. He'd bought it for four hundred yen in a second-hand shop the year before the Dragon Clan existed. It was the softest piece of fabric in the known universe. It had been washed so many times the cat was barely a ghost. It was, in the strictest sense, the only thing in this entire palace that was *his*.

He was going to put them on. And then he was going to crawl into the nest and lie face down and *sleep*, for the little while that he'd been granted, alone, with nobody rearranging him by the ankle.

He dried off. He wrapped the towel round his waist. He padded, barefoot and dripping, across the warm stone floor of the chambers to the dressing alcove where the carved chest stood that held his things.

He opened it.

He looked inside.

He stood there for a while.

Then he closed the chest, very gently, and opened it again, in case it had been a trick of the light.

It had not been a trick of the light.

There was nothing of his in the chest. No sweatpants. No cat. No hoodie, no socks with the hole in the heel, no shorts, no faded band T-shirts, no flannel pyjama bottoms with the moose print that he had sworn he would never let a living soul see. All gone. Every single soft, comfortable, scruffy, human thing he had ever worn to bed, vanished, as if it had never existed.

And in their place —

Silk.

Folded, layered, heaped, gleaming. Black silk, so dark it swallowed the lamplight. Red silk, the deep heart-red of the walls, heavy as water. Gold silk, thin as breath, embroidered all over with tiny gold scales that caught the light and threw it back in a thousand small fires. Midnight-blue silk worked with silver. Brocade and damask and something so fine it was almost transparent, which Kage closed the chest on *very quickly* and then had to open again because his hand was still in it.

Robes. All of them. A dozen at least. Long, loose, wide-sleeved, sashed, cut in the style that the emperor wore — that *all* the dragons wore, in the palace, when they wore anything — and embroidered, every one of them, at the hem and the cuffs and the collar, with coiling dragons in gold thread.

Kage lifted the top one out. It unrolled from his hands in a long slither of black, so heavy and so fluid it felt like holding a waterfall, and on the back of it, across the shoulders, worked in gold so fine and so dense that it seemed to stand up off the fabric, was a single enormous dragon, wings spread, coiling round and round itself — black and gold, horned like a broken crown.

He held it up against himself. It was exactly his length. The sleeves would fall exactly to his wrists. The shoulders were exactly his shoulders.

*Someone measured me,* he thought, and a cold little chill went down the back of his still-damp neck. *Someone measured me exactly. I never stood still for a tailor. I never stood still for anybody. When did they—*

He thought of the nest at four in the morning. A hand moving along his arm in the dark, slow and absorbed, from shoulder to wrist. A hand at his waist. Along his back. He had thought, half-asleep, that it was just — that the emperor was just —

*Measuring.*

"Oh, you *absolute*—" Kage said aloud, to the chest, to the robes, to the empty room, and then stopped, because there was no word, in any human language, that he could safely finish that sentence with in this wing of this building.

He knew exactly who had done this. He didn't even have to wonder. There was only one creature in this palace who would look at a pair of grey sweatpants with a hole in the knee and a T-shirt with a cat on it, and see not *comfortable clothes* but a personal, aesthetic, imperial insult, an offence against beauty itself, a stain on the walls of his chambers, and simply *remove* them from the world. Without asking. Without telling. The way you'd remove a dead fly from a windowsill.

*The cat,* thought Kage. *He took the cat.*

He stood there in his towel for a long moment, holding the black robe, with the steam from the bathing room curling around his ankles and the lamplight moving on the silk, and he felt something rise up in his chest that was very hot and very bright and very close to genuine fury.

And then he felt it go out. Softly. Like a candle in a draught.

Because what was he going to do? Go out and buy more? He could. He had the card. He could walk out of here through the fire-station door and into Reykjavík and into some shop and buy a hundred pairs of sweatpants and a thousand cat T-shirts, and then he could — he could take them somewhere, somewhere that wasn't—

He was so tired.

He was so tired, and the robe was so soft, and it was warm from the lamps, and the chest was full of robes, and the nest was right there.

He put it on.

It slid over his shoulders like warm oil. Cool, for half a second, and then not cool at all: the silk took the heat of his skin and held it, and gave it back, and settled around him in long heavy folds that moved when he moved and a half-second after he moved, like water following a stone. He tied the sash. He tied it tight. It came loose anyway, immediately, the silk too slippery to hold a knot, and the robe slid open a hand's width at the chest and then, as he turned, slid down off one shoulder entirely, exposing it to the warm air of the chambers.

He pulled it back up. It slid back down.

He pulled it back up and held it there with one hand and stood very still and said, through his teeth, to nobody: "*Of course*."

He went to the nest. He held the robe closed. He waded in.

The pillows took him like a soft dark sea, and the furs closed over his legs, and the smell of the emperor rose up from every thread of every blanket — so thick and warm and familiar that it went into him like a drug, and his whole body went heavy all at once, every muscle letting go. He lay face down. He put his face in a pillow that was, for the moment, unshredded. The black silk of the robe settled over his back and his legs like a second, very expensive skin.

*Just a little while,* he thought. *Just a little.*

He was asleep before he finished the thought.

* * *

He woke because the room had changed.

It was nothing he could have pointed to. No sound. No light. Just the air: it had been warm, and now it was *hot*, a close, heavy, enveloping heat that pressed down on the nest from above like the lid of an oven, and the smell that had been lying quietly in the blankets had stood up, and filled the room, and was *everywhere*.

Kage lay very still with his face in the pillow and his eyes closed.

He knew without looking. He always knew.

The emperor was standing at the edge of the nest.

He hadn't made a sound coming in. He never did, when he didn't want to. But Kage could feel him there the way you can feel the sun on the back of your neck — a pressure of heat, a weight of attention — and he could hear, if he held his breath, the very faint, very slow, very deep sound of breathing. In. Out. In. Like a bellows at rest.

And something else. A low sound. A rumble, almost below hearing, rising slowly out of that enormous chest.

Kage turned his head on the pillow and opened one eye.

The emperor stood over him with the lamplight behind him, so that he was a vast black shape edged in gold, the horns rising into the dark like the branches of a burned tree. His robe hung open. His hair poured down over his shoulders and his chest. His tail lay along the floor at the edge of the nest in a long, slow, contented curve, and the tip of it was lifting, and curling, and settling again, and lifting.

He was looking at Kage. At the robe.

His eyes, in the dimness, were glowing faintly. Not reflecting — *glowing*, two low banked coals of gold, and the pupils in them were wide and black and soft, wider than Kage had ever seen them, black almost to the edges of the gold. He was looking at the black silk lying over Kage's back and legs, at the gold dragon coiling across Kage's shoulders, at the place where the robe had slipped, again, in sleep, and fallen half off one shoulder, baring it to the lamplight. He was looking at it the way the sea looks at the shore.

"*Better*," said the emperor.

It came out of him low and rough and thick, the voice not quite in its usual register, as if it had come up through a great deal of smoke on its way out.

Kage turned over.

He did it with as much dignity as a man could manage in a nest of pillows wearing a robe that would not stay on, which was to say almost none. He sat up. He held the robe closed at his throat with one fist. He looked up at the emperor of all Vestroia, standing over him in the dark with those soft black eyes, and he said, with great and terrible calm:

"Where are my clothes?"

"Burned," said Nillious.

"...All of them?"

"They offended me."

"The—" Kage's voice cracked. He cleared his throat. "The grey ones? With the—"

"With the hole." The emperor's lip curled. "Yes."

"And the — the T-shirt. The black one. With the—"

"*Especially* the cat," said the emperor of all Vestroia.

Kage looked at him for a long, long moment.

Then he lay down again, very slowly, and put his face back into the pillow, and screamed into it. Silently. With his whole soul.

Above him, from that vast, dark, smoke-edged shape at the edge of the nest, there came a sound. Low. Rough. Rolling. It took Kage a second to recognise it, because he had heard it so rarely.

The emperor was laughing.


* * *

The tea was the one thing in the chambers that was his.

Not the kettle, technically. The kettle was brass, and old, and heavy, and had a spout shaped like a dragon's neck with a little open mouth at the end, so that every time it boiled it screamed through its teeth. It had come from somewhere in the depths of the palace on the day Kage asked for *a kettle, please, just a normal kettle, anything*, and it was beautiful and absurd and entirely un-normal, and he loved it. Not the brazier it sat on, either, a squat iron thing on three clawed feet in the corner by the carved screen, which the emperor could have lit with a breath and which Kage lit, stubbornly, with a box of matches from a petrol station in Akureyri.

But the tea was his. A battered tin of strong black tea from a supermarket in the human world, bought with the black card in a moment of spite, the cheapest thing on the shelf. And the cup was his: one plain white porcelain mug, chipped at the rim, that he had smuggled into the palace in the bottom of a duffel bag eleven months ago and that had somehow, so far, survived every purge.

*Don't jinx it,* he told himself, measuring tea into the pot. *Don't even think about it. If he hears you thinking about the mug, the mug is gone. The mug is in a better place. The mug is with the cat.*

The robe kept sliding off his shoulder. He kept pulling it back up. He'd given up on the sash entirely; he'd retied it four times and four times it had slithered loose like a living thing, so now he held the robe closed with his left hand and did everything else with his right, which made making tea into a kind of one-handed juggling act that he refused, on principle, to let anyone see him struggle with.

Someone was seeing him struggle with it.

"Go on," said the emperor.

He was lying on the long low divan by the brazier, the one heaped with black and gold cushions, on his side, propped up on one elbow, his head resting on his fist. He'd shrugged off his robe at some point. It lay in a dark pool on the floor behind him, the gold dragons on its hem glinting where the lamplight touched them, and he was bare to the waist, all dark bronze and lamplit muscle and long black-and-gold hair spilled across the cushions in a river. His tail hung off the end of the divan and lay across the floor and curled, idly, round one leg of the little tea table, so that whenever Kage reached for anything the table *moved* very slightly, nudged, as though it had a heartbeat.

His eyes had not left Kage since the laughing stopped.

*Don't look at him,* Kage told himself. *Do not look at him. It's like looking at the sun. It's like looking at the sun if the sun was half naked and smirking. You will go blind and you will deserve it.*

"So the emperor," he said, to the teapot, "made his horse a senator."

The emperor's brows went up. "A horse."

"A horse. Incitatus. His favourite. He gave it a house made of marble and a manger made of ivory and a collar of jewels and eighteen servants, and he invited it to dinner, and he said he was going to make it a consul. That's — very high. One of the highest offices in the empire."

He poured water on the leaves. Steam rose. The kettle screamed one last little scream through its brass teeth and fell silent.

"Was it a good senator?" asked the emperor.

"I—" Kage paused. "Nobody's ever asked me that."

"A sensible man," said Nillious, with satisfaction. "He looked around his senate and he chose the one creature in the room he could trust."

"Most historians think he was mocking the senate. Showing them they were worth less than a horse."

The emperor's mouth curved slowly. "Then he was *more* sensible still." His tail tapped the leg of the tea table, once. "What happened to him?"

"His own guards killed him."

"Ah." The emperor's smile didn't change. "Then not *quite* sensible enough." The gold eyes moved, lazily, toward the great black doors of the chambers, toward the corridor beyond where Artorias stood guard. And back. "Another."

Kage told him about Troy.

He told it while the tea steeped, standing at the little table with one hand holding his robe shut and the other wrapped round the warm white mug, and he told it well, because he was tired and the lamplight was soft and stories were easier than thinking. The siege that went on for ten years. The heroes and the quarrels and the ships on the beach. And then the morning the Greeks were gone, their camp burned, their ships vanished from the horizon, and on the empty beach in front of the walls stood an enormous wooden horse.

"And the Trojans," said Kage, "dragged it into the city."

The emperor stared at him.

"They dragged it," he repeated slowly, "through their own gate."

"They had to take down part of the wall to fit it."

"They *broke their own wall*. For a wooden animal."

"It was very large. And very beautiful. And they thought it was an offering to their god."

The emperor was silent for a long moment. The tail had stopped moving. Then he said, with a kind of awe, "*Humans deserve everything that happens to them.*"

"There was a priest," Kage said, "who told them not to. He said — *I fear the Greeks, even bearing gifts.* He threw a spear at the horse."

"Good. And?"

"And two giant serpents came up out of the sea and ate him. And his sons."

Something rumbled on the divan. Kage glanced up despite himself. The emperor had laughed again — soft, low, delighted — and his eyes had gone half-lidded with pleasure. "Serpents," he said, as if tasting it. "From the sea. For being *right*." He sank a little further into the cushions. "That is a good story. I like that story."

*Of course you do,* thought Kage. *Of course the part you like is the giant snakes eating the only sensible man in the city. Why did I think you would like the moral. There is no moral. You are the snakes.*

He lifted the lid of the pot and looked at the tea. Almost.

He told him about the khan. A boy born on a cold grassland with nothing, whose father was poisoned and whose family was abandoned, who was captured as a slave and escaped, who gathered the tribes one by one until he had the largest land empire the human world had ever seen.

The emperor sat up a little.

"He'd send a messenger before every city," Kage said. "*Open your gates and submit, and you'll live. Close them, and you won't.* The ones who opened, lived. Traded. Paid tribute. Grew rich, some of them, under him. The ones who closed—" he shrugged, one-shouldered, the robe slipping "—there's a city where they say he had the skulls stacked into pyramids. One pyramid for the men. One for the women. One for the children."

"Yes," said the emperor softly. "That is how it is done."

"He also built the best postal system in the world," Kage went on. "Horses, stations every few miles, so a message could cross the whole empire in days. And he let everyone keep their own gods, as long as they paid their taxes. And they said a girl carrying a gold plate on her head could walk from one end of his empire to the other and nobody would touch her, because everyone was too afraid of him to steal."

The emperor was looking at him with an expression that Kage did not like at all. It was interested. It was *very* interested. It was the face of someone reading a recipe.

"I would have liked," said Nillious, slowly, "to meet him."

"He would have liked you too, Your Radiance," said Kage, without thinking. And then, because his mouth had gone off on its own again: "That's what worries me."

He went back to the tea very quickly.

There was a silence behind him. Not a hostile one. Something heavier, warmer, more patient. He heard the cushions shift. He heard the faint slither of the tail drawing back across the floor, unwinding from the leg of the table, which stopped its small heartbeat movements and went still. He heard nothing else at all.

He poured the tea.

It went into the white mug dark and strong and steaming, the colour of the river under the stars on the terrace, and the smell of it came up into his face, malty and bitter and plain and *human*, the cheapest tea on the shelf, the best smell in this entire palace. He put the pot down. He wrapped both hands round the mug — let go of the robe, which immediately slid open and off his left shoulder; fine; *fine*; he was too tired to care — and he lifted it and breathed in the steam and closed his eyes.

The heat came up behind him.

He felt it before anything else, the way you feel the sun come out from behind a cloud. A wall of heat at his back, close, closer, so close that the silk of the robe went warm all at once against his spine as if it had been ironed on him. Then the smell: smoke and spice and hot metal, deep and dark, filling his lungs over the smell of the tea. Then — very slowly, very carefully, the way you would lower something heavy and precious onto a shelf you did not entirely trust — the weight.

The emperor's chest came to rest against his back.

It was like leaning against a wall that had been standing in the sun all afternoon. Solid and broad and *hot*, so hot that the heat soaked through the silk and into his skin and down into the muscles of his back, which went soft without asking his permission. A long arm came round him. Not grabbing. Just resting, slung loosely across his stomach over the black silk, a hand spread flat and broad and heavy below his ribs. And the tail came round the other way, low, sliding across the floor and up and around his waist, over the slipping silk, once, twice — and settled there, warm and heavy and absolutely certain, holding the robe closed at his middle as no sash in the world had managed to do.

*Oh,* thought Kage, very faintly. *Oh. That's — that's actually helpful. That's the first helpful thing anyone's done all day. I hate that it's the tail.*

The emperor bent his head.

He bent it slowly, the way he'd done in the throne room, the black-and-gold hair falling forward over Kage's bare left shoulder and down his arm like a curtain of warm water. His jaw came to rest against the side of Kage's head. Then lower. His chin settled into the curve between Kage's neck and shoulder, on the bare skin where the robe had slid away, heavy and warm, and stayed there. Kage felt the faint roughness at the jawline where the avatar thinned into scale. He felt the breath, slow and deep and scorching, moving against his collarbone. In. Out. Each exhale stirring the fine hairs at the nape of his neck.

The emperor turned his face, just slightly, into his neck. Just below the ear. Just above the mark.

And breathed.

The rumble started in that vast chest pressed against Kage's back. He felt it before he heard it — felt it in his own spine, in his own ribs, a deep, slow, continuous vibration, the sound a mountain might make if a mountain could be content. It went through him like warm water through a sponge. It went into his bones. It went into the mark under his ear and the mark went soft and warm and heavy, pulsing slow, in time with the rumble, in time with the breath, in time with something that was not his heart.

*Peace,* thought Kage.

He stood very still with the white mug in his hands and the steam curling up into his face and the emperor of all Vestroia folded round his back like a living cloak of fire, and he thought, with enormous, astonished, bone-deep relief: *peace*.

The stories had worked. That was the thing. He'd given the emperor what he'd demanded. The horse, the wooden horse, the khan. He'd been interesting. He'd been *useful*. And the emperor was pleased — Kage could feel it, feel the pleasure radiating out through that chest and that arm and that tail like the heat off a banked fire, slow and deep and steady. Not the bright, terrifying, coin-on-the-hoard delight of the throne room. Something quieter. Something sleepier. Something *settled*.

He was going to sleep tonight.

He could see it. He could see it as clearly as he could see the tea in his mug. In a few minutes he'd drink this, and put the mug down, and the emperor would make that low pleased sound and scoop him up — he always scooped, never asked, carried him like a parcel he had personally signed for — and they'd go to the nest, and the emperor would lie down on his side with the great heavy arm across Kage's chest like a fallen column and the tail round his ankles, and the rumble would go on and on in the dark, and Kage would lie there inside it like a pearl inside an oyster, and he would sleep. Properly. Eight hours. Maybe nine. Nobody prowling. Nobody rearranging him by the ankle. Nobody growling at the door. The emperor had been angry last night; tonight the emperor was content; content dragons *slept*.

He took a sip of tea. It was perfect. Bitter and strong and very hot.

He closed his eyes.

*Thank you,* he thought, to nobody, to the universe, to the brass kettle with the screaming mouth. *Thank you. I will be good. I will be so good. I will tell him about the Romans tomorrow. I will tell him about the Romans for a week.*

The emperor's lips moved against his neck.

"Why," said Nillious, softly, conversationally, his breath warm on the mark, "aren't you wearing the ring I gave you?"

* * *

The tea went cold in Kage's mouth.

Not literally. Literally it stayed exactly as hot as it had been. But something happened inside him — a long, smooth, cold slide, like a drawer of ice being pulled open in his stomach — and he felt the sweat come out on him all at once. At his hairline. Down his spine, under the silk, under the hot weight of the emperor's chest. In the palms of his hands, against the white porcelain of the mug.

He swallowed. It went down like a stone.

*The ring.*

The ring.

He remembered it perfectly. He remembered it the way you remember a car accident. Twenty-nine days ago — no, thirty — a night not unlike this one, in this room, in the nest, the emperor lying on his back with Kage drawn up against his side, idly turning one of his own rings round and round on one long dark finger. He wore several. Heavy, ancient, ugly, magnificent things that sat on his claws like trophies. This one was the biggest. Thick yellow gold, so dense and so deep in colour that it looked almost orange in the lamplight, worked round its whole circumference in a band of tiny jagged points, like a miniature crown — and set in the top a single stone, black and flawless and slightly *wrong*, a stone that did not reflect light so much as hold it, the way a well holds water.

*This was a crown,* the emperor had said, turning it. *The king of Ysmarre wore it. A world of glass. Glass towers, glass roads, glass ships on glass seas. Very pretty. Very brittle.* The ring had turned and turned. *He knelt to me with it on his head. I took the head off and kept the crown. Melted it with a breath, in my hand. It's warmer, now. Glass is always so cold.*

And then he had taken it off his own finger and held it out.

*Yours.*

And Kage had said — *thank you*. Very politely. *Thank you, Your Radiance, it's beautiful.* And taken it, and held it, and felt the weight of it in his palm, which was approximately the weight of a small stapler, and looked at it, and looked at the band of tiny jagged crown-points that had once sat on the head of a man who was now not a man and not even a skull, and slid it onto his finger, where it hung off him like a hula hoop off a pencil. It would only fit his thumb. Barely.

He had worn it for exactly one day. On his thumb. Clacking against everything. Knocking against the edge of his table every time he picked up a pen.

And then he had taken it off, and put it in a little black lacquered box, and put the box in the second drawer of his table, next to the emperor's seal, and told himself that it was for safekeeping. That a thing like that should be kept safe. That it was too valuable to wear every day. That he would put it back on soon.

Twenty-nine days.

He had not put it back on.

*Say something,* screamed his brain. *Say something. Say anything. Say* anything *—*

"I—" said Kage.

The emperor waited. His chin rested on Kage's shoulder. His breath moved slow and warm on the mark. The arm across his stomach did not tighten. The tail round his waist did not tighten. Nothing tightened. That was the worst of it. The emperor was not angry. The emperor was *waiting*, perfectly patient, perfectly comfortable, the way a cat waits outside a mousehole in a house with no other exits.

"I—" said Kage again. His mouth was very dry. He was holding the white mug so hard his knuckles hurt. "I like — white gold."

Silence.

*Oh, that's good,* said some distant, horrified, spectating part of him. *That's interesting. That's a fact about you. Keep going. Keep piling up facts. Maybe if you pile up enough facts, he'll forget the question.*

"And diamonds," said Kage.

Silence.

"And — pearls."

Silence.

It went on.

And in the silence, very slowly, very terribly, Kage *heard* what he had just said.

He looked down at the arm across his stomach. Dark bronze. The long dark claws. And on those claws, gleaming in the lamplight: rings. Gold rings. *Yellow* gold rings, heavy and deep and glowing, set with black stones. He looked at the tail around his waist — black scales, every one edged in a line of gold. He looked at the hair spilling over his shoulder — black, streaked with gold. He looked up, past the edge of the carved screen, at the walls of the chambers: dark red silk, worked with gold. The columns: black wood, inlaid with gold. The lamps: gold. The kettle: brass, which was practically gold. The robe *he was wearing*: black silk, with a dragon worked across the back in gold thread so dense it stood up off the fabric. The dragon was *black and gold*. It had *horns like a broken crown*.

Everything in this room was black and gold.

Everything in this *palace* was black and gold.

The emperor's *hoard* was gold. Yellow gold. Rivers of it. Mountains of it. Every coin and every crown and every ring the emperor of all Vestroia had ever taken from every king he had ever beheaded was yellow gold, and he had melted them down and built a palace out of them and slept on them, and they were the *colour of his eyes*.

And Kage had just stood in the middle of that palace, in a robe embroidered with the emperor's own image, and told him that what he really liked was *white gold. Diamonds. Pearls*. He had just told a dragon — the dragon, the emperor, the thing with a hoard the size of a city — that he didn't like the colour of his hoard.

That he didn't like the colour of *him*.

*Oh,* thought Kage. *Oh, I'm going to die.*

*I'm going to die in a robe. I'm going to die in a robe I didn't even choose. He's going to burn me where I stand and Smough will eat the ashes and the only thing left will be this mug, and then they'll burn the mug too, because it's white, and I've just told him I like white—*

The cold sweat had gone from his palms to his whole body. He was standing in an oven, wrapped in a dragon, and he was *freezing*.

Against his neck, the emperor's breath stopped.

One second. Two.

And then something shook against Kage's back.

It started very small. A tremor. A faint, rhythmic shudder in that vast hot chest, like a mountain having a quiet dream. Then it got bigger. Then it came up out of the emperor's throat, into Kage's neck, hot and low and rough against the mark — and Kage realised, with a lurch of total disbelief, that the emperor of all Vestroia was *laughing*.

Not the landslide laugh of the throne room. Not the rough surprised laugh from earlier at the nest. This was something else. Something low and dark and helpless and deep, pressed into Kage's shoulder, shaking them both, a laugh that went on and on and made the tail around Kage's waist twitch and tighten and loosen and tighten again, like a cat's tail twitching while the cat is having the best dream of its life.

"*White gold*," said the emperor, into Kage's neck.

"Your Radiance, I—"

"*Diamonds*."

"I didn't *mean*—"

"*Pearls*," said Nillious, and the laugh broke out of him again, and he turned his face into Kage's throat and Kage felt teeth — not biting, just there, just the edges, pressed against the skin as the emperor laughed against it. "Oh, my clever thing. Oh, my *clever little liar*."

*Liar.* Kage's whole body went rigid.

"You put it in a box," said the emperor.

The laugh was still in his voice, but under it now there was something else — something smooth and dark and very, very amused.

"A black box. In the second drawer of your little table. Under my seal. Next to Garrow's *rabbits*." The teeth moved, very slightly, against Kage's throat. "Twenty-nine days ago. You wore it for one day, on your thumb, and it knocked against your table forty-one times, and you made a face every time, and then you put it in a box and closed the drawer and told yourself you were *keeping it safe*."

Kage closed his eyes.

*He knew,* he thought. *He knew. Of course he knew. He's known the whole time. He counted the* knocks. *He's been waiting twenty-nine days to ask, just to see what I'd say, and what I said was* pearls—

"I wanted to see," murmured the emperor, as though Kage had spoken aloud, "what you'd say." His lips curved against the mark. "I did not expect it to be so *bad*."

"It was very bad," Kage agreed hoarsely.

"It was the worst lie I've ever heard. And I have heard kings beg." The arm across his stomach drew him back, slowly, until there was no space at all between his spine and that furnace of a chest. "White gold. In *my* chambers. In *my* robe. With *my* mark on your throat." Another low ripple of laughter. "Do you know what Ornstein would have done, if he'd heard you?"

"Died," said Kage. "Of offence."

"Died of offence," the emperor agreed, deeply pleased. "Smough would have eaten him. It would have been a very good evening."

Kage's heart was still trying to climb out of his chest. But under the terror — very slowly, very strangely — something else was coming. Something warm. Something that came up out of the place under the emperor's mouth and spread down into his chest and soaked out through his arms like hot water through cold cloth. His shoulders, which had locked up somewhere around his ears, began very slowly to come down.

He was not going to die.

The emperor was not angry. The emperor was *amused*. Which was, in its own way, much more frightening — because Kage had seen what made the emperor angry and he could plan for it, he could *strategise* around it. But what made the emperor laugh was a much smaller and stranger and more dangerous list, and Kage had just found his name on it.

"Your Radiance," he said carefully, "I really do like your gold."

"I know."

"I like it very much."

"I know. You look at it when you think I'm asleep."

*Oh, God.*

"And I'll wear the ring. Tomorrow. I'll — I'll find a chain, I'll wear it round my neck, I'll—"

"No," said the emperor.

He lifted his head.

Kage felt the weight leave his shoulder, felt the hair slide back off his arm in a long warm rush. The arm across his stomach moved — not away, just *up*, the broad hot hand sliding up the black silk over his ribs to his chest, and then down his arm, slow, the claws trailing lightly along the silk, to his wrist. It closed round Kage's wrist. It drew his left hand away from the white mug — gently, the way you'd lift a child's hand off a hot stove — and the mug, released, slid an inch on the table and stopped.

The emperor held Kage's hand up in front of them both, in the lamplight.

And in the emperor's other hand — Kage didn't see where it came from; it simply *appeared*, the way the Shadows appeared, the way everything in this palace appeared when the emperor wanted it to — was the ring.

The yellow gold. The tiny jagged crown-points. The black stone that held light like a well.

*He went to the drawer,* Kage thought, dazed. *He went to my table, and opened my drawer, and took out my little box, and took the ring out, and brought it here, before he even asked. He knew exactly how this conversation was going to go. He knew before I did.*

*Of course he did. That's the problem. That's always been the problem—*

"There is a world," said the emperor softly, turning the ring in the lamplight, "four doors over from this one. Its seas are warm and very shallow, and full of shellfish, and the shellfish make pearls. Pearls the size of your fist. The kings of that world wear crowns of white gold, and have diamonds sewn into the skin of their foreheads, so that they glitter when they speak."

Kage went very still.

"I passed it," said Nillious, "on my way to this one. I left it alone." The ring turned. "It was boring."

A pause.

"It is not boring anymore."

"*No*—" Kage twisted round in the circle of the arm, as far as the tail would let him, which was not far, and looked up into the emperor's face, and the emperor was smiling down at him, slow and dark and *delighted*, the gold eyes glowing, the pupils wide and soft and black. "No. Your Radiance. No, no, no. I was *lying*. You said it yourself, I was lying, it was the worst lie you've ever heard, I don't like white gold, I *hate* white gold, I hate pearls, I think diamonds are vulgar, I'm a *yellow gold person*, I'm the yellowest gold person you've ever *met*—"

"I know you were lying," said the emperor. "That's why it's funny."

"Then *why*—"

"Because you said it." The smile widened. Too many teeth. "And now I have thought of it. And I cannot unthink a thing, little strategist, any more than you can. You've taught me that today." The ring went on turning. "Perhaps I'll go. Perhaps I won't. Perhaps one day I'll be bored again, and I'll remember that my strategist once said *pearls* to me in my own chambers while wearing my robe, and I'll go and see how many fit in a crown."

*I just doomed a civilisation,* Kage thought. *I just doomed an entire civilisation. Over jewellery. Somewhere four doors over there's a king with diamonds in his forehead sitting on a white gold throne eating breakfast and he has no idea that eighteen-year-old Kage just made a stupid excuse about a ring and now he's on a list. He's on the list. I put him on the list.*

He wanted to throw up. He wanted to laugh. He wanted to lie down in the nest and pull a pillow over his head and wait for the heat death of the universe. He did none of these things, because the emperor had taken his left hand and was spreading the fingers, one by one, very gently, with one long dark claw.

The claw came to rest on his fourth finger.

Kage's heart stopped.

*No,* he thought. *Not that one. Anything but that one. The thumb. The index. The little finger, I'll wear it on the little finger like a mafia boss. Not that finger. He doesn't know what that finger means. He can't know. He's a dragon. Dragons don't have — that's a human thing, that's a* very *human thing, that's the most human thing there—*

"Your Radiance," he said, and his voice came out thin, "maybe the — maybe a different finger—"

"Why?" said the emperor.

"It's just — that one's — in human culture, that one—"

"Your Romans," said Nillious, "believed that a vein ran from this finger straight to the heart." The claw moved along the length of it. Slowly. From the knuckle to the tip. "*Vena amoris*. The vein of love. They thought a ring on this finger would hold the heart in place. So it could not wander."

Kage stared at him.

"I read it," said the emperor, "this afternoon."

*The library card,* thought Kage, with a sort of dazed, distant, perfect horror. *The library card. The library card. I gave him the library card at eleven this morning and by four o'clock he'd read about the vena amoris.*

*What else has he read. What else has he read. Oh, God. He's going to find the Kama Sutra. He's going to find Machiavelli. He's going to find* Sun Tzu. *He's going to find—*

"Hold still," said Nillious.

He took the ring between his thumb and forefinger, and *breathed* on it.

It wasn't a large breath. It was barely a sigh. But the air around the ring went to ripples, and the yellow gold went — slowly, beautifully, terribly — from yellow to orange to a deep dull glowing red, the colour of a coal at the bottom of a fire. The tiny crown-points softened. They began, very faintly, to *droop*. The black stone did not change at all; it sat in the middle of all that glowing metal like an eye in a burning face.

The emperor slid it onto Kage's fourth finger.

It went over the knuckle, loose, burning — *burning*, Kage felt the heat of it hit his skin like a brand and his whole body jerked against the chest behind him and a sound came out of him, sharp and high — and then the emperor's hand closed over his. Completely. The great dark fingers folding over Kage's fingers, the palm pressing down over the ring, holding it, holding *him*. And under that hot hand Kage felt the metal *move*. Felt it shrink. Felt it tighten round the base of his finger like a living thing, like a small gold snake drawing its coils closed, closer, closer, until it fit. Until it fit exactly.

The pain was white. For one second, it was white.

And then the mark on his neck *answered*.

Heat came pouring out of it — not burning heat, a different kind, thick and slow and heavy as honey — and went down through his chest and along his arm and into his hand, and met the burn under the emperor's palm, and *swallowed* it. Just like that. The pain went out like a candle. There was only warmth. Warmth in his finger, warmth in his hand, warmth spreading up his arm and into his chest and down, down, pooling low in his stomach, under the tail, under the emperor's other hand, a deep, liquid, drowsy, dizzy warmth that made his knees go soft and his head fall back against the emperor's shoulder and his breath come out of him slow and shaking.

The emperor opened his hand.

The ring sat on Kage's fourth finger, gleaming. It was no longer a hoop. It was *his* size — not a millimetre too large, not a millimetre too small, fitted to the base of his finger as closely as if it had been cast there. The tiny crown-points had settled into a smooth, low, glinting band. The black stone looked up at him like an eye. And the skin beneath it was not burned. Not red. Not blistered. Perfect. Just very, very warm.

He tried, very slightly, to turn it.

It didn't turn.

He tried to move it toward the knuckle.

It did not move toward the knuckle.

"Now it fits," said the emperor, against his ear. Satisfied. Low. Rough with smoke. "Now it will not knock against your little table. And now—" his lips brushed the mark, once, light as a moth "—you will not lose it in a drawer."

Kage looked at his hand.

He looked at it for a long time.

There was a thought. He could feel it, somewhere, very far down. Something about *off*. About a ring that did not come off. About the knuckle, and the gold, and the vein that ran to the heart, and a heart that could not wander. About Rome. About crowns. About a glass king and his glass world and his glass head, and how cold it must have been, and how warm the gold was now. The thought was there. It was moving. It was trying very hard to come up.

It was so warm.

The emperor's hand was spread across his stomach again, heavy and hot, and the tail was round his waist, and the chest at his back was rumbling, and the mark under the emperor's mouth was pulsing slow and sweet and steady, and the warmth that had swallowed the burn was still there, still pooled low and heavy in him, and his knees were soft and his head was on the emperor's shoulder and the thought was —

— the tea. He'd left the tea. The white mug was sitting on the table an inch from where he'd put it, still steaming faintly, going cold. That was a crime. That was the cheapest tea on the shelf and it was perfect and he was letting it go cold.

He reached for it. His left hand. The ring glinted.

The emperor's hand came over his before he got there, and lifted it away from the mug, and brought it up — slowly — to the emperor's own mouth. And the emperor kissed the ring. Just the ring. A slow, dry, deliberate pressure of lips on gold, on the black stone, on the skin of Kage's finger just beneath it, where it was very warm.

Kage's whole face caught fire.

"*Your Radiance*—"

"The tea will keep," said the emperor.

He picked Kage up.

He did it the way he always did it — no warning, no asking, one arm under Kage's knees and the other at his back, the black silk sliding and pooling and spilling over the emperor's forearms, and Kage was off the floor and up against that chest and being carried across the warm dark room toward the nest as if he weighed nothing at all, as if he were a parcel the emperor had personally signed for, which, Kage thought, staring at the ring on his hand, he now apparently *had*.

The nest took them both.

The cushions rose around them like a dark warm sea, and the furs, and the scent of smoke and cedar, and the emperor lay down on his side with the great heavy arm across Kage's chest — the column, the fallen column — and the tail came round and wound itself around his ankles, and then, after a moment of what felt like consideration, slid higher, and wound itself round his calf, and settled there, warm and certain, like a cat that has decided where it lives. The emperor's face went into his hair. The breath went in, long and slow and greedy. The rumble started.

*Sleep,* thought Kage. He could feel it coming, pulling at him, heavy and liquid and dark. The ring was warm on his hand. The mark was warm on his throat. The robe had slid off his shoulder again and he didn't care. He didn't care about anything. *Sleep. Finally. Eight hours. Nine. I'll think about the pearl world tomorrow. I'll think about the vena amoris tomorrow. I'll think about Ornstein's face, and Smough's teeth, and Artorias in the moonlight saying* there is a closer than that, *and the Aquatic Clan and Drago and the sly messiah and Rome—*

"Now," murmured the emperor, into his hair. "The carpenter."

Kage opened his eyes.

"...Your Radiance?"

"You said it was long." The arm across his chest drew him closer. The tail tightened round his calf. The voice was low and drowsy and absolutely, immovably royal. "So start at the beginning. I want all of it. The carpenter, and his twelve, and the one who was tired, and the governor and his basin." A slow, warm breath against the mark. "And then the slave on the road. And then the burning girl."

*No,* thought Kage, staring up at the carved canopy in the dark. *No. No, no, no. Please. I'll do anything. I'll wear the ring. I'll wear it forever. I'll wear it to my* funeral. *Just let me—*

"Kage," said the emperor.

The name went into him like a hook. He felt it catch.

The mark pulsed.

And Kage lay in the dark of the emperor's nest, with a dead king's crown welded to the vein that ran to his heart and a dragon's arm across his chest and a dragon's tail round his leg and a dragon's mouth resting, warm and patient, against the place on his neck that he never thought about — and he took a breath, and let it out, and heard his own voice, hoarse and drowsy and resigned, begin:

"There was an empire," he said. "A long time ago. The biggest one we'd ever had—"

And the emperor's teeth settled, very lightly, on the mark, and stayed there, and the rumble deepened under Kage's back like the sea coming in, and the lamps in the chambers dimmed one by one as though someone had told them the story was starting, and Kage went on talking into the dark, about roads, and legions, and a dusty little province at the very edge of the world, where nobody important ever lived, and where nothing that mattered could possibly—
