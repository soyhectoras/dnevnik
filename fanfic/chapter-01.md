# Chapter One: The Emperor's Pillow

The portal spat them out into a cold so absolute it felt personal.

Kage landed on wet grass, kept his balance out of sheer spite, and arranged his face. Serene. Kind. The smile of a priest who has heard every sin in the village and forgiven all of them except yours. Behind it, his molars were trying to chatter their way out of his skull.

*Three weeks,* he thought. *Three weeks chasing a boy with the attention span of a firework, and the reward is Iceland. In October. Brrr.*

Behind him the portal hung upright in the air for one long, indecent second, a blade of blue light taller than a door, humming the way power lines hum before a storm. Then it folded in on itself. There was no sound to it, exactly; there was a pressure, a crackle that went through the fillings of his teeth, a smell of struck flint and ozone, and then the air where it had been was only air again, grey and wet and moving. The grass beneath it was scorched in a perfect oval. Steam lifted off the burn like breath off a corpse.

Dan Kouzo came through a half-step after him and did not keep his balance at all.

"Whoa—whoa—*whoa*—"

He went down on one knee, caught himself with a palm in the mud, and came straight back up the way a spring comes back up, already turning in a circle, already talking. "Okay. Okay! That was *so* much smoother than the last one. Where are we? Is this—" He stopped. He breathed in. His face did something complicated. "Oh, man. Oh, it's *cold.* Why is it this cold? Kage. Kage, why is it this cold?"

"Because," Kage said, gently, as though explaining death to a child, "we are in Iceland."

"*Iceland?*"

"Iceland."

"Like—the country Iceland?"

*No, Dan. The other one. The theme park.* "The country Iceland."

Dan wrapped both arms around himself and hopped from foot to foot, his red-and-white jacket pulled up to his ears, which did nothing at all, because the wind here did not respect jackets. The wind here came straight off the sea with nothing in its way for a thousand kilometres, and it had been waiting a long time to meet someone from a warmer country. It went into Kage's sleeves. It went down the back of his collar. It found every one of the piercings that lined the cartilage of his ears and turned each small silver stud into a needle of ice pushed through the flesh; he could feel them individually, like a constellation of tiny, specific grudges. The silver spikes on the shoulders of his leather jacket sang faintly as the gusts passed over them. His hair—the long blue fall of it that lived permanently over his left eye—whipped across his face and stuck there, wet with the fine salt mist that wasn't quite rain and wasn't quite not.

He did not lift a hand to fix it. Fixing it would look like discomfort. Discomfort would look like weakness. Weakness, in front of a recruit, was a crack in the stained glass, and once a congregation saw one crack it started looking for more.

So Kage stood there in the wind, hair in his eye, ears full of frozen metal, and smiled his priest's smile, and felt the cold work its way into the muscles of his cheeks until he became distantly, clinically certain that if the wind changed direction his face would simply split along the smile, like a plate.

*Strategy,* he told himself. *Strategy is strategy. You can thaw later. You can thaw in a bath the size of a swimming pool. You can thaw for a week.*

The third of them came through last.

Drago did not stumble. Drago, in his human avatar, stepped out of the dying edge of the portal as if out of a doorway in his own home, and the light went out behind him, and for a moment he simply stood on the burned grass and was very tall. Lean, long-boned, built like something designed for speed and only reluctantly for standing still. His hair was red—not the polite auburn of shampoo advertisements but the red of an ember you blow on—streaked through with white, and from it two pale horns swept back along the line of his skull, smooth as polished bone. The plates along his forearms, where a human would have had only skin and the fine down of hair, caught the grey light in overlapping crimson scales. His eyes were crimson shading into gold. Behind him, low to the ground, his tail moved.

It was a long tail, red-scaled, heavy at the root and tapering to a fine tip, and it did not move the way a cat's tail moves, idly, with a kind of commentary. It moved the way a snake moves before deciding something. It swept once to the left across the wet grass, leaving a smooth dark track in the dew, and then it went still and straight and rigid behind him like a drawn line.

Drago lifted his face to the wind.

Kage had seen it a hundred times and it still did something to the back of his neck. The nostrils flared—not delicately, not the way a person sniffs at milk to check if it's turned, but wide, the whole face participating, the upper lip lifting very slightly off the teeth. He held the breath. He tasted it. His head turned, a few degrees at a time, towards the hills, towards the sea, towards the building, the way a compass needle settles, and with each degree the pupils of those crimson eyes narrowed until they were not round at all but thin dark slits, the eyes of something that hunts in bright places.

"Dragons," Drago said.

Dan stopped hopping. "What?"

"Many dragons." Drago's voice was low, and something underneath it was lower still, a vibration felt more in the sternum than heard. "Here. Close." He took another long breath through the nose and held it. "Too many."

"Well," said Kage pleasantly, "that is sort of the point."

The crimson eyes came round to him.

And stopped.

It was a small thing. Anyone else would have missed it. Kage did not miss things; it was his entire personality, or had been, until recently, when it had turned out that he had missed one or two things of some size. But he saw this: he saw Drago's gaze land on him, on his face, and then drift down, slow, to the high collar of his jacket where it closed around the left side of his throat. He saw the dragon's nostrils flare again. He saw the tail—that long, rigid, deciding tail—go suddenly and completely motionless, as though someone had nailed the tip of it to the earth.

Drago did not say anything. His face did not change. That was the strange part. A human face would have changed: a flicker of surprise, of disgust, of pity, something. Drago's face simply became very still, still the way the surface of a lake is still in the moment before something underneath it moves.

Under the leather of his collar, the mark on Kage's neck was warm.

It was always a little warm. It was an old mark, healed smooth; he had it from—he had had it for a while. It sat on the left side, low, where the neck slopes into the shoulder, and it was warm the way a stone is warm after a day in the sun, a slow, held, mineral warmth, and as they stood there on the scorched grass it grew a little warmer, and a little warmer again, and gave one soft, deep pulse that was nothing like his heartbeat.

Kage noticed the warmth the way you notice that a room has a carpet. Yes. It was there. It was fine. His attention touched it and slid off it like water off oiled glass, and went, as it always did, somewhere more useful.

"Come on," he said, turning his back on the dragon's stare with the unhurried grace of someone who has never once in his life been afraid of being bitten. "You'll freeze, and then Dan will complain about freezing, and then I'll have to listen to it, and none of us want that."

"*Hey,*" said Dan.

"I'm being inclusive. I'm saying *none of us.*"

Behind him, after a beat too long, he heard Drago's footsteps follow.

* * *

The fire station stood at the edge of the burned field like something the world had put down and forgotten to pick up again.

Red brick, two storeys, the brick gone dark and soft-looking with decades of salt and rain, stained in long black tears beneath every window ledge. The windows were the old industrial kind, grids of small panes in iron frames, and half the panes were broken; the broken ones were black, and the whole ones were black too, because there was no light behind any of them. Two enormous garage doors took up most of the front wall, each one a grid of glass on top and blind brown panelling below, built in the days when engines were tall and red and came out roaring. Nothing had come out of them roaring for a very long time. Four short yellow bollards stood in front of them in a row, paint peeling, like a small and very disappointed guard of honour. On the left side of the building a rusted iron ladder climbed the brick to the roofline and stopped there, going nowhere, the way ladders on abandoned buildings always seem to go nowhere. A round steel bell hung over the right-hand door. It had no clapper.

Behind the station the land rose in black hills—not the soft black of earth but the jagged, glassy, blistered black of old lava, furred here and there with a pale green moss so thick and bright it looked fake, like stage dressing. Beyond them, very far off, the hills turned to mountains and the mountains went up into a cloud the colour of dishwater and simply stopped being visible, as if they had been erased there. To the right, past a low line of dunes, the sea was a sheet of hammered pewter, and the light that came off it was the flat, sourceless, directionless light of a place where the sun is a rumour.

There was no town. There was no road, really—two old ruts in the grass, filled with standing water that reflected nothing. There was the wind, and the station, and the gulls somewhere out over the water screaming at each other about something that was clearly very important to gulls.

Dan slowed to a walk. "This is it?"

"This is it."

"This is—your clan's base." He looked at the bollards. He looked at the broken windows. He looked at the ladder that went nowhere, and at Kage, and back at the ladder. "It's, uh. It's really… rustic?"

*Ah,* thought Kage. *He's trying to be polite. Look at him go. Someone raised him.* Out loud, in the warm, patient tone of a man who knows something you don't and is going to enjoy the moment you find it out, he said: "Appearances can be deceiving."

"That's what people say when the appearance is bad."

"That's what people say," Kage agreed, "right before the appearance gets *so* much worse. Not this time, though." He spread his hand at the brick, the moss, the dark and staring panes, like a realtor presenting a ballroom. "Remote. Volcanic. No neighbours. Nobody asks questions when a fire station has no fires. And the authorities are looking for us in the cities, because they assume that anyone who wants to brawl wants an audience." He smiled. "We have an audience. We just keep it inside."

He walked. The ruts sucked at his boots. The cold had gotten into the soles now and was climbing his shins with real ambition.

How far was the nearest town, actually? He'd looked it up once. Forty kilometres? Fifty? There was a bus, he thought, or there had been, twice a day along the coast road, and if someone outside—if anyone, his mother, his old landlord, anybody—knew where he was, then they would have to come out along that road, and if he himself were ever to walk out along it, the first thing he'd need would be—

Tea.

The first thing he needed was tea. God, he hoped someone had put the water on. Black tea, very strong, with that cardamom thing they did in the kitchens, in one of those glasses with the gold rims that were so thin he was always slightly afraid of biting through them. He could practically feel it. He could feel the steam on his face. He was going to sit down on a cushion and hold that glass in both hands until his fingers came back to life one by one, like lights coming on in a house.

His mood improved enormously. He did not notice that it had needed to.

Dan, meanwhile, had found his second wind and was jogging along beside him, breath smoking. "So how many of you are there? In the clan? Are there a lot of brawlers? Is everybody here a Dragon? Wait—did you build this? Did you *find* it? Can you even just *take* a fire station? Is that legal? Actually, don't answer that, it's probably not legal, nothing's legal anymore, that's the whole—"

"Dan."

"Yeah?"

"Breathe through your nose. You're losing heat."

Dan shut his mouth, offended, and breathed through his nose, and then said through his nose, "*Is* everybody here a Dragon?"

*Patience,* Kage thought. *Patience is a strategic resource. You are spending it wisely. You are investing it in a golden retriever who learned how to fight.* "You'll see."

Drago said nothing at all. Drago walked half a step behind them on Dan's far side, a position that put his own body between Dan and the building, and Kage did not miss that, either. The dragon's tail had come unpinned from the earth and moved again now, but low, very low, the tip of it barely clearing the grass, and it lashed—not wide, not dramatic, just a short, hard, twitching flick, left and right and left, metronomic, the tail of a predator walking into a place that smells of other predators. Every few steps his head lifted and he tasted the wind again, and every time he did, the low thing in his chest that was not quite a growl and not quite a sound at all came again, so deep that Kage felt it more in the wet ground than in the air.

Fair enough. If Kage had a nose like that, he would have been growling too.

Because even he could feel it now.

It started somewhere around the bollards. It was nothing he could have pointed to. The wind did not change; the light did not change; the gulls kept screaming. But the air between him and the garage doors had gotten heavier, the way the air gets heavy before thunder, when you can feel the weather in your teeth and in the roots of your hair and in some old, animal pocket at the base of your skull. The air had a texture now. It was thick. It was close and dark and charged, and it pressed, faintly, on the skin of his face and the backs of his hands like the heat off an oven door that someone has opened in the next room.

It was a mood.

It was not *his* mood.

*Oh,* Kage thought, with the dull, resigned clarity of a man who has just heard the particular footstep of a particular teacher in the corridor outside a classroom where he has not done the homework. *Oh, no. He's sulking.*

He knew it the way you know your own hand in the dark. He had felt the emperor's moods before—felt them in rooms, felt them through walls, felt them, once, through three floors of stone and a closed door, from the far end of a corridor, so strongly that his hands had started shaking around a cup of tea and he had not understood why until the door at the end of the corridor opened. He was getting better at reading them. He was getting very good, actually. He thought, privately, that he could now distinguish between at least nine separate flavours of imperial displeasure, the way people who live by the sea can tell you nine kinds of grey.

This one was number six. This one was low and smouldering and endless. This one was coiled.

This one said: *you were gone too long.*

Which was outrageous, frankly. Which was a level of hypocrisy so grand and pure it was almost architectural. *He sent me.* He had sent Kage. He had lounged on that throne three weeks ago with his chin on his claws and his eyes half-lidded and gold as poured coin, and he had said, without any particular emphasis, *Bring me the Dragonoid and his boy,* the way another person might say *pass the salt.* And Kage had gone. Kage had done three weeks of hotels and train stations and drizzle and rumours and dead ends and one truly terrible night in a hostel in Rotterdam, for the clan, for the strategy, for the *emperor*, and now here he was coming home with the Dragonoid *and* his boy, gift-wrapped, on schedule—well, *roughly* on schedule—and the emperor was sulking at him. Through a wall. From—Kage estimated—roughly four hundred metres inside the building. Like a storm cloud with a grudge.

He would never, under any circumstances, say any of this out loud.

He'd sooner say it to the sea. He'd sooner walk into the sea and say it to the fish. And then stay there with the fish, because he had seen what happened to people who told the emperor of Vestroia that he was being unreasonable, or rather he had seen what was left of the furniture they had been standing near, and he had no ambition whatsoever to become furniture.

The mark on his neck throbbed again. Warm. Warmer. A slow, deep, deliberate pulse, like something knocking politely from the other side of a door.

He noticed that it was there. He thought about tea.

Beside him Drago had stopped walking.

They were ten metres from the doors. The dragon stood in the wet grass with his feet planted and his head lowered and his whole body gone hard and narrow, every line of it pointing at the station, and his tail was no longer lashing at all. It was held out stiff behind him, a little off the ground, the fine tip of it trembling with a vibration that went all the way up the spine. The scales on his forearms had lifted. Kage had never seen that before; he hadn't known they could. They stood up off the skin by a few millimetres each, all of them at once, the way the hackles of a dog stand up, the way the feathers of a bird flare before it strikes, and in the grey light every edge of every scale glittered.

His lips had come back off his teeth. His teeth were not a human's teeth.

"*Drago?*" Dan's voice went up. "Dude. What's wrong?"

"I know that," Drago said. He was not talking to Dan. He was talking to the building, or to whatever was in the building, and his voice had dropped into a register that Kage felt in the soles of his boots. "I know that *scent.*"

*Ah,* thought Kage.

*Well. That was always going to happen.*

"Kage." Drago did not take his eyes off the doors. "*Who* is in there?"

There were a number of things Kage could have said. He had prepared most of them on the train. He had a short version and a long version and a version for if Dan cried, which Dan had not, so far, and a version for if Drago lunged at him, which—looking at those raised scales—remained very much on the table.

What he said, with his priest's smile so fixed now and so cold that he could feel it as a separate object sitting on the front of his skull, was: "The founder of the clan. You'll meet him." And then, because it was true, and because the truth told sideways is the most useful tool a strategist owns: "He's been expecting you."

He stepped between the yellow bollards. He put his hand flat against the right-hand garage door.

The panel under his palm was cold, cold enough to sting, wet with sea-mist, the paint blistered into little bubbles that crackled when he pressed. Beneath that—a long way beneath it, deeper than any wall should go—something was warm. Not the door. Not the brick. Something on the other side of the brick, the way a body under a blanket is warm. He felt it come up through the wood and the paint and into the bones of his hand, a slow, heavy, total heat, and with it the mood, so thick now that it was nearly a sound, nearly a voice, nearly a word, and the word was *mine* and also *late* and also *finally*—

He did not interpret it. He had learned not to interpret things. He pushed.

The door did not slide up on its rails the way a garage door should. It swung inward, smooth and silent and heavy as a vault, on hinges that did not exist, into a dark that did not belong to the building, and out of that dark, all at once, came the heat.

It rolled over the three of them like a tide. Warm, close, golden-dark, thick with the smell of resin and smoke and old wood and something sweet and burnt beneath it, like honey left too long on a flame—it poured out over the threshold and over the bollards and the wet grass, and the wind off the Icelandic sea hit it and simply gave up, and the cold that had been eating Kage alive for the last twenty minutes let go of him all at once, every needle in every piercing melting at the same moment, so suddenly that he had to lock his knees.

Somewhere far inside, very far, deeper than any fire station had any right to be deep, a lantern swung on a chain. Gold light moved across dark red walls. And under all of it, so low it was barely there, so low it might have been the building itself breathing, something enormous exhaled.

Kage smiled into the dark like a man coming home, and stepped inside.


* * *

The warmth took them first.

It came over the threshold like the breath of something very large that had been asleep for a long time just on the other side of the brick, and it closed over Kage's face, slid down his collar, went into his frozen lungs and stayed there. He nearly made a sound. He did not make a sound. The priest's smile held, serene and benevolent and slightly glazed, and behind it every cell in his body that had spent the last hour in Icelandic wind lay down on the floor of his soul and wept with gratitude.

*Oh,* he thought. *Oh, there you are. Hello. I missed you. I'm never going outside again.*

The thought had a small shape at the very end of it, like a step missing from a staircase, and his mind went over it without looking down, and then he was inside and the door was swinging shut behind them, heavy and silent on hinges that did not belong to any fire station, and the wind was gone as if someone had cut its throat.

The dark inside was not the dark of an abandoned building. It was a chosen dark. A kept dark. The dark at the bottom of a jewel box, where the light has to be invited in and is never allowed to stay long.

The hall went on.

That was the first thing the eye refused, and the second, and the third. The fire station outside had been perhaps thirty metres from end to end, red brick and broken glass and two rusted garage doors. The hall inside did not care what the fire station had been. It stretched away under a ceiling lost in smoke and shadow, held up by columns of dark wood so thick that three people could not have linked hands around them, each one carved from root to crown with a single dragon coiling upward through stylised flame, scales picked out in gold leaf that caught the lamplight and let it go again, slowly, like a held breath. Lamps of pierced gold metal hung from chains at different heights, dozens of them, hundreds, and every one of them threw its pattern outward so that the walls, the floor, the faces of anyone walking beneath them were laced in moving shadow, star and petal and thorn, never still. The carpets were deep red and old gold and swallowed footsteps whole. Brass braziers stood along the walls, their coals banked low and breathing. The air was resin and smoke and something mineral underneath, like stone that had been in a fire for a very long time and remembered it.

Somewhere, far off, water was running. Somewhere further off, something very large shifted its weight, and the floor knew about it before Kage's ears did.

"*Whoa,*" said Dan.

The echo came back wrong. It came back late, from much too far away, as though the hall had taken the word somewhere to look at it before returning it.

"Kage." Dan had turned in a full circle and was beginning a second. His cheeks were still red from the cold. His eyes were enormous. "Kage, this is— how is this— the building is, like, *tiny!*"

"It's bigger on the inside," Kage said gently.

*Every time,* he thought. *Every single time, that face. I should charge admission. I should have a little booth at the door. Twelve hundred krónur to have your understanding of physics violated, children half price.*

Drago had stopped walking.

He had stopped the way an animal stops: all at once, all of him, the whole long body of his human avatar going rigid from the swept-back pale horns to the red-scaled tail, which had been lashing since the portal and now hung perfectly still, its tip barely touching the carpet. His nostrils flared. His crimson-gold eyes moved over the nearest column, the coiled dragon, the particular way its claws had been carved — three forward, one back, the back claw hooked over a ring of flame — and something went across his face that Kage had not seen on a Bakugan's face before and did not have a name for.

Drago put his hand flat against the wood.

"This is the Hall of the Rising," he said. His voice had gone very quiet. "The eastern approach. You walked it at dawn, and the light came in through—" He stopped. He looked up, into the smoke, where there was no dawn and no window, only lamps. "He rebuilt it."

"Drago?" Dan had come back to his side. "You know this place?"

"I was a hatchling in these halls." Drago did not take his hand away from the column. The plate-like scales along his forearm had lifted slightly, the way a cat's fur lifts. "Every pillar. Every lamp. He remembered every stone." The quiet in his voice changed register, went down into his chest and came out as something closer to a growl. "And he put it inside a *fire station.*"

"Space was at a premium," Kage said.

Drago turned his head and looked at him. It was not a human look. It went on a beat too long, and his head was tilted a few degrees further than any human neck would have found comfortable, and his pupils had narrowed to two red-gold seams.

"Who built this?" Drago said. "No human hands did this."

"No," Kage agreed pleasantly. "No human hands."

He had asked, once. Early on, when he'd still been asking things. He had stood in the empty bay of the fire station with frost on the inside of the windows and watched the emperor walk to the far wall and put one clawed hand flat against the brick, exactly the way Drago was touching the column now, and the next morning there had been a door in the brick, and behind the door there had been this. He'd asked how. The answer had been a look. He had not asked twice.

*Dragons,* he thought, watching the lamplight crawl over the gold leaf, *have the same relationship with luxury that fire has with oxygen.* Humans wanted comfort. A sofa, a radiator, a fridge with something in it. Dragons did not want comfort. Dragons wanted to be surrounded, at every moment and from every angle, by evidence of their own magnificence. Every surface in this place said *mine*. The gold was not decoration. The gold was a hoard that had been taught to stand up and hold the ceiling. They slept on it. They ate off it. They would, Kage was fairly sure, have paved the floors with it if gold had been better for the knees.

And he lived here.

A year ago his entire fortune had been a jacket, a deck, an attitude, and a very precise knowledge of which convenience stores threw out their onigiri at what time. Now he lived here. Some mornings he still woke up and lay very still and waited for the ceiling to resolve itself into something with a damp stain on it.

He thought about the spoons.

He thought about the spoons a lot, actually. There was a dinner service in the emperor's dining hall — forty-eight place settings, every piece heavy as a threat — and the spoons were gold. Not gilded. He'd checked, with a thumbnail, on the underside, when nobody was looking. Gold all the way through, and engraved with scales so fine they felt like skin. One spoon. One single spoon slipped into the inside pocket of his jacket, a quiet visit to someone in Reykjavík who did not ask questions, or no, Reykjavík was too small, online, then, through a third party, and he would be *rich.* Not rich-for-a-week rich. One spoon was a year's rent. One spoon was a year's rent somewhere with a radiator that actually worked and a door that—

Except that it wasn't a fantasy, was it, because he'd said it out loud once. At dinner. Half joking, turning the spoon over in the candlelight: *I wonder what one of these would go for.* And the emperor had looked at him down the length of the table, and then, without a word, had reached out one long clawed hand and pushed the entire tray of cutlery across the black wood toward him, forty-eight spoons ringing like a bell, and said: *Take them.*

So the spoons were not the problem. The spoons had never been the problem. Kage could have every spoon in the palace. He could have the forks too. The problem was the rent. Rent implied a flat, and a flat implied a door the emperor did not own, and that would be — there would be problems. He could picture the shape of those problems fairly clearly; they were tall, and they had a tail, and—

Anyway. He was hungry. When had he last actually eaten? There'd been a sandwich at a petrol station outside Akureyri that had tasted like cold wet cardboard and regret, and before that a bowl of something in the lab yesterday, and he should really find out whether the kitchen had made that lamb thing again, the one with the apricots, because he could eat that lamb thing every day for the rest of his life and die happy.

"Kage?"

"Mm." He smiled. "Sorry. Long trip. This way."

* * *

He gave the tour the way he gave everything: lightly, quickly, with the absolute confidence of someone who knows that the answer to any question is *later.*

"This is the public wing," he said, walking backward a few steps so he could watch their faces. "Training, sparring, research, lounge. Everything a growing brawler needs. Food, if you ask nicely. Hot water, which, coming from outside, I think you'll agree is the main attraction."

"And that?" Dan pointed.

At the far end of the hall, where the columns marched away into the dark and the lamps grew fewer and further apart, there was a gate. It was black. Not painted black: black the way the bottom of a well is black, metal that drank the lamplight and gave nothing back. It was twice the height of the tallest column and carved with something that Kage's eyes had long ago learned not to try to follow, because the carving did not quite stay where it was put. It was closed. It was always closed.

On either side of it stood a figure in human avatar, so motionless that for the first several months Kage had thought they were part of the carving. Then one day one of them had turned its head to watch him cross the hall, and he had realised that they were not statues, they had simply not needed to move.

One of them turned its head now. Very slowly. Its eyes caught the lamplight like a cat's at night: two flat discs of reflected gold.

"Clan territory," Kage said. "Members only."

"How much of this place is members only?" Drago asked.

"Oh," said Kage, "about ninety-nine percent."

Dan laughed, thinking it was a joke.

Drago did not laugh.

* * *

The sparring corner was a raised floor of polished black stone set beneath three scalloped arches, the ring marked out in a band of inlaid gold. Two of the clan's younger brawlers were working through drills inside it with practice staffs — a lanky boy with a shaved head and a girl with her hair in two tight braids, both maybe nineteen, both quick and good and sweating. The clack of the staffs went up into the arches and came back softened.

Along the wall behind the ring stood their Bakugan.

Kage did not look at them, particularly. He had stopped looking at them, particularly, some time ago. He was aware of them the way one is aware of furniture. A woman in human avatar with horns like split green-black obsidian and a long tail coiled at her feet; a broad, heavy man with a ridge of dull bronze scales running from his temple down into his collar. They stood with their backs to the wall and their arms at their sides and they watched.

Not the way coaches watch. Coaches watch technique. Coaches look away to check a clipboard, to say something to someone, to scratch their nose.

These did not look away. Their eyes followed every movement of their own brawler and only their own, and their pupils were thin as needles, and the woman's tail had begun, very slightly, to move — the tip lifting, curling, settling, lifting again, in time with the girl's footwork.

The boy feinted high and swept low, and the end of his staff caught the girl across the outside of her shoulder. Not hard. A practice hit, the kind that leaves a bruise for two days and is forgotten by the third.

The sound came from the wall.

It was not loud. Kage felt it before he heard it — felt it come up through the soles of his boots and into his shins, a low vibration, the bass note of a cathedral organ played by something with a much larger chest than a human woman's. Every lamp on that side of the hall trembled on its chain. The lace of shadow on the floor shivered.

The boy went white. He dropped his staff. He did not apologise to the girl. He turned to the wall and lowered his eyes and said something low and fast that Kage didn't catch, and stood there with his head bowed, and did not move until the bronze-scaled man behind him — his own Bakugan — stepped forward and put a hand on the back of his neck and drew him, not gently, to the side.

The girl walked to the wall.

The obsidian-horned woman took her arm and turned it over, palm up, then palm down, then pushed the sleeve back with one long nail and bent her head and looked at the reddening mark on the shoulder with the total, unblinking focus of something examining prey it has already decided not to share. She breathed in, once, at the bruise. Her lip had drawn back from her teeth, and the teeth were not human teeth. The girl stood still and let her. The girl's face showed nothing at all. When the inspection was over, the woman's hand settled at the base of the girl's skull and stayed there, a weight, a claim. Her slitted eyes went to the boy across the ring, and stayed there too.

"Uh," said Dan. "Overprotective much?"

"Bakugan take partnership very seriously," Kage said. "Here, especially."

*Normal,* his mind supplied, smooth as oil. *Normal. Tuesday.* It slid over the girl's shoulder and the woman's teeth and the boy's white face and found nothing in any of it to catch on.

Drago was not looking at the woman's teeth. Drago was looking at the girl's neck, at the place just above the collar of her training shirt where, half-hidden by a braid, there were two pale crescents of old healed skin, silvered, the shape of something closing.

Then Drago's eyes moved, and Kage felt them land on his own collar. On the left side. Where the jacket sat high.

The mark there was warm. It had been warm since the door. It was nothing — it was an old mark, he'd had it a while — and his attention went to it and slid off it, the way water slides off a waxed leaf, and he was already turning, already gesturing onward with an open palm, already smiling.

"Gym's through here."

* * *

The gym was a long vaulted chamber that looked as though someone had tried to describe a human gym to a dragon from memory, and the dragon had nodded politely and then built what it actually thought strength was for. There were weights, but they were rough-hewn blocks of basalt with bronze handles sunk into them. There were climbing ropes, but they hung from a ceiling so high the tops disappeared into smoke. One entire wall was rock — real rock, black and wet and cold, the bones of the island pushed up through the floor of the palace — and three of the clan's brawlers were on it, chalk-handed, hanging on by their fingertips forty feet up with nothing beneath them but stone.

Above, a gallery ran around the room behind a lattice of carved gold. In the gallery, figures stood and watched.

Kage counted them without meaning to. Seven. Seven Bakugan in human avatar for seven humans on the floor. Not one of them was talking. Not one of them was doing anything but watching.

"Is there a pool?" Dan asked.

"There's a hot spring. Lower level. Clan only." Kage smiled. "Join, and I'll show you."

"That's not fair."

"Life rarely is."

* * *

The lab was the only room in the public wing with cold light in it. After the gold-dark of the halls it was almost painful: blue-white monitors banked three high along a curved wall of dark wood, holographic projections hanging in the air over a central table like ghosts that had been asked to stand very still. Terrain maps rotated slowly — lava fields, ice sheets, a canyon, a drowned city — each one netted with fine glowing lines that converged and pulsed at certain points.

"Are those—" Dan was already moving toward them, his whole body tilted forward like a dog that has seen a ball. "Are those B.A.M. Zones? You *map* them?"

"We predict them," said a voice from behind the monitors. "Mapping is for amateurs."

A boy unfolded himself from a chair that had been turned to face the wall. Nineteen, maybe, all angles and elbows, a grey hoodie with the sleeves shoved up, headphones around his neck, and a face that had not seen daylight in some time and did not miss it. He nodded at Kage with the specific casualness of someone who has been in the room when Kage was very tired and very rude, and survived.

"Toma," said Kage. "Analytics. He's the reason we win more often than we have any right to."

"He's the reason I don't sleep," said Toma, and turned back to his screens, and the hood of his sweatshirt fell away from the side of his neck.

Two crescents. Silver. Healed.

Behind him, sitting cross-legged on the edge of the desk where no chair was, there was a young man with ash-grey hair and short black horns and a thin grey tail that hung down the side of the desk and curled loosely, possessively, around one leg of Toma's chair. He was reading the same monitor Toma was reading. When Dan leaned in close to the central table, his face lit blue, practically breathing on the projection, the young man on the desk did not look up from the screen. But his head turned. A few degrees. Toward Dan. And kept turning, slowly, smoothly, until it reached an angle where a human would have had to move his shoulders, and did not move his shoulders, and stayed there.

Dan didn't notice.

Kage noticed. Kage noticed that he had noticed, and filed it, neatly, alongside the spoons and the lamb with apricots, in the part of his mind where things went to be not-thought-about. Strategy. Toma's dragon was territorial about the lab. Toma's dragon was territorial about *Toma*. Useful to know if you were planning to put Dan in here for any length of time, which Kage was.

"Our prediction accuracy," he said, "is about eighty-one percent."

Dan made a sound like a kettle.

* * *

They took the long corridor back.

Kage told himself it was so they would pass the fresco. That was partly true.

The fresco ran the full length of the western gallery, perhaps two hundred metres of wall from floor to ceiling, and the lamps along that corridor had been hung lower than anywhere else in the palace so that you walked beside it rather than beneath it, close enough to touch. It was a city. It was a city at dusk, under a sky of bruised violet bleeding to rose at the horizon: a fortress city built into the face of a cliff, or carved out of it, tower upon tower upon tower climbing up the stone until they vanished into cloud, every tower crowned with a small dome, every one of its thousand windows lit gold from within. A great gate stood open at the foot of the walls, and through the gate the streets poured out like a river of fire — lanterns, market awnings, crowds of figures in robes, and through the crowds, in slow procession, enormous armoured beasts draped in red and gold, their backs as broad as houses, plodding toward something outside the frame.

The detail was unbearable. You could see individual lamps in individual windows. You could see the faces of the crowd. If you stood close enough, you could see that some of them were looking up, toward the highest tower, as if waiting for something to appear there.

Drago stopped in front of it. Of course he did.

"The capital," he said.

He said it the way you'd say the name of someone who has died.

"Who painted this?" Dan asked softly. He'd stopped bouncing. The fresco did that to people. It made you lower your voice the way a cathedral does, or a grave.

"He did," said Drago.

Kage hadn't known that.

He looked at the wall again. Really looked, for the first time in months. And now that he was looking he could see it: the places where the plaster had not been brushed but cut. Long sure gouges. Lines that went deep into the wall and came out again with no hesitation, four of them at a time, parallel, the width of a hand's span apart. The violet of the sky was not quite even, as though it had been laid on with a palm. In one corner the red of the processional banners was darker than the rest, and dull, and had dried in a way that paint does not usually dry.

*Huh,* Kage thought. *Well. Every artist has his medium.*

He turned away from the wall, and there was someone standing in the archway at the far end of the corridor.

Copper-red hair, cropped close. Gold eyes, molten, steady, catching the lamplight. A ridge of scales running up the side of the throat like a seam where something had been stitched shut.

Doesn't-Blink.

He was not looking at Dan. He was not looking at Drago. He was looking at Kage. He was always looking at Kage. Kage held the gaze for exactly one second, smiled his pleasant priest's smile, turned his head back toward the fresco to say something to Dan—

—and when he looked again, the archway was empty.

*Love that,* he thought. *Love it when they do that. Never gets old. I'm sure my heart is fine.*

"We're being followed," said Drago quietly.

"We're being *accompanied*," Kage corrected. "It's a palace. Palaces come with staff."

*Staff,* said the part of his head that had stayed up late with him through every strategy meeting, every recruitment, every long night of mapping and planning, *that could take your head off with a yawn and get a yawn's worth of exercise out of it.*

They had been watching him for a long time. He knew that now — or knew it as much as he allowed himself to know anything along those lines. Months before he'd noticed. Back when he'd been running himself to the bone building the clan, recruiting, strategising, sleeping four hours a night on a couch in the lab, too busy and too clever and too sure of himself to feel the weight of eyes on the back of his neck. Back when he'd thought the Shadows were bodyguards. Back when he'd thought they were the emperor's.

They *were* the emperor's.

He had just been wrong about what, exactly, they'd been guarding.

Round the next corner, a long black braid swung out of sight, and behind it, for half a breath, the tip of a tail. One of the twins. Left, or Also Left. He'd given up trying to tell. They swapped places when you weren't looking; he was fairly sure they swapped places when you *were* looking, too, and just did it faster. Calling them both Left had been a strategic decision. It reduced his error rate to zero.

And all the while, underneath everything, the weather.

That was the only way he'd ever found to describe it. The palace had a mood the way the sky has a mood, and the mood of the palace was the emperor's, and today it had been building since before they'd come through the door. Kage felt it the way you feel a storm in the joints of your fingers. The lamps were burning lower than usual. The coals in the braziers had sunk to a dull red sulk. In the hall outside the gym, a servant in human avatar carrying a tray of glasses had been walking so carefully, so very carefully, that not a single glass had chimed against another. On one of the dragon columns they'd passed, a fresh set of gouges ran down through the gold leaf, four of them, deep, pale wood showing raw beneath. They had not been there three weeks ago.

*Bring me the Dragonoid and his boy,* Kage thought, looking at the raw wood. *His words. And apparently three weeks is a crime. Apparently the emperor of all Vestroia, conqueror of five clans, survivor of a dead world, has been sitting in the dark taking it out on the furniture because I didn't come home in time for dinner.*

He would, if he could help it, never think that sentence again, either, because the mark on the left side of his neck had just pulsed — once, warm, slow — as if something somewhere had heard.

* * *

The gallery that led to the lounge had windows.

They weren't really windows. Kage had stopped thinking of them as windows a long time ago. They were arches, deep-cut, scalloped along their upper edges the way old stone is scalloped when something has been carving at it with great patience for a very long time, and beyond the arches was a balustrade of pierced sandstone, and beyond the balustrade was the night.

A full moon, white and heavy, hung low over dark trees. Beneath it a river ran slow and black, and on the far bank were the shapes of other buildings, domed and terraced, and a few lights in them, small and warm. Small glass lamps stood on the balustrade, one at the foot of each pillar, their flames dead still in air that did not move. The moonlight came through the lattice of the balustrade and lay across the floor of the gallery in a carpet of shadows, intricate as lace, so that walking down it felt like walking across the pattern of something written in a language older than any of them.

It was night out there. It was very clearly, very beautifully night.

Dan stopped at the nearest arch. He put his hands on the sandstone rail, and looked out at the moon, and then he looked at his phone, and then he looked at the moon again.

"Kage," he said. "Why is there a moon? It's, like, two in the afternoon."

"Don't ask the windows questions," Kage said. "They lie."

He had tried, early on, to work it out. He'd kept a notebook. *West gallery — moon, river, south-facing? Lounge — stars, mountains, a different river. Dining hall — sunset over sea, which sea?* He'd drawn little diagrams. He had tried to fix each window to a point on a compass, a hemisphere, a time zone. He had been meaning to ask which of the skies was real, and which ones were memories, and whose memories, and whether they were showing something that was still there or only something that had been once. He'd been meaning to ask. And then he'd been busy, and then he'd been tired, and then at some point it had simply stopped seeming like a question. The notebook was in a drawer somewhere. He couldn't have said which drawer.

Drago stood behind Dan and did not look at the moon. He looked at the floor, at the lace of shadows the lattice threw across the stone, and Kage watched his jaw work.

"The southern gallery," Drago said, very low. "This was the view from the southern gallery."

"It's a nice view," Kage said, and meant it.

Drago looked at him then, and whatever was in his face, Kage decided not to read it.

* * *

Halfway down the moonlit gallery there was a door standing a little open, and through it — blue.

Blue and teal and silver, after so much red: it hit the eye like cold water. A chamber hung with silk the colour of deep lagoons, walls carved and fretted in pale gold, lamps of pierced brass hanging low and lit, a carpet the blue of a midnight sea worked with gold vines. A bed stood against the far wall under a canopy of sheer teal hangings that moved very slightly, though there was no draught. Low sofas heaped with embroidered cushions. A table with a silver tea service, untouched, gleaming. And beyond, through three cusped arches, a view of a city: domes and spires and minarets, all blue in the moonlight, stretching away to the horizon, lit here and there with gold.

Dan actually stopped breathing for a second. Kage heard it.

"Whose room is *that?*"

"Mine," said Kage.

"*That's* yours? You get *that?*"

"I get that."

He did not go in. He did not even really look in. He looked past it, at the city, at the domes, at the moon on the domes, the same moon as the gallery's though the angle was impossible.

He had slept there three nights.

Three nights in that beautiful bed under those beautiful hangings, with the city glittering in the arches and the silk shifting in the not-wind. The first night he had lain awake for hours, too wired to sleep, running numbers on the clan's expansion. The second he'd slept like the dead. The third he'd dreamed of heat, and of something breathing very close to him in the dark, and woken alone with the silk pulled back from the bed as if by a hand.

On the fourth night he had gone to sleep in that bed and woken up somewhere else.

Now the blue room was the most expensive wardrobe in the northern hemisphere. His jackets hung there. His boots stood in a neat row beneath a window that looked out over a city that might or might not exist. The tea service was polished every day by someone he never saw. Nobody had slept in the bed since. He was quite sure nobody would.

Sometimes — not often — the memory came up on him sideways, the way it did now, unbidden and very sharp. Waking in the dark and not knowing where he was. The smell of smoke and hot stone and torn silk. An arm across his chest with the weight of a fallen column, heavy and furnace-hot and absolutely immovable. Something coiled around his ankles, long and scaled and warm, and drawn tight. Feathers in his hair, from pillows that had been opened in the night by claws. And at the back of his neck, slow and enormous and very close, a breathing he could feel in his own ribs.

He had tried, the first few times, to go back to the blue room. He had got up in the dark, very carefully, a centimetre at a time, and on two occasions he had actually made it as far as the door. He'd tried. And then — he couldn't really have said when — he had stopped trying. It had simply seemed simpler. It had seemed like the kind of thing that wasn't worth the strategic expenditure.

The memory went the way they all went, smoothly, without a sound, and left nothing behind but the faint warmth on the left side of his neck.

"Man," Dan breathed. "This place is insane."

"You get used to it," Kage said.

Drago, behind him, was not looking at the room. Drago was looking at Kage. Had been looking at him, Kage realised, for some time. Through the whole of the memory. As though he could see the shape of it on Kage's face, even through the smile.

Kage reached out and pulled the door of the blue room gently shut.

"Lounge is just ahead," he said. "There's tea. Real tea, hot, as hot as you want. I'd say you've earned it."

At the end of the gallery the moonlight stopped and another light began: softer, bluer, scattered across a high dark arch like dust. Starlight. A great deal of it. Beyond the arch there was the low warm glow of lanterns, and the far-off murmur of a river, and the scent of tea and night-blooming flowers, and the faint, steady creak of wicker.

Kage stepped aside to let his guests through first, as a good host should. He smiled at them. He smiled at the stars.

Somewhere deep in the palace, beneath their feet, in the ninety-nine percent, something very large exhaled — and every lamp in the gallery behind them leaned, very slightly, toward the sound.


* * *

The last arch opened onto the night.

Not Iceland's night, either: not that flat, iron-coloured nothing that had been pressing its thumb into Kage's eyes for the past hour. This night was deep and blue and absurdly generous, and it had been poured over a valley that did not exist on any map of this planet. The terrace ran the length of a stone gallery, arch after arch after arch, each one framing its own slice of sky the way a jeweller frames a stone, and every slice was crowded with stars. Not the polite, scattered stars of a city. The real ones. The Milky Way lay across the heavens like spilled salt across black lacquer, so thick and so close it looked as if you could put your hand out between the columns and come back with sparkling fingers.

Below, far below, a river moved through the dark — a wide, slow, silver road — and on it boats drifted with lanterns at their prows, small gold coals floating on liquid mercury. Beyond the river, mountains. Bare, ancient, heaped like sleeping animals, their ridges silvered where the starlight touched them and black where it did not. On the near bank, scattered across the dry valley floor, fires burned at the mouths of what might have been tents or might have been caves. Palms stood up against the sky like the black feathers of some enormous bird.

It was warm. That was the obscene part. The air was dry and soft and smelled of resin and stone that had spent all day in the sun, and somewhere a fountain was talking to itself.

Dan stopped so abruptly that Drago walked into his back.

"Okay," Dan said. "Okay. Where's — that's not — Kage, it was *snowing.*"

"It was sleeting," Kage corrected gently. "Snow has dignity."

He kept walking, because someone had to, and because he had learned early that the worst thing you could do in this palace was stand still in a doorway looking amazed. The palace liked that. The palace *noticed* that.

The lounge had been built along the gallery the way a hearth is built into a wall — not as an afterthought but as the point of the whole structure. Wicker sofas, dark and deep, piled with cream cushions and pillows the colour of rust and dried blood. Low round tables of black wood, each crowned with a brass lantern whose flame never flickered, never guttered, never did any of the small honest things a flame is supposed to do. Rugs lay over the flagstones in overlapping layers, red and ochre and indigo, patterned with medallions and vines and, if you looked long enough, dragons — always dragons, coiled in the borders, swallowing their own tails in the corners, peering out from the foliage with stitched gold eyes. Potted palms. Brass urns. Iron sconces bolted to the pillars, each burning steady and amber.

At the far end of the terrace, where the gallery curved and the arches grew smaller with distance, two figures stood at the balustrade looking out over the valley.

One of them was a girl in a long dark dress, her hair pinned up, her shoulders turned slightly inward against a cold that did not exist here. The other was taller, much taller, wearing a wide-brimmed hat and a pale shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbow — and from beneath the hem of his long coat a tail lay along the flagstones, dark-scaled, its tip moving in a slow, idle rhythm, left and right, left and right, like a cat watching a bird.

His hand was on the small of her back.

Kage watched it, for exactly the length of a breath, the way one watches a painting. The girl leaned on the balustrade; the hand stayed. She pointed at something on the river; the hand stayed. She shifted her weight; the hand moved with her, adjusting, flat and broad and patient, a hand that had decided where it lived. It did not stroke. It did not grip. It simply *remained*, the way a mountain remains.

*Sweet,* Kage thought, and moved his gaze along to the next arch, and did not think about it again.

"Sit," he said to his guests, gesturing at the nearest sofa with the open, benevolent palm of a man welcoming pilgrims. "Please. You've been dragged across half the world and through a hole in the air. Tea is the least the Dragon Clan can do."

Dan sat. Dan sat the way Dan did everything — all at once, with total commitment, so that the wicker creaked and a cushion shot out from behind him and had to be rescued. Drago did not sit. Drago stood beside the sofa with his arms folded, his red-scaled tail held low and stiff behind him, its tip twitching against the rug. His crimson-gold eyes went to the arches, the stars, the river; then to the two figures at the far end of the terrace; and stayed there a long moment, narrowing. His nostrils flared once.

Kage took the chair opposite — wicker, high-backed, cream cushions, pleasantly sinful — and crossed one leg over the other, leather creaking, spikes on his shoulders catching lantern-light.

He did not ring for anyone. He never did.

The servant came anyway.

It came from nowhere in particular, out of the shadow between two columns where Kage would have sworn there had been nothing but shadow, and it moved across the rugs without any sound at all. Human avatar, of course — they were all human avatars here, outside the arena; Kage had long ago stopped finding that strange and started finding it efficient. It was a young man, or the shape of one, bare-armed and barefoot, with hair the yellowed white of old bone tied back from a face as smooth and still as poured wax. Two small horns like thorns. A tail like a length of wet rope. It carried a brass tray on which sat a teapot, three small glasses in silver holders, a dish of dates, and a plate of thin golden pastries that shed flakes like gold leaf.

It set the tray down on the table between Kage and Dan. It poured. The tea came out of the spout in a long amber thread, dark as the river outside, and steam rose from the glasses carrying the smell of mint and something smoky underneath.

It did not look at Dan. It did not look at Drago. It looked at Kage — once, briefly, the way one checks that a candle is still lit — and then it bowed, folding at the waist with a fluid exactness that no human spine would have survived, and walked backwards into the shadow between the columns and was not there anymore.

Dan stared at the place where it had been.

"Does he… do that a lot?"

"Constantly," said Kage. "You get used to it."

*You don't,* said the back of his skull, *but you do stop screaming.*

He picked up his glass. The silver holder was warm, and the tea burned his tongue in that exquisite way that only matters after a day spent in Icelandic sleet, and for one second Kage closed his eyes and allowed himself to simply be a body that had been very cold and was now warm. His fingers came back to him one by one, like soldiers trickling home from a lost war.

*Three weeks,* he thought. *Three weeks of airports and bus stations and that unspeakable hostel in Rotterdam, and a portal that spits you out sideways, and now tea. Worth it. Possibly. Ask me again when I can feel my toes.*

"So," said Dan. He had a pastry in one hand and a glass in the other and gold flakes on his chin, and he leaned forward with his elbows on his knees, eyes bright as a lit fuse. "This place. It's yours?"

"It's the Clan's," said Kage. "I run it."

"You *run* this? Like — all of it? The gym and the lab and the—" he waved the pastry at the infinite starlit sky, scattering gold, "—the space? The weird windows?"

"I run the humans. I run the strategy. I run the budget." Kage tilted his head, smile serene. "The windows run themselves."

"The budget," Drago repeated. He had not touched his tea. "For all this."

"For all this," Kage agreed pleasantly. "And for anything else the Clan needs. Equipment. Travel. Food. Tuition, for the ones still in school. Legal fees, for the ones who get caught brawling on the wrong side of a fence." He reached into the inner pocket of his jacket — it took two tries; the leather was still stiff with cold — and drew out a card. Matte black. No numbers embossed on the front, no bank logo, nothing but a single tiny mark in the corner that might have been a stylised claw. He turned it between two fingers so the lantern-light slid along its edge. "The Clan can pay for anything."

Dan's eyes went round. "Is that—"

"Yes."

"Like the *black* black one? The one with no—"

"No limit," said Kage, in the tone of a man reporting the weather.

He tucked the card away again. His heart, as it always did when he touched that card, made a small, undignified flip, like a fish in a bucket.

Because he still could not believe it. He had held it a hundred times now, used it in grocery stores and train stations and once, memorably, at a two-in-the-morning ramen stall where the owner had looked at it and then looked at Kage's face and then looked at the card again with an expression of profound spiritual crisis — and every single time, some small, starved, eleven-year-old part of him braced for the machine to beep and decline. It never did. It would never do so. Somewhere in the cold marble guts of the human banking system lay a heap of imperial gold so vast that it had changed the weather of several markets, and some fraction of it — Kage did not like to think about how large a fraction — answered to this rectangle of black plastic and to him.

The gold had come from Vestroia. Some of it, at least. The emperor had looked upon this world's creatures and their strange paper wealth and their flimsy little numbers on screens, and he had found them laughable — but he was not a fool, and he understood that an empire needs hands to move in a world, and in this world, hands were made of money. So the treasury had been moved. Part of it. Into human vaults, under human names, so that the empire could *operate*, as the Tall One had put it, in that voice like a blade being slowly drawn.

Kage remembered the night the card had been given to him. He remembered turning it over in the lamplight of the emperor's chambers, and saying — because he was eighteen and tired and his brain had been running on strategy and cold coffee for nineteen hours straight and his mouth had briefly escaped supervision — *Okay, but what's the limit?*

And he remembered Nillious looking at him.

The emperor had been lying on his side among the cushions with his head propped on one fist, his hair spilling black and gold across the torn silk, and he had looked at Kage for a long time with those molten eyes, the pupils narrowing, slowly, to two thin black seams. And then he had smiled. It was not a large smile. Nillious did not do large. It was a slow, curling, terrible little thing that began at one corner of his mouth and showed, before it was finished, the long white point of one tooth.

*What,* the emperor had asked, with real and dreadful curiosity, *is a limit?*

Kage had not slept particularly well that night. Though, in fairness, that might have been the arm.

"That's insane," Dan said, with enormous respect. "That's completely insane. You could buy, like — a car! You could buy a *helicopter*."

"I could," said Kage. "I bought a scarf."

*It's a very good scarf. Cashmere. I left it in the—*

He did not finish the thought. He picked up a date instead, and turned it over between finger and thumb, and observed that it was glossy and dark and wrinkled like an old man's knuckle, and ate it, and it was very sweet.

Drago had at last unfolded his arms. He did not sit, but he moved closer to the sofa, closer to Dan — a slight, unconscious adjustment, the way a large animal shifts its body between its young and the dark beyond the firelight. He looked out through the nearest arch at the river, the boats, the mountains. His tail curled once around his own ankle and released.

"Kage," he said. "Every Bakugan in this MOD. The servants. The ones in the gym. That one." A tilt of the chin toward the far end of the terrace, where the tall figure in the hat still stood beside the girl, still with his hand on her back. "Are all of them Dragon types?"

"All of them," said Kage. "This is the location of the Dragon Clan."

"A Dragon Clan." Drago turned the word over as if testing it for poison. "There was no such thing in Vestroia. There were the dragons, and the rest. We did not need a word for it."

"This isn't Vestroia," Kage said, mildly. And then, because the stars outside the arches were very clearly Vestroian, or near enough, and the river was a river no human had ever seen, and the palace around them was — as Drago had recognised within three steps of the door — the Dragon Palace of Vestroia remade stone by stone, he added: "Mostly."

Dan looked from one of them to the other. "Wait, so there's a *word* for it now? Why?"

Kage set his glass down. He laced his fingers together over one knee. And there it was — the switch inside him, the one that slid across like a bolt in a well-oiled lock whenever the conversation turned to the shape of things. Strategy. Systems. Pieces on a board. His voice dropped half a tone and lost its silk, and if anyone at that table had known him well, they would have recognised it as the only voice he used when he was telling the truth.

"Ever since brawling was outlawed," he said, "the Bakugan in this world have had nowhere to go. No arenas. No battles. No legal reason to exist at full size, and no one to pair with who isn't breaking the law to do it. So they did what living things do when the world gets hostile. They found their own kind. Bakugan of the same type began grouping together. Sharing territory. Sharing resources. Sharing brawlers." A small, gentle shrug. "Forming Clans."

"There are more?" Drago's head came around, sharp. "More than this one?"

"Five more." Kage counted them off on his fingers, unhurried. "The Insect Clan. The Dino Clan. The Aquatic Clan. The Mammal Clan. The Avian Clan. Each with its own MOD, its own territory, its own brawlers." He spread his hands. "Six clans. Six houses. One very small, very angry world."

"Six," Drago said, very quietly.

Something moved behind his eyes — some old arithmetic, some memory of tribes and wars and banners, of other species of Bakugan marching under other colours across the plains of a world that no longer existed. Kage watched him do the sum, and watched him not like the answer.

"And they all just — get along?" Dan asked.

"They're very different," said Kage, which was true. "Different territories, different cultures, different ideas about what a brawl should look like. The Insect Clan won't let you into their MOD unless you can find the door yourself. The Aquatic Clan has rules about shoes." He let a small, wry smile touch his mouth — the first one all evening that he did not have to hold in place by force. "But on one thing, every Clan agrees. The authorities were wrong to take brawling away. Brawling is what the Bakugan *are*. You can't outlaw a Bakugan's nature any more than you can outlaw a fire for being hot. So the Clans have decided, together, not to follow those orders." He met Dan's eyes. "We brawl. We train. We keep our partners. We keep each other. And we wait."

"For what?"

"For the world to change its mind," said Kage. "Or for us to change it."

*And meanwhile,* murmured the colder voice at the back of his head, the one that kept the actual ledgers, *the Insect Clan pays tribute in rare minerals every new moon, the Dino Clan's leader came here on his knees in March and has not stopped sweating since, and the Avian Clan sends its best brawlers to train in our gym because they were told to, and they come.* Different Clans. Allies, if you were being polite. If you were an outsider. If you were a boy with gold flakes on his chin who still believed in the word *alliance*.

The emperor did not say *allies*.

The emperor said *colonies*. He said it out loud. He said it the way other people said *furniture*.

Kage took a sip of his tea.

"Okay," said Dan slowly. "Okay. So — you brought us here because…"

"Because I want you to join the Dragon Clan," Kage said.

He let it sit there in the warm air between them, simple as a stone dropped into a still pool. Then he leaned forward, elbows on his knees, mirroring Dan's posture exactly — open shoulders, open hands, eye contact level and frank and unafraid — and let the warmth come into his voice the way a priest lets it come into a benediction. He had practised this. Not the words. The *weight*.

"You're the best brawler I've ever seen, Dan. You know that, don't you? Instinct like yours can't be taught. And Drago—" a nod, respectful, carefully measured, "—Drago is a legend. Literally. Wherever brawling survives, it survives because of people like you. But right now you're out there alone, dodging patrols, brawling in parking lots, passing your partner off as a cousin from out of town to every patrol that squints at his horns — and one day someone is going to catch you, and they will take him away, and that will be the end of it. Here, nobody takes anybody away. Here you have a gym, and a lab, and an arena, and opponents worth fighting, and more resources than you could spend in ten lifetimes." He let a small, rueful laugh escape. "Believe me, I've tried."

Dan's face was doing something complicated and luminous. It was the face of a boy who has been holding his breath underwater for a very long time and has just seen, overhead, the wavering silver of the surface.

*There it is,* Kage thought, with a cold, clean satisfaction that he did not let anywhere near his face. *Hook. Line. Golden retriever.*

It was not even a lie. That was the beauty of it. Everything he had said was true. The gym was real. The lab was real. The money was so real it gave him palpitations. Dan was the best brawler Kage had ever seen, and the Dragon Clan needed him, and the strategy was sound — had been sound three weeks ago when Kage had drafted it at two in the morning on the emperor's own writing desk, under the emperor's own lamp, while the emperor slept behind him in the nest with one arm thrown across the place where Kage was supposed to be and a low, displeased rumble going on and on in his chest like a distant engine, because Kage was not there, because Kage was at the *desk*, because Kage was being — what was the word Smile had used later, so gently, so courteously, with that dreadful, patient smile — *withheld*.

Kage blinked. The tea had gone cold in his glass. He put it down.

"It sounds amazing," Dan breathed. "Seriously. Drago, it sounds—"

"We should discuss it."

Drago's voice was low. Not loud, not angry. Something more careful than either. He had come to stand at the arm of the sofa now, directly beside Dan, one hand resting on the cushion behind Dan's shoulders — not touching him, never quite touching him, but there. His tail lay along the rug in a long red curve, a line drawn around the two of them.

"In private," Drago said. "The two of us. Before any decision is made."

Dan's face fell slightly, the way a flame bends in a draught. "Drago, come on, it's *Kage*, he's—"

"In private, Dan."

Kage raised both hands, palms out, all gracious surrender. "Of course. Of course you should talk. This isn't something you decide over tea." *Even very good tea. Even tea a dragon brought you on a brass tray with no sound and no soul.* "Take as long as you need. You'll have rooms here tonight regardless. Talk it over. Sleep on it. Whatever you decide, the Clan will respect it."

*The Clan will not respect it,* noted the ledger-keeper behind his eyes. *The Clan does not know what that word means. But the Clan is not making the offer. I am.*

Drago looked at him.

It was a long look. It was not a human look. Drago's head tilted — slowly, a few degrees further than a human neck would have chosen to go — and his eyes moved over Kage's face, and then lower. To the collar of the jacket. To the side of Kage's neck, where the dark leather sat close against the skin and the skin beneath it was very, very warm.

His nostrils flared.

He did not say anything. That was the worst part. A human would have said something. A human would have said *what's that* or *are you okay* or *you smell strange*. Drago just went still — the full, predatory stillness of a creature listening to a sound beneath the threshold of hearing — and stayed still, one heartbeat, two, three.

Then he said, quietly, "Did you choose this clan, Kage?"

And Kage opened his mouth to answer, smooth and immediate, *of course* — and something in his head turned over, a little, like a sleeper rolling in bed. A thought began. It began somewhere far back and low down, in some dim cellar of himself, and it started to climb, and it had a shape, almost, the shape of a question, the shape of *did I* —

— the tea, he thought. He'd let the tea go cold. Criminal. The pot would still be hot, though; those brass pots held their heat for hours, there was something in the metal, or something in the dragons, and the mint here was very good, fresh, they grew it on one of the terraces further in, he could have a second glass —

"Of course," said Kage, and smiled. "I founded the human half of it."

He reached for the teapot and poured himself a second glass. The tea came out hot. It always did.

Drago watched him do it. Drago's tail tightened around Dan's ankle — Dan didn't seem to notice — and stayed tight.

"I see," said Drago.

He did not say anything else.

Kage sipped his tea and admired the stars and did not, at any point, wonder what Drago saw.

"Okay," Dan said, and let out a long breath, and visibly decided to be reasonable. "Okay, okay. We'll talk. We'll talk tonight. But I'm telling you right now, Drago, the gym alone—"

"Tonight," Drago agreed.

"—like, did you *see* the gym—"

"I saw it."

And that was the moment, as it always was the moment, when the air changed.

It was not a sound. Kage would have been able to hear a sound. It was more like the feeling of a cloud crossing the sun on a day when there is no sun and no cloud — a dimming that happened behind the eyes rather than in front of them, a faint pressure on the eardrums, the lantern flames on the nearest table leaning, all of them at once, a single degree toward the dark between two columns.

Smile was standing at Kage's shoulder.

He had not walked there. He had not come through the arch, or along the gallery, or out of the shadows. He had simply become present, the way a word becomes present on the tip of the tongue, and he stood now at Kage's left side and a half-step behind, close enough that Kage could feel the heat coming off his skin like the heat off a stone at dusk. His dark face was turned down toward Kage, and his small curled horns caught the lamplight, and his white-gold eyes were soft as candle-flames, and he was smiling.

It was a beautiful smile. Courteous. Gentle. Faintly fond. It was the smile of a host who is so very glad you came, and who has already decided which of your bones he will keep.

Dan jumped so hard his tea slopped over his wrist. Drago's lips peeled back from his teeth before he could stop them, and a sound came out of his chest — short, low, a warning — and then cut off as his own mind caught up with his instincts.

Smile did not look at either of them.

"Kage," he said. His voice was soft, musical, unhurried. Every word was set down carefully, like a cup on a saucer. "The emperor will see them."

Kage set his glass down very precisely in its ring of spilled tea.

"Now?" he asked, with perfect, priestly calm.

"Now," Smile agreed, still smiling.

*Now,* Kage thought. *Of course now.* He had been in this palace long enough to have become fluent in a language that no human dictionary contained, and in that language, *now* had a very specific meaning. *Now* did not mean *at your earliest convenience*. *Now* did not even mean *immediately*. *Now*, in dragon, meant *you are already late, and he has been counting.*

He rose. He brushed a single gold pastry flake from his knee. He turned to his guests, and his smile was the smile of a man walking out onto a stage, and his face felt, from the inside, like a pane of glass in a hard frost — one small tap, and it would go to pieces.

"Gentlemen," he said. "Shall we? The founder of the Dragon Clan would like to welcome you personally."

Dan was already on his feet, wiping tea off his wrist onto his jeans, his eyes alight with fresh curiosity. "The founder? Who's the founder?"

"You'll see," said Kage.

Drago said nothing at all. But he walked very close behind Dan as they crossed the terrace, his shoulder a hair's breadth from Dan's shoulder, and he did not take his eyes off Smile's back.

Smile led them. Smile always led. He went ahead of them along the gallery, under the arches, past the lanterns, his bare feet soundless on the rugs and then on the cool bare stone, and his tail moved behind him in a long, slow, contented sway, and the lantern flames bent toward him as he passed and straightened again behind.

At the far end of the terrace, the girl at the balustrade had turned to watch them go. The tall figure in the hat had not. He was looking at her. His hand was still on her back.

The gallery ended in a doorway, and the doorway opened onto a corridor, and the corridor went down. Not by stairs. It simply sloped, gently and continuously, into a warmer and darker place, where the walls were dark wood carved so densely with scales and vines and wings that they seemed to breathe in the unsteady light. The air thickened. The smell of resin deepened into smoke, and under the smoke, something else, something hot and mineral and alive, like the inside of a forge, like the breath of a very large animal asleep in a very small room.

And as they walked, the mark on Kage's neck began to throb.

It was a slow pulse. It was a warm one. It beat beneath the collar of his jacket in a steady, patient rhythm, and he noticed it in the way you notice your own tongue in your mouth — briefly, and then not at all. It beat once. And again. It was not in time with his heart. His heart was going quick and light, nerves and caffeine and three weeks of exhaustion all jostling for room in his ribs. This was slower than that. Deeper. Heavier.

It was in time with something else.

Something far ahead of them, at the bottom of this long, sloping, breathing dark, waiting on a throne.

*Columns,* Kage thought, walking. *The throne room has those very good columns. I'll look at the columns.*

And he lifted his chin, and fixed his smile, and followed Smile down into the heat.


* * *

The doors of the throne room did not open so much as consent.

They were three times the height of a man and carved from a wood so dark it drank the lanternlight, banded in gold that had gone the soft, sullen colour of old honey. Smile laid one hand flat against them — not a push, Kage had noticed long ago, never a push; more like a greeting between two things that had known each other for a very long time — and they swung inward without a sound. No hinge-groan. No draught. Just the slow parting of a darkness that had been waiting on the other side, the way a mouth waits.

The heat came first.

It rolled out over the threshold in a single breath, dry and resinous and heavy as wet wool, and it smelled of everything this palace smelled of, only more: myrrh smoke and hot brass, old cedar, candle tallow, the mineral tang of a forge — and under it, threaded through it the way a vein runs through marble, the smell of *him*. Embers. Spice. Something animal and enormous that the back of Kage's skull recognised before the front of it had finished being polite.

Behind him, Kage heard Dan suck in a breath through his teeth.

Behind *Dan*, he heard Drago stop breathing altogether.

*Good,* Kage thought, and stepped through.

* * *

He had tried, once — in the early days, when he had still believed that understanding a place was a way of owning it — to measure the throne room.

He had paced it. Door to dais, four hundred and twelve of his own strides. Wall to wall, he had lost count somewhere in the two hundreds when the columns started to repeat themselves in a way he did not like, and he had stood in the middle of all that dim gold and red and realised that the room was larger than the building that held it, and the building was larger than the fire station that held *that*, and that if he kept counting he would arrive at a number that made his teeth hurt.

So he had stopped counting, and started looking at the columns instead.

There were thirty-six of them. They rose out of the floor in two great files like the trunks of a petrified forest, each one so wide that three Kages holding hands could not have ringed it, each one fluted from base to crown in long black grooves edged with gold leaf. Their capitals flowered into carved dragon wings, folded and overlapping, scale upon carved scale, and between the wings the stone had been worked into faces — reptilian, serene, eyes shut — so that the whole ceiling seemed to rest on the dreaming heads of the dead. Braziers stood between them on tripods of black iron, and their fire was the deep, clotted orange of something that had been burning for a thousand years and did not intend to stop. Smoke rose from every one of them and gathered under the vaulting in a slow brown sea, so that the ceiling, if there was a ceiling, was only a rumour.

Down the centre ran a carpet the colour of old blood, so long that its far end dissolved into the haze.

At that far end, there was a dais. Seven steps of black stone, each one edged in gold. And on the dais, a throne.

And on the throne—

Kage looked at the columns.

The eleventh column on the left had a crack in the gold leaf about a third of the way up that looked, if you tilted your head, like the coastline of Italy. He had found it on his second week. He had been very grateful for it ever since.

* * *

The Shadows were already there.

Of course they were. Smile had been at Kage's shoulder a breath ago, his footsteps silent on the carpet, his small courteous smile fixed in place like a brooch — and now he was not. Now he stood at the foot of the dais as if he had been standing there for an hour, hands folded, head bowed at an angle that was very nearly respectful. Kage did not turn around to check whether the doors behind them were still being held. He had stopped doing that, too.

There were five of them. There were always five.

The Tall One stood at the emperor's right hand, on the top step, where no one else was ever permitted to stand. His hair fell to his waist in a sheet of white so pale it looked silver in the brazier-light, and his horns swept back from his temples like two long blades of bone, and his face had the cool, finished perfection of a statue that has been told it is beautiful and found the information beneath it. He did not look at the guests. He looked at a point somewhere above their heads, which was worse.

Doesn't-Blink stood two steps below him. Copper-red hair cropped close to a skull that seemed too narrow, too fine, for the eyes it held — and the eyes were molten, a deep pooled gold, and they were fixed on Drago, and they did not blink. They would not blink. Kage had once sat through a four-hour budget meeting opposite Doesn't-Blink and had come out of it with a headache and the private conviction that he had been audited by a lizard. The scales that climbed the Shadow's throat in a raised, glittering scar caught the firelight when he swallowed, which was rarely.

Left and Also Left flanked the bottom of the steps. Identical: black hair in a single heavy braid each, grey eyes like rain on slate, the same faint smile-less mouth. Kage was almost certain that when the doors had opened, the one on the left had been on the right. He had stopped trying to keep track some months ago. There was a kind of peace in surrender.

And Smile, at the very foot of the carpet, dark and lovely, small curled horns like the beginnings of a ram's, white-gold eyes and that gentle, patient, unbearable smile — the smile of a person who has been waiting for you to make a mistake and is prepared to wait a little longer, out of kindness.

Five human shapes. Five tails, Kage thought, and found them without trying: the Tall One's pale and still as a draped rope; Doesn't-Blink's red-scaled, the tip lifting and lowering in a slow, measured rhythm, like a metronome counting down to something; the twins', black, crossed behind them in a way that made it impossible to tell which belonged to whom. Smile's was wrapped once around his own ankle, neat as a garter.

This, Kage thought, was not a clan.

This was a *pantheon*. He had walked into a room with a full set of Greek gods — the cold one, the cruel one, the pair who were probably a myth about the seasons, the one who smiled while he weighed your soul — and at the top of it all, lounging on the mountain, the one who threw the lightning.

At some point in the last year, without Kage being consulted, his life had turned into an alien romance manhwa. One of the long ones. Pastel cover, title thirty words long. *I Was Only the Clan's Strategist, So Why Is the Dragon Emperor Using Me as a Pillow?* Volume one of an ongoing series. Three hundred chapters and every single one of them ended on a close-up of somebody's collarbone.

Where was the save button. Where was the menu. Who did you contact, in this situation, to report a bug.

He kept walking. The carpet was soft enough to drown in. The priest's smile sat on his face like a mask of thin porcelain, and he could feel, very faintly, the exact place along his jaw where it would crack.

* * *

The air in the throne room was wrong.

Kage felt it before he had gone twenty paces. It was not the heat — the heat was always here, the heat was the room's native language. It was the *weight*. The smoke under the vaults hung low and motionless, pressed down as though by a palm. The braziers burned without flickering, every flame standing straight and sullen. The gold leaf on the columns seemed dimmer than it should, and when Kage passed the first of the tripods, he saw that its iron legs bore three long fresh scores — parallel, deep, bright metal showing through black, the kind of marks a hand leaves when it closes on something and does not care what happens to it.

On the arm of the throne, under the gold dragon's head, there would be more. Kage did not need to look. He knew the emperor's moods the way sailors know weather: in the knees, in the back teeth, in the skin. And the mark beneath his collar — the small, old, unimportant mark on the left side of his throat, the one he never thought about — had begun to throb the moment the doors opened, slow and heavy, not in time with his heart.

*Three weeks,* he thought. *He told me to go and get them. He told me personally. And then he sat here for three weeks and broke the furniture because I went.*

He would never, under any circumstances, say this out loud. There were some sentences that were less like sentences and more like stepping off a roof.

He reached the foot of the dais. He did not look up. He bowed — exactly as deep as was correct, a degree less than would be servile — and in the corner of his eye he saw Dan, two paces behind, half-copy the motion out of sheer social panic and then stop, and Drago not bow at all.

And then the emperor breathed in.

It was not a sound, quite. It was a change in the room — the way the room changes when a wave pulls back from the shore and you understand, too late, how much water there is. Kage felt it pull at the loose spikes of his hair. Felt it drag past his throat, past the collar of his jacket, past the mark. Somewhere above him, enormous lungs filled slowly and completely with the air that had touched him, and held it, and *tasted* it.

The breath went out again in a long, low rumble that Kage felt in his sternum before he heard it. It was the kind of sound a mountain makes when it settles. It went through the floor and up through the soles of his boots, and the gold leaf on the nearest column hummed with it, faint as a struck glass.

The Shadows, all five, lowered their heads a quarter-inch. In unison.

And the room *changed*.

The smoke under the vaults loosened, began to drift, to curl. Every brazier along the hall flared at once, a soft roar of flame rising half a foot higher, throwing new gold up the columns until the carved wings on their capitals seemed to stir. The weight lifted off the air like a hand taken from a throat. Kage felt it on his skin — physically, ridiculously, a warmth moving over his face and down his arms like stepping out of shade into sun — and the mark at his neck stopped throbbing and simply *glowed*, a low, contented heat, as though something had been put back where it belonged.

*Ah,* thought Kage, with the serene detachment of a man watching his own house burn. *So that's where the weather went.*

"You took," said a voice above him, "your time."

* * *

He made himself look up. Not at the face — never straight at the face, not at first, that was how you lost a whole minute of your life — but at the throne.

It was carved of the same black wood as the doors, and it was not so much a chair as a reliquary for a god. Its back rose in a great spread fan of gilded flame and scrollwork, curling up and up into points like a crown of dark fire. The seat and the back were upholstered in red velvet so deep it looked wet. And the arms ended in two golden dragon heads, jaws parted, eyes set with something that caught the light like garnets — and on the left one, the gold had been gouged in four long ragged furrows, so deep the wood showed through underneath, as if something had sat here for three weeks with its hand resting on that head and slowly, absently, closed its fist.

The emperor lounged across all of it like spilled ink.

He was too big for the room, which was too big for the building. That was always the first thing: the sheer animal *mass* of him. Even sprawled, even at ease, he took up the throne the way a storm takes up a sky. Shoulders broad enough to carry a yoke. A chest that rose and fell in the slow, deep, unhurried rhythm of something that has never once in its existence had to run from anything. His black robe was embroidered in gold at the sleeves and hem — dragons, coiling, devouring each other's tails — and it hung open from throat to belt, carelessly, the way only something that has never felt cold or shame or the need to explain itself wears clothing. Under it his skin was dark bronze and burnished by the firelight, and the muscle beneath it was not a human's muscle, not the kind built in gyms; it was the dense, layered, functional bulk of a body made for tearing.

His hair fell over all of it in a black flood, past his shoulders, past his ribs, coarse and heavy and streaked through with gold — not dyed gold, not human gold, but the gold of the inside of a flame. From it rose the horns: dark, ridged, backswept, curving from his temples and splitting into lesser points so that they formed, together, the jagged silhouette of a broken crown. Smoke rose off him. Not from anything — from *him*, from the skin itself, in thin grey threads that curled up past his face and vanished into the haze, as if he were very slowly, very patiently, burning.

He had his chin resting on two knuckles of one hand. The nails on that hand were long, and dark, and came to points, and were not nails.

And his eyes—

Kage had once, in his first month, tried to decide what colour they were, and had given up. Amber, in some lights. Blood-gold, in others. Right now, in the new flare of the braziers, they were the colour of honey with a fire behind it, and the pupils in them were not round. They were long and black and narrow as a blade laid on its edge, and as Kage watched, they widened — slowly, the black spreading through the gold like ink through water — and then contracted again, all at once, to slits.

The emperor looked at him the way a mountain looks at the valley it is about to fall into.

"Twenty-one days, Your Radiance," Kage said, and his voice came out smooth and calm and sweetly reasonable, because he had practised this voice in mirrors until it could survive anything. "The boy moves around a great deal."

"Hm."

That was all. A single syllable, low and closed, from somewhere at the bottom of that enormous chest. It was not agreement. It was not forgiveness. It was the sound of something being *noted* — filed away in a ledger whose pages were made of skin.

And then Nillious's gaze left Kage, unhurried, and travelled — down the dais, down the carpet, past Smile's bowed head — and settled on the guests.

His mouth, which had been a flat, hard line a moment ago, curved.

Kage had seen that smile before. It was a very particular smile. It was the smile of a predator that has been hungry and bored for three weeks, and has just been handed — on a single day, at a single moment — both the thing it wanted and something else to break.

*Oh no,* Kage thought, without much pity. *He's in a* good *mood.*

* * *

Something moved on the floor.

It came down the steps of the dais the way a river comes down rock: a long, heavy, sinuous length of black, scales the size of a thumbnail, each one edged in a thin line of gold that caught the firelight as it moved. It made no hurry about it. It slid from under the hem of the emperor's robe and down over the gold-edged stone, step by step by step, thick as Kage's thigh at its root and tapering, over a length that did not bear thinking about, to a tip that ended in a flat, wicked spade of bone. It reached the foot of the dais. It crossed the last yard of carpet.

It found Kage's boots.

It went around them once — a slow, warm, unhurried loop, a weight that settled over the leather like the coil of a sleeping python. It went around them again, higher, over his ankles. Then it rose. It wound up the backs of his calves in a long, deliberate spiral, warm through the fabric of his trousers, heavier than it had any right to be, the scales sliding against each other with a soft dry whisper like silk dragged across sand, until it had wrapped both his legs together from ankle to knee and the spade-tip came to rest — lightly, possessively, *casually* — against the side of his knee and lay there, flicking once.

Kage did not look down.

He was looking at the columns.

They really were *extraordinary* columns. The fluting, for one thing. Twenty-four flutes per column — he had counted — each one terminating at the base in a little carved scroll like a curled claw. The way the gold leaf had been laid into the edges of every groove so that, when the braziers flared, the whole column seemed to be made of long vertical rivers of fire running down black rock. The capitals: wings over wings, every scale individually carved, and between them those sleeping dragon faces, eyes shut, lips parted a fraction over teeth — and if you looked at the fourth column on the right, just there, just below the third wing, there was a chip in the gold where the stone showed white underneath. Like a tooth with a cavity. Fascinating. He had never noticed it before. He would have to have it seen to.

There was, he was fairly sure, a tail around his legs.

He was not going to look at it. Looking at it would make it a conversation.

His face did not move. The priest's smile did not slip by so much as a hair. Beside him, he felt rather than saw Dan's eyes go to the floor, to the coils, to Kage's face, and back to the floor, and Kage gave no sign — not a flicker — that there was anything at all on the carpet worth looking at. If you behaved, absolutely and perfectly, as though a thing was normal, then for everyone in the room except one, it became normal. That was the first lesson of strategy. That was also, he suspected, the first lesson of being a house cat.

The tail tightened, very slightly, around his knees.

Somewhere above him, the emperor made a sound so low and soft it was almost not a sound. A rumble, deep in the chest, continuous, like a great engine idling.

The Tall One's eyes, for the first time, moved. They went to the coils around Kage's legs. They went back to the point above everybody's heads.

*I am the leader of this clan,* Kage thought, studying the capital of column number nine with profound scholarly interest. *I run its finances. I designed its training regime. I personally negotiated the terms with three other MODs. I am a strategist. I am eighteen years old and I have a black card with no limit on it. I am a respected figure in the human brawling underground.*

The tail flicked against his knee. *Mine,* it said, in a language older than words.

*And I am standing in the middle of a throne room,* Kage concluded, *being worn like a bracelet.*

He cleared his throat, gently.

"Dan Kouzo. Drago." He turned — just from the shoulders up, the tail did not allow more and he did not ask it to — and lifted one hand toward the throne with the grave, generous courtesy of a man presenting a cathedral. "Allow me to introduce the founder of the Dragon Clan. His Radiance, Emperor of the Dragons and of all Vestroia—" a beat, perfectly placed "—Nillious."

* * *

There was a silence.

It lasted exactly as long as it took for the name to cross the space between them and land.

Then Dan said, "*Nillious?*" — and his voice cracked up through two octaves on the second syllable, and echoed off thirty-six columns, and came back to them smaller.

Drago did not say anything at all.

Kage had been watching Drago since the doors. It was his job to watch everyone, but he had been watching Drago especially, because of all of them, Drago was the one who did not yet understand where he was. Drago in his human avatar was all lean edges — tall and red-haired and white-streaked, pale horns swept back close against his skull, crimson-gold eyes too bright in a face that tried very hard to look human and did not quite manage. His tail was red-scaled, sleeker than the emperor's, and since they had come through the doors it had been carried low and still, rigid, the way a cat carries its tail in a strange house.

Now it lashed. Once, hard, from side to side, so fast the air cracked. The plates of scale along his forearms rose — physically *rose*, lifting from the skin like the hackles of a dog — and his lips peeled back from his teeth, and from somewhere deep in his narrow chest came a sound that no human throat could make: a low, grinding, rattling growl, like stones dragged over stones.

Every one of the five Shadows turned their head toward that sound. In perfect unison. At precisely the same speed.

Then they turned their heads back.

Doesn't-Blink's tail stopped its slow metronome sway and simply hung, poised, in the air.

"Of course," Dan was saying. He'd run both hands into his hair; he looked like someone who has just stepped into a familiar room and found all the furniture nailed to the ceiling. "Of *course*. Dragon Clan. Dragon. He's — he's a *dragon*. And he's — he was the emperor of — I should've *known*. How did I not—Drago, did you know?"

"No," said Drago, through his teeth.

"He's the one who—" Dan rounded on Kage, all bright outraged sincerity, as though Kage had personally hidden a snake in his lunchbox. "You didn't *say*. You said 'founder'. You said 'a really strong Bakugan who wants to bring brawling back'. You didn't say it was *him*!"

"I said the founder of the Dragon Clan," said Kage gently, "and that he is a very strong Bakugan who wants to restore brawling. All of which is true."

"That's — that's lying with the truth!"

"That," said Kage, "is called *briefing*."

Above them, the rumble in the emperor's chest deepened by a fraction. Kage did not look up, but he did not need to. He knew that note. It was amusement.

"Okay." Dan's chin came up. It was a good chin, Kage thought distantly; stubborn, square, the chin of a boy who had never once in his life had the sense to be afraid of the right things. "Okay. No. We said we'd think about it, and we thought about it, and the answer's no. Forget it. Deal's off. Come on, Drago, we're—"

"No."

The emperor did not raise his voice. He did not need to. The word simply *arrived*, from the top of the dais, low and dark and absolute, and it fell on the room like a bolt sliding home in a lock. The braziers dipped. The smoke under the vaults went still.

Dan's mouth stayed open on the next syllable. Nothing came out of it.

"You walked through my door," said Nillious.

He had not moved. His chin still rested on his two knuckles. His claws lay against the gilded jaw of the dragon armrest, and the gold under them was scored. Only his eyes had shifted, and the pupils in them were narrow as needles.

"You crossed my threshold. You breathed my smoke. You let my clan show you its rooms and its weapons and its secrets, and you drank tea in my house, from my cups, at my table." A pause, in which something huge and patient seemed to settle more comfortably on the throne. "In my house, little one, the door *is* the agreement. You said yes with your feet before your mouth had thought of the question."

"That's not—" Dan's voice was much smaller now. "That's not how agreements *work*—"

"It is how *mine* work."

Kage studied the coastline of Italy on column eleven.

He had walked through that door himself, once. A long time ago — eleven months? ten? — with a duffel bag over one shoulder and a plan, a very good plan, and he remembered pausing on the threshold, remembered the wash of warm resinous dark on his face, remembered thinking very clearly that this was an opportunity and that if it went wrong he could always—

The capital of the fourth column on the right had a chip in the gold leaf. Just below the third wing. Like a cavity in a tooth.

He really would have to have someone look at that.

* * *

"Humans," said Nillious.

He said it the way someone else might say *mould*, or *damp*, or *the smell in the cellar* — not with hatred; hatred required you to take a thing seriously. With a kind of weary, connoisseur's distaste. His gaze moved over Dan, slowly, top to bottom, the way a buyer at a market runs his eye over a goat he has no intention of purchasing.

"This world's creatures. Soft-skinned. Short-lived. They break when they are dropped, and they are dropped very often." The tip of his tongue touched one long canine, idly, and withdrew. "They cannot fly. They cannot burn. They cannot see in the dark. They spend a third of their lives unconscious and the rest of it frightened. They have no scales, no claws, no fire, and they are proud of the most remarkable things." The pupils narrowed. "An inferior life form, in every measurable sense."

"*Hey!*" said Dan.

The emperor did not look at him. He was not speaking to Dan. Kage understood, with the cold precision that had kept him alive this long, that he was speaking *past* Dan — to Drago, over the boy's head, the way you discuss a dog in the dog's presence.

"And yet," Nillious went on, and the word *yet* dropped into the room like a stone into a well. "And *yet*, when one of them stands behind us in the arena, we grow. We become larger. Stronger. More *ourselves*. Something in the soft little animal feeds the fire." He tilted his head — and it went too far, a degree past where a human neck would stop, a slow serpentine cant that set the horns at an angle and made the firelight slide down their ridges like water. "A curious arrangement. I have not decided whether it is a gift or an insult. So I am patient with them. Perhaps one in two of them is worth the air he takes."

He let that sit.

"I am being," he added, "generous."

*Well,* thought Kage, gazing up at the carved wings on column fourteen with an expression of tranquil devotion, *at least it's honest.*

It was, in its way, almost refreshing. The authorities who had outlawed brawling had said a great many beautiful things about *public safety* and *responsible coexistence* and *the welfare of all citizens, human and Bakugan alike*, and had meant by them, as far as Kage could tell, *sit down and be quiet*. Nillious said exactly what he meant. Nillious thought humans were livestock with a single useful trait, and said so, from a throne, with his tail wound round a human's legs, and did not seem to feel there was any contradiction there whatsoever.

Because there wasn't, Kage supposed. Not to a dragon. The humans out there — the ones on the streets of a hundred cities, the ones in the government offices, the ones who walked their dogs past a ruined fire station in Iceland and never once wondered what was inside — those were *this world's creatures*. Weather. Background. Useful, one in two, as you might use a horse.

The humans *in here* were a different business.

The humans in here were *owned*.

The tail shifted against the backs of his knees, a long slow ripple from root to tip, like a cat resettling in a sunny window. The mark at his throat was warm. Kage looked at the fluting.

* * *

"You haven't changed," said Drago.

His voice was very quiet and very hoarse, as though it had been dragged up from somewhere it had been buried. The growl had stopped. That was worse. Kage watched his tail and saw it had gone motionless, the tip curled tight as a fist.

"I have no need to change." Nillious's gaze finally, fully, settled on him. It was a heavy thing, that gaze; Kage could see it land, see Drago's shoulders take the weight of it. "Change is for things that are not yet finished. You, on the other hand, Dragonoid—" and the smile came back, slow and terrible, showing teeth "—you've become very *small*."

"It's an avatar."

"It suits you."

Drago's scales rose another fraction along his forearms. "You're sitting on a throne in a *fire station*, Nillious."

"I am sitting on a throne," said Nillious, unbothered. "What surrounds it is irrelevant."

"In a fire station. In *Iceland*. Calling yourself emperor of a world that—" Drago's jaw worked. "Vestroia's *gone*. You know it's gone. You were emperor of something that doesn't exist anymore. You're a former emperor of *nothing*."

"*Former*." The emperor rolled the word in his mouth as though tasting a wine that had gone off. "You always did have trouble with tenses. I am the current emperor of Vestroia, Dragonoid, because I am *here*, and I am the emperor, and where I am, Vestroia is. An emperor is not a chair. He is not a mountain or a palace or a set of borders drawn on hide. An emperor is a fact. The empire is wherever I choose to stand." His claws tapped once, lightly, on the gouged gold head of the armrest. "Today, I choose to stand in Iceland. Tomorrow, perhaps, the rest of it."

"Then you're emperor of a fire station."

"And you are not emperor of anything at all. Which of us should be more embarrassed?"

"I never *wanted*—"

"No. You only wanted me not to have it. For a very long time. Very *loudly*."

"Because you *took* it—"

"Because I was *stronger*—"

"Because you cheated—"

"Because you *lost*—"

Kage kept his face perfectly, beatifically still, and watched two apex predators — two beings older than written language, older than agriculture, older than the concept of *taxes* — conduct, in a voice like the grinding of tectonic plates, the precise argument two seven-year-olds have over who is allowed the top bunk.

*Hatchlings,* he thought. *They're hatchlings. Thousands of years old, the size of office buildings, they could level Reykjavík before lunch — and they're doing* "it's mine" "no it's mine" *in front of the help.*

He did not let his gaze drift toward Dan, who was staring from one dragon to the other with the expression of a tennis spectator at a match where the ball was on fire. He did not let it drift toward the Shadows. He kept it, very firmly, on the coastline of Italy.

And under it all, he could feel it. The joy.

That was the strange thing — the thing he had stopped finding strange. He could feel the emperor's mood the way he could feel the temperature of the room, and the emperor's mood was *good*. Not merely good: radiant. Expansive. Like a cat which has been shut in a cupboard for three weeks and has just been let out into a room full of birds. The black weight that had pressed the smoke down to the floor when they came in was gone entirely, scoured away, and in its place was something vast and warm and wickedly satisfied that hummed through the coils around Kage's knees and up through the mark at his throat. Kage was back. And the old enemy had walked through the door on his own two feet. And it was all, from where the emperor sat, the very best of days.

Nillious was not bullying Drago because he was angry. He was bullying Drago because he was *happy*. Kage had known that would happen. He had, if he was honest — which he never was, out loud — counted on it.

Double pleasure, he thought. Double damage. He made a small note to have someone send a fruit basket to the guest quarters. It would not help, but it would be on record.

* * *

Then the emperor stopped.

It happened between one word and the next. One moment the old quarrel was rising, gaining heat, Drago's voice climbing — and the next, Nillious simply lifted his chin off his knuckles, and the argument died in the air like a candle flame pinched out.

He sat up.

It was a slow movement, a coiling, the whole huge body gathering itself upright on the throne with a sound of shifting silk and the faint dry hiss of scale, and the smoke rising off his skin thickened as he moved, and the Shadows — all five — straightened by a hair.

"This," said Nillious, "is tiresome."

He looked down at Drago. And his face, for the first time since they had come in, did something Kage had not seen it do before.

It softened.

Not really. Not in any way that touched the eyes, which remained exactly what they were. But the mouth eased. The brow smoothed. The head tilted, slowly, too far, and the voice that came out of him was low and almost warm, like a banked fire, and it was the most frightening thing Kage had heard all day.

"We are the last of the old blood, you and I," the emperor said. "On this whole soft wet world, there is nothing else left that remembers. We have quarrelled for longer than these creatures have had *names*. And to what end? A wound that never closes. A scab picked at for a thousand years." His claws turned over on the armrest, palm up — an open hand, an offering, the nails curving from it like a cage. "Come. Let us heal old wounds, Dragonoid, instead of reopening them. Stand at my left hand. The other clans already kneel to me; they only lack the courtesy to say so. Together, we would rule them all."

Drago stared at him.

"You'd rule them anyway," he said slowly.

"Yes," said Nillious. "But it would be *neater*."

*Oh,* thought Kage, admiring a wing on column nine with real, pure, scholarly feeling. *That's good. That's very good. I should have written that.*

Because it was the perfect offer — he could see that at once, with the part of his mind that was always running numbers, always moving pieces across a board. It was generous. It was sincere, or sincere enough. It offered Drago everything Drago had ever wanted except the one thing he could never accept: it asked him to stand at someone's *left*. And it was made in front of witnesses, in the throne room, with the Shadows watching and the boy watching and Kage watching, so that when Drago refused — and he would refuse; it was built for refusal the way a lock is built for one key — the refusal would be the unreasonable thing. The petty thing. The hatchling thing.

"No," said Drago.

"No." The emperor let the word lie between them. His hand closed, slowly, back into a loose fist on the gold. "I wonder why."

"You know why."

"I know *exactly* why." Nillious leaned forward. Just slightly; just enough that the firelight fell differently across his face and caught in the gold streaks of his hair and turned his eyes into two coins burning at the bottom of a well. "Because you are *afraid*."

Drago's tail lashed.

"You are afraid of standing beside me," said the emperor softly, "because beside me, everyone can see the difference. You are afraid of losing. You are afraid of being *seen* losing. You always were. That is why you spent a thousand years snapping at my heels instead of going for my throat." The tilt of his head deepened, curious, predatory, almost tender. "You were afraid then, Dragonoid. You are afraid now. I can smell it on you from here."

The room was very quiet.

Even the braziers seemed to burn without sound. Kage could hear Dan breathing beside him — short, fast, furious — and he could hear, beneath it, a faint high ringing in his own ears, the kind that comes before a storm breaks.

Drago straightened.

All the lean edges of him seemed to sharpen at once. The scales along his forearms stood fully on end, a rough red armour. His pupils had gone to slits as thin as the emperor's. His tail rose behind him, slowly, until its tip stood high and rigid as a raised blade — and when he spoke, his voice was not the voice of the avatar at all. It came from somewhere much deeper and much older, and it had fire in it.

"Brawl me."

The emperor's eyebrows rose, very slightly. "Here?"

"Here. Now. You and me. If you're so sure I'm afraid—" Drago bared his teeth "—*prove it.*"

"Drago," Dan said — and then, catching up, catching fire, the way Kage had known he would, the way boys like Dan always did, "yeah. *Yeah!* You heard him! We'll take you on right now!"

Kage looked at the columns and did not smile.

*Got you,* he thought.

* * *

The emperor smiled instead.

It began at one corner of his mouth and spread, slowly, inexorably, like a crack running through ice — and it went on and on, past where a human smile would have stopped, until it showed far too many teeth, long and white and sharp, and the light caught every one of them. The rumble returned to his chest, deeper than before, so deep Kage felt it in the coils around his legs, felt it in his own back teeth, felt the gold leaf on every column in the hall begin very faintly to sing.

"Accepted," said Nillious.

The tail around Kage's legs began to move.

It unwound — slowly, reluctantly, a long warm slide of scale over the backs of his knees, his calves, his ankles, leaving behind a ghost of heat that went on lying there after the weight was gone. Kage did not look down at it. He did not need to. He felt every inch of its leaving.

And then, as the emperor rose from his throne — and he rose, and rose, and the shadow of him fell over the steps and over the Shadows and over Kage like the shadow of a cliff — he came down one step. Just one. Unhurried. The robe swinging open around him, gold dragons coiling at its hem, smoke unspooling from his shoulders. He did not look at Kage. He was looking at Drago, still, with that smile.

But his hand came down.

It came to rest on the back of Kage's neck, under the blue fall of his hair, and it was so large it covered the whole nape and curled round to the sides of his throat with room to spare. It was hot — hotter than skin should be, fever-hot, forge-hot — and Kage felt the points of the claws settle against his skin, not pressing, not breaking anything. Just *there*. Just resting. Five small, sharp facts.

Then the thumb moved.

It slid down, slow, along the left side of his throat. Over the collar of his jacket. Under it. And came to rest, with absolute precision — without looking, without searching, as though it had always known the way — on the mark.

The mark *flared*.

Heat went through Kage like a struck match. It went down his spine and out along his arms and into his fingertips and his knees and the backs of his eyes, a single bright pulse, and for one long moment he was not thinking about the columns at all; he was not thinking about anything; there was only the hand and the heat and a low voice in his blood saying *here, here, here, you are here.*

The thumb pressed once. Lightly. A seal on wax.

And lifted.

"Watch closely, little strategist," the emperor said — low, almost absently, for Kage alone; and though he still did not look down, the words went into the place under Kage's collar and stayed there. "I want you to see this."

Kage let out a breath he did not remember taking. The priest's smile was still on his face. He checked. It was still there. It had held. It always held.

*The eleventh column,* he thought distantly. *Italy. Still there. Good.*

Nillious lifted his other hand.

He did not gesture, exactly. He simply *reached* — into the air in front of the dais, into the hot gold dimness between himself and Drago — and closed his claws on nothing, and pulled.

The air tore.

It came apart with a sound like silk ripping and thunder rolling at the same time, a long jagged seam of light splitting the smoke from the floor to a height of thirty feet, and the light was the colour of the inside of a furnace. The seam widened. Its edges crackled with blue-white fire, curling back like burning paper, and through it — through the widening wound in the world — came a wind that was not a wind but a *breath*: scorching, sulphurous, dry as the inside of a kiln, carrying with it a far, low roar like an ocean made of stone.

The braziers along the hall bent their flames toward it, all at once, like courtiers bowing.

Through the portal, Kage could see black rock. Rivers of something bright and slow. A red sky, low, boiling with ash.

The heat hit him full in the face, and after twenty-one days of wind and wet and grey, of chattering teeth and frozen piercings and the sea-cold getting into the marrow of his bones — Kage's whole body, without permission, without consulting him at all, *relaxed* into it, like a cat into a sunbeam.

*Oh, wonderful,* he thought, with deep resentment. *Now I'm going to enjoy this.*

The emperor's hand left his nape. The heat of it stayed.

"Come," said Nillious, to no one, to everyone, and walked toward the burning door.


* * *

The portal did not so much open as *tear*, and on the other side of the tear there was fire.

Kage stepped through it first, because the leader of the Dragon Clan did not let guests see him hesitate, and because the emperor's tail had uncoiled from his ankles only a moment before and he could still feel its weight there, like the memory of a shackle that had been politely removed so he could walk. Heat struck him in the face. Not warmth — heat, the honest, animal heat of a world that had never been introduced to the concept of October. The air tasted of sulphur and struck flint. Ash drifted down through it slowly, fat grey flakes, the way sleet had blown outside the fire station an hour and a lifetime ago, and settled on the shoulders of his jacket, on the spikes, in his hair.

His fingers, which had been quietly dying since the portal, came back to life one by one with a prickling that was almost pain.

*Oh,* Kage thought, with deep resentment. *Oh, that's nice. That's actually nice. I hate that.*

He stood on a shelf of black basalt that ran out ahead of him like a broken jetty into an ocean of smoke. Below — far below — the ground split open in long, glowing seams, and through those seams ran lava, not red but a molten, liquid orange that brightened to white where it moved fastest, braiding and unbraiding around islands of cooling crust. Spires of rock stood up out of the fire like the fingers of something buried. The sky, where there was sky, was the colour of a bruise, and the light came from underneath, so that every face and every stone was lit the wrong way round, as though the world had been hung upside down to dry.

Dan came through behind him and immediately said, "Whoa," with the sincerity of a boy who had been handed the best day of his life on a burning plate.

Drago came through after Dan and said nothing at all.

And then the emperor stepped through the tear, and the arena noticed.

Kage felt it before he saw it — the mark under his collar flared, a hot coin pressed against the side of his throat, and the pulse beneath it skipped like a stone across water. The lava below them *moved*. Not much. A shudder along its length, a ripple running outward from the place where Nillious stood, the way water flinches from a dropped weight. The ash stopped falling for one long second and hung in the air, as though it were waiting for permission.

Nillious did not hurry. He never hurried. He walked to the end of the basalt shelf with his robe open and the gold dragons on it catching the underlight, his tail sliding behind him over the black stone with a sound like silk being dragged across a blade, and at the very edge, where the rock fell away into nothing and fire, he stopped and looked down at the pit as though it were a bath that had been drawn for him at exactly the right temperature.

Then he let go of the shape he was wearing.

Kage had seen it before. Dozens of times. He had stopped flinching around the eleventh, and he had stopped looking away around the thirtieth, and he had never — not once — stopped feeling the small, cold, sensible part of his brain stand up and quietly leave the room.

It began with the horns. They did not grow so much as *remember* how large they were; they ran backwards from the emperor's skull in ridged black arcs, splitting, branching, gold welling up through the cracks in them like light through a broken crown. The long hair lifted as if in a wind and then was not hair, was spines, was a mane of black blades edged in molten gold. The broad back bowed. Something under the skin moved in a way bones do not move, a slow, tidal shifting, and the bronze skin darkened and split into plates and the plates lifted and locked, each one the size of a door, then the size of a wall. The tail lashed once and lengthened, and kept lengthening, a black river with a bladed tip that hissed through the lava below and came up steaming. Claws hooked the edge of the shelf and the basalt cracked under them like sugar glass.

And he kept going.

Up, and up, until Kage had to tilt his head back, and then back further, and then stop pretending he could take it all in. Eleven storeys of armoured dragon rose out of the smoke, crown-crested and spined, wings half-mantled like the black sails of a ship come to burn a city. The eyes opened last. Gold. Each the size of a window. Slit down the middle with a black so deep that it seemed to have weight.

They found Kage — instantly, unerringly, the way a compass finds north — and rested on him for a moment.

The mark beneath his collar went from warm to *hot*.

*Hello,* Kage thought faintly, priest's smile perfectly in place, his heart trying to come out through his ears. *Hello, Your Majesty. You are very large today. Congratulations on being very large.*

Beside him, Dan made a sound that was not a word.

Drago had not moved during any of this. Now he looked at Dan — a long look, the kind of look that soldiers give each other before going over a wall — and then he walked to the edge of the shelf too, and his tail lashed, and he went.

Drago's change was different. Faster, cleaner, a flare of red light that unfolded from the inside out — scales the colour of fresh blood and old wine sliding over each other, white-gold plating flashing at the throat and the joints of the wings, the wings themselves cracking open like the doors of a furnace. He rose, too, and he was huge, and in any other arena on any other day he would have been the most frightening thing in it.

He came up to Nillious's chest.

The two dragons faced each other across the rivers of fire, and the arena held its breath.

*Well,* Kage thought. *This is a fair fight. In the sense that it is a fight.*

* * *

There was no signal. There never was, when Nillious brawled; the beginning of a fight was whatever moment he decided it was, and everything before that was just the universe being given a little longer to say its prayers.

The emperor's head dipped. The black spines along his back and shoulders rose — rippled — stood on end like the hackles of a vast, furious cat, and the gold in the cracks between them brightened to a white glare.

"Shuriken Spikes," Kage said, crisply, a heartbeat after it had already begun.

That was how they had always worked. Nillious acted; Kage named it. The official record of every brawl the Dragon Clan had ever fought showed a brawler calling abilities and a Bakugan obeying them, and the official record was a lovely piece of fiction that Kage was rather proud of. Teamwork. Synergy. The dream.

The spikes tore free.

Dozens of them — hundreds — black-gold blades spinning off the emperor's back in a storm, each one the length of a bus, each one screaming as it cut the air. They came down on Drago like a rain of guillotines.

"Drago, move!" Dan yelled.

Drago was already moving. He threw himself sideways, wings snapping wide, and the first wave of spikes punched into the basalt where he had stood and *detonated* — not an explosion but a shattering, the rock cracking open in starbursts, black shrapnel flying, lava gouting up through the wounds. The second wave came at an angle, hunting, and Drago rolled in the air like a hawk and let three of them pass so close they took sparks off his plating.

The third wave was going to hit. Kage saw it before Drago did — saw the spread, the pattern, the way Nillious had thrown the spikes not *at* Drago but at the places Drago would have to go — and felt, through the mark, a hot dark ripple of anticipation that was not his own.

Drago planted his feet on a crust of cooling rock, reared, and opened his jaws.

"Fire Wall!"

A sheet of flame went up from the ground like a curtain dropped in reverse — red, then orange, then a white so fierce it bleached the colour out of everything. The spikes hit it and screamed and slagged, melting in mid-flight, dripping down the face of the wall in long gold tears.

Kage felt the emperor's annoyance through the mark like a flick against the inside of his skull.

*Ah,* he thought. *He's going to take that personally.*

"Now!" Dan was bouncing on the balls of his feet, red jacket bright as a flag against the black stone, hair whipping in the hot updraft. "Drago — Burning Breath!"

Drago came through his own wall of fire.

He came out of it already breathing — a column of flame so tight and so bright that it was less fire than a lance of light, and it crossed the gap between the two dragons in an instant and struck Nillious full in the chest.

For a moment the emperor disappeared inside it.

The mark at Kage's throat *burned*. He did not stagger — the leader of the Dragon Clan did not stagger — but his hand came up, all on its own, and pressed flat against his collar, and under his palm he felt it: heat and pain and something huge recoiling, a sting like a slap across a face that had never in its long existence been slapped. For one ugly half-second he could not tell where the burn was. On the emperor's chest, or in his own.

The flame cleared.

Nillious stood in the smoke with his chest scored black and glowing, the plates there cracked and smouldering. He looked down at the damage. Then he looked at Drago.

He did not roar. That was the worst of it. A lesser dragon would have roared. Nillious only lowered his head, slowly, the great crowned skull sinking until his eyes were level with Drago's, and his lips peeled back from teeth the size of standing stones, and from deep in that armoured chest came a sound that Kage felt in his teeth and the soles of his boots and the bite on his neck: a low, rolling rumble, like a mountain clearing its throat.

It was not anger. Kage knew the emperor's anger. This was the other thing.

This was *delight*.

*Oh no,* Kage thought, *he's having fun.* And then, because his mind had a cruel streak and liked to finish sentences: *Somebody is about to be very, very dead, and on paper it's going to say "friendly match."*

The two dragons met in the middle of the pit.

The sound was enormous. It was not one sound but a hundred — armour on armour, the crack of wings, the wet hiss of lava thrown up in sheets, the shriek of claws on plating, the deep, percussive *boom* of two bodies the size of buildings slamming together. The basalt shelf under Kage's feet jumped. Rocks broke off the nearest spire and fell end over end into the fire.

"The B.A.M. Zones!" Dan shouted, already running. "Come on, come on, where are they—"

He sprinted off along the shelf, left, towards a broken causeway of crust that zigzagged out over the lava, and Kage — with the gentle, unhurried smile of a man taking a walk in a cathedral garden — went right.

* * *

The trick to a volcanic arena, Kage thought, picking his way down a slope of black scree, was the same as the trick to a contract. Everyone looked at the big print: the fire, the dragons, the screaming. Small print was what killed you.

He read the ground as he went. Basalt that was black and glassy and sharp-edged was old — cooled, solid, safe. Basalt that was grey and dull and soft-looking was crust, a skin over something moving; you could see it if you knew how to look, a faint heat-shimmer standing up off it, a slight sag, like a mattress on which something heavy has been lying. Ash that lay smooth and untouched was suspicious. Ash with cracks running through it was worse. Where the ground smelled of rotten eggs, the vents were close; where it hissed, they were closer.

Overhead, the fight went on, and it was not a fight he could stop watching, even sideways, even while placing each foot with surgical care.

Nillious did not fight like a soldier. Drago did — Drago fought with discipline and form, with feints and guards and clean, efficient strikes, a warrior who had been taught. Nillious fought like the thing soldiers had been invented to keep out. He used his size like a weapon and his weight like a second weapon. He did not dodge so much as decline to be where the blow landed. When Drago's claws raked across his shoulder, he simply turned his head and bit down on Drago's wing, and *held*, and shook, the way a dog shakes a rat — once, almost lazily — and Drago screamed and tore free with a spray of sparks and blood that hissed when it hit the lava.

Through the mark, Kage felt something warm and heavy and pleased spread through him like a drink on an empty stomach. He set his teeth against it and kept walking.

*I could die in lava,* he observed to himself, sliding three metres down a slope of loose stones on his heels, *and the black card would outlive me. Somewhere in a bank vault in Zürich my credit limit would continue to exist, unlimited, eternal, a monument. They'd bury the card. I wouldn't get a funeral but the card would.*

The slope levelled. He came out onto a wide terrace of old, black, glassy rock, and there — set into the ground at the far end, half-hidden by a curl of smoke — something pulsed green.

A B.A.M. Zone. A circle of light the width of a small room, etched into the stone in glowing lines, rising and falling like slow breathing. A cross was drawn in its centre. Health.

Kage glanced up. Nillious's chest was still scored, still smouldering where the Burning Breath had caught him, and the plates around the wound flexed oddly when he moved — a stiffness, a hitch. Not much. Nothing anyone else would see.

He crossed the terrace at a run.

He felt the zone before he reached it — felt the mark on his throat tug towards it like a hook under the skin — and then his boot came down inside the circle, and he went down on one knee and slapped his palm flat against the glowing cross.

Light went up his arm.

It was green and cold and electric, and it went up through his wrist and his elbow and into his shoulder, and when it reached the base of his neck it found the mark and *poured into it*, the way water finds a drain. For a moment Kage felt it leave him — felt something go out of him and across the air and into the colossal body above, a long, steady pull, as though a thread had been knotted to the inside of his collarbone and someone very large was reeling it in, hand over hand. His vision went white at the edges. His knee ground into the stone.

High above, the plates on the emperor's chest *closed*. The scorch-marks faded from glowing to black to nothing. Nillious threw his head back and the sound that came out of him was not a roar but a long, rising, satisfied exhale that shook ash out of the air in a grey curtain.

And then — just for a second — the golden eyes tilted down. Found him, kneeling in the green light on the terrace far below.

Kage felt that look on his skin like a hand.

*Yes,* he thought, a little dizzily, getting up, brushing ash off his knee with immense dignity. *You're welcome. I'll invoice you.*

Nillious turned back to Drago, and his tail moved.

It came round from behind him in a long, flat arc, low over the lava, so fast that the air screamed around the blade at its tip. Drago saw it coming a fraction too late; he got his arms up and his wings half-closed and the tail hit him anyway, broadside, with a sound like a cathedral bell being struck with a cathedral. He went backwards through a spire of rock. The spire exploded. He went through the one behind it, too, and that one only cracked, and he slid down its face in a shower of gravel and fire and lay for a moment in the lava with steam pouring off him.

"*DRAGO!*"

Dan's voice came from somewhere on the far side of the pit, raw and furious.

Kage turned his head. Across the rivers of fire he could see a small red figure, running flat out along the zigzag causeway — leaping a gap, landing, stumbling, running on. Dan ran the way he did everything: as if there were no such thing as the ground giving way, as if belief alone were a kind of footing.

*Golden retriever,* Kage thought, not without a certain reluctant fondness, *who learned to fight. And to run along molten crust. God help us all.*

Then Dan slid to a stop, dropped to his knees, and slammed both hands down onto a circle of light that pulsed, Kage saw, a bright, electric blue.

Speed.

* * *

Drago came up out of the lava like a struck match.

The blue light hit him from below and ran over his scales in rippling bands, and when it reached his wings he *moved* — not the controlled, disciplined movement of before but something else, a blur, a smear of red across the burning air. One moment he was lying broken at the foot of the cracked spire. The next he was above Nillious, and then beside him, and then behind him, raking claws across the black plating of the emperor's back in three long strikes so fast that the sound of them arrived all at once, a single scream of metal.

The emperor turned to meet him and Drago was not there. He was below. He was at the emperor's flank. He was gone again.

Kage watched, and his mind did what it always did, which was count.

Speed boost. Three, maybe four seconds of that before the B.A.M. began to decay. Drago would use it to land as many hits as possible, which was sensible, and the hits were landing, which was bad, but each one was shallow — claws dragging across plating, not through it. Fast and light. A wasp stinging a bear. Nillious's hide could take a hundred of those.

Unless Drago was building to something.

Kage looked at the pattern of the strikes — back, flank, back, wing, flank — and saw it: Drago was circling. Herding. Every pass drove the emperor half a step further to the right, towards a seam in the lava where the crust had broken and something whiter and hotter was welling up from underneath. A trap of his own.

*Clever,* Kage thought. *Clever, clever dragon. You've done this before.*

He opened his mouth to say something — and through the mark came a single, wordless, scalding pulse of *I know*, and he closed it again.

Nillious let himself be herded one more step. Then a second.

On the third, when Drago came round again at full speed, the emperor did not turn to meet him. He did not try to follow him. He simply stopped, and planted his claws, and *stood there*, and let Drago hit him.

Drago struck the emperor's shoulder at the speed of a falling star.

It was like watching a car hit a mountain.

The impact sent a ring of shockwave out through the smoke, flattening it, and the sound came a half-second later, a deep and terrible *crunch*. Nillious rocked back on his heels — one step, the whole of his vast weight shifting — and the plates on his shoulder caved and cracked and gold welled up in the cracks like blood. But Drago had hit him with everything: momentum, speed, the whole of his body. And the whole of his body was now pressed against the whole of Nillious's.

The emperor's arm came round him like the closing of a door.

Kage watched the blue light flicker and go out along Drago's scales. Watched the red dragon's wings beat once, twice, frantic, against the black bulk that held him. Watched Nillious lower his great crowned head until his jaws were beside Drago's ear, and felt — not heard, *felt*, through the mark, through his bones — the shape of what the emperor said to him.

He did not know the words. He did not need them. It was the tone of a creature leaning very close to something it had already decided to break.

Then Nillious threw Drago away from him like a toy flung across a nursery. Drago tumbled through the air, caught himself on his wings at the last moment, skidded across a crust of lava with his claws throwing up sparks, and stopped.

The two dragons faced each other again, chests heaving, smoke pouring from their jaws.

Both of them were hurt. Drago worse. Kage did not let his face change.

* * *

The second B.A.M. Zone lit up in the space between.

It was on a high, flat plateau of rock that stood up out of the lava like a table — a plateau roughly equidistant from where Kage stood on his terrace and where Dan crouched on his causeway, joined to each of them by a ridge. It pulsed gold. Not green, not blue. Gold, the colour of the emperor's eyes.

Power.

Kage saw it. Dan saw it. They looked at each other across the pit, and Kage smiled, a small, polite, private smile, and Dan grinned back at him, wild and fierce and utterly without guile, and they both ran.

Dan was faster. Of course he was. He ran the way he breathed, carelessly, magnificently, sixteen years old and certain of his own bones. He took his ridge in long, bounding strides, leaping from rock to rock, red jacket flying.

Kage was not faster. Kage was reading the ground.

He saw, from the corner of his eye, Dan's ridge. Saw the long dull grey stretch of it just before the plateau — dull, not glassy. Soft-looking. A slight sag in it, like a mattress. A heat-shimmer standing up off it so faint you would only notice it if you had spent an entire very long winter learning to read this arena, alone, at night, with an emperor's voice in your head telling you to do it again.

Crust. Thin crust. A skin over a hollow.

He saw it, and he knew, and he thought — very clearly and very quietly — *I should tell him.*

The thought sat there for a moment in his mind, small and bright and reasonable.

Then the mark at his throat pulsed, warm, approving, slow as a heartbeat that was not his own, and he thought instead, with the same clarity: *Dan will learn more from falling than from being told. And the clan needs him to learn. And the emperor needs to win this one cleanly, because Drago will only stay if he loses cleanly. That's strategy.*

Which was true. Which was all true.

He kept running, and did not shout, and his priest's smile did not move.

Dan reached the grey stretch at a full sprint, took one stride, two—

The ground opened under him like a mouth.

It went with a sigh more than a crack: a long section of crust folding inward, dropping, ash pouring down into the gap in a grey waterfall. Dan dropped with it. Kage saw his hands fly up, saw the red of his jacket disappear, heard Drago — far away, across the fire — make a sound that no dragon should ever make, a high, splintered howl of pure terror.

Then a cough. A yell, muffled, furious, *alive*. Kage let out a breath he did not admit he had been holding.

The crust had dropped Dan onto a ledge maybe four metres down — a lip of old basalt that ran around the inside of the hollow above a sluggish, sullen glow far below. Not lava. Not quite. Heat, and ash, and a sheer wall to climb. He was stuck. He was coughing. He was very much not going to reach a B.A.M. Zone in the next thirty seconds.

*He'll be fine,* Kage thought. *He's got that face. People with that face survive things. It's practically a law.*

Somewhere at the very back of his skull a smaller, colder voice began to say something — something about what kind of person watches a sixteen-year-old fall through a floor of molten rock and calls it a lesson, something about who he had been a year ago and whether that boy would have shouted — and the thought rose, and rose, and was almost a whole sentence—

—and the heat off the lava was really extraordinary, wasn't it, his jacket was going to smell of sulphur for a week, he'd have to have it cleaned, did the palace have a dry cleaner, of course the palace didn't have a dry cleaner, the palace had *servants*, terrifying ones, he could ask Smile, Smile would know—

He reached the plateau.

The gold zone pulsed under his boots. Up close, he could feel it humming — not a sound but a vibration, the deep and hungry thrum of power that had not yet decided whose it was. Across the pit Drago had thrown himself towards the hollow where Dan had fallen, wings spread, trying to reach his partner; Nillious had not moved. Nillious was not looking at Drago at all.

Nillious was looking at Kage.

Waiting.

The mark burned. Kage's hand was already moving. He did not remember telling it to.

He knelt in the centre of the gold, and pressed his palm flat against the stone.

* * *

It was not like the health zone. The health zone had pulled — a thread reeled in, steady and cool. This did not pull.

This *opened*.

Gold went up his arm like a flood through a broken dam, and it did not stop at the mark, it went *through* the mark — and for one long, endless second, Kage was not entirely in his own body. He was kneeling on a plateau of black stone with his hand on the ground and his head bowed. He was also eleven storeys high. He was also looking down through eyes the size of windows at a small blue-haired figure kneeling on a table of rock in a sea of fire, and the figure was — the figure was —

*Mine,* said something, in a voice that was not a voice, that was heat and weight and the slow, grinding rotation of the stars.

Kage came back into himself gasping, on both knees now, both hands braced on the stone. Gold light was pouring off him in long, rippling streamers, up and away into the air, across the pit, into the vast black body of the emperor.

Nillious *changed*.

The cracked plates on his shoulder sealed. The gold in his armour brightened until it was too bright to look at, until every seam between every scale was a line of fire. The spines along his back rose, and kept rising, longer, sharper, black blades edged in white. The crown on his head — the great branching sweep of the horns — *lit*, from the inside, gold flooding up through them until they blazed above his skull like a corona, like a black sun with a ring of fire around it.

The lava in the pit began to rise towards him. Not a wave — a tide, the whole burning surface of the arena tilting, drawn up in great slow ropes of molten light that coiled around his legs and up his body and into the ring of fire above his head.

The ash had stopped falling. The air was absolutely silent.

Drago turned from the hollow where Dan had fallen, and looked up, and Kage saw him understand.

*Eclipse Crown,* Kage thought, the name arriving from nowhere, from the mark, from inside the vast mind he had for one moment been part of, and he said it aloud because that was his job, because the official record would show a brawler calling an ability and a Bakugan obeying, and it was a lovely piece of fiction and he was proud of it:

"Eclipse Crown."

His voice came out quiet. It carried anyway.

The ring of fire above the emperor's head contracted.

All of it — the lava, the light, the gold — drew inward to a single point between the horns, a point so bright that the arena went dark around it, every shadow suddenly black as ink, every face turned to silhouette. It hung there for a heartbeat. Kage felt it hang in his own chest, a held breath that was not his.

Then Nillious lowered his head, and let it go.

It did not look like fire. It looked like the absence of everything that was not fire — a beam of gold-white so dense it bent the air around it, the edges of it rippling, the stone beneath it simply ceasing to be stone, turning to glass, turning to light. It crossed the pit without a sound. The sound came after.

Drago had time to get his wings up.

It was not enough. It could not have been enough. Kage watched the beam strike the shield of red wings and *press*, and press, and for one moment the wings held, white-gold plating flaring, and Drago's roar went up into the dark, defiant, wordless, magnificent—

—and then the wings folded, and the light went through.

Drago fell.

He fell slowly, the way huge things fall, as if the air were reluctant to let him go. Red light was coming off him in sheets, flaking away like burning paper, his true form unravelling as he dropped. He went down past the edge of the plateau, past the black spire, into the smoke. The great wings went out like a candle.

And around him, around all of them, the arena began to dissolve.

It went from the edges first. The far walls of the pit — the spires, the bruise-coloured sky — thinned to grey, and then to nothing, peeling away in long, soft strips like the pages of a burning book. The heat went with them; Kage felt it go, felt the cold of the real world creeping back in at the margins, felt his fingers start to forget that they had ever been warm. The lava dimmed. The basalt under his hands lost its texture, its weight, became a suggestion of stone and then only light.

The last thing to go was the emperor.

He stood above the ruins of the arena with his crown still burning, and he was looking down — not at the place where Drago had fallen, not at the hollow where Dan was still clawing his way up the wall — but at the plateau, at the small figure kneeling on it in the fading gold.

The mark on Kage's throat throbbed, once, slow and deep and satisfied, in time with something that was not his heartbeat at all.

He did not look away. He could not have said why.

And the fire folded closed around them both, like a hand.


* * *

The arena let go of them the way a fist lets go of something it has finished with.

Heat went first. It drained out of the air all at once, sucked back through the closing seam of the portal like breath through clenched teeth, and the throne room's resin-dark cool closed over Kage's head like water. Then sound: the lava rivers' long roar cut off mid-syllable, and in its place came the small, enormous quiet of the hall — the hiss of the braziers, the tick of cooling gold, a column of incense smoke straightening itself after the disturbance like a courtier smoothing his robes. Then weight. His boots found marble. His knees, which had spent twenty minutes sprinting across basalt that wanted to eat them, informed him that they had opinions and would be sharing them tomorrow.

*Warm,* he thought, with real grief. *I was finally warm, and I spent every second of it running.*

Drago came through last.

In the arena he had been a red storm with wings, a thing the size of a cathedral, fire in his throat and fury in every scale. Here, the portal set him down on the carpet as a young man on his hands and knees. His tail lay flat behind him, the crimson scales dulled to the colour of old brick. The plates on his forearms had gone grey at the edges, as if something had burned through the shine and left only the shape. His breath came out of him in thin ribbons of smoke, and every one of them shook.

Nillious was already on the throne.

Kage had not seen him cross the hall. He never did. One moment the portal was a wound of blue light in the air; the next it was gone, and the emperor was sitting in the dark red velvet as though he had never risen from it, one leg stretched long, one hand loose over a gold dragon's head, the black robe fallen open, smoke rising from his parted lips in a single unhurried thread. The gold in his hair caught the brazier light and held it. He was not breathing hard. Victory did not tire him. Victory fed him, and Kage could feel the meal settling, through the mark at his throat, like a heavy stone sinking into warm water.

"Drago!" Dan was already on his knees beside his partner, both hands on the plated shoulders, careless of the heat still bleeding off them. "Drago, hey — are you okay? Talk to me, buddy—"

"I'm sorry." Drago's voice was rough, all gravel and ash. He didn't lift his head. "We lost, Dan. I failed you."

"What? No. *No.*" Dan shook him a little, as if he could rattle the words back out. "You didn't fail me. I failed *you*. I ran straight into that trap like an idiot. If I'd just — if I'd looked at the ground for one second instead of—"

*Yes,* Kage agreed silently, folding his hands behind his back. *You should have looked at the ground.*

He had. He had seen the crust there before either of them reached it: a skin of black stone a shade too smooth, a shimmer of heat a shade too lazy, the subtle wrongness of a floor that was not a floor. He had taken the long way round, and he had let Dan take the short one, and he had not said a word. Strategy. Not cruelty. He was very clear on the difference. He had needed to be, for a long time now, in a palace where the difference was not always observed.

"You both failed."

Nillious did not raise his voice. He never needed to. The words came down from the dais slowly, one by one, each laid in its place like a stone on a grave, and the hall arranged itself around them. The braziers seemed to burn lower. The smoke stopped moving.

Drago's head came up. Something showed in his teeth.

"Drago failed," the emperor went on, in the tone of a man reading an inscription he finds faintly boring, "because he fought with his memory instead of his body. He fought a battle from very long ago. He lost that one, too." The amber eyes slid, without hurry, to Dan. The pupils were thin as knife-cuts. "And the boy failed because he believes courage is a map." A pause, exactly long enough to hurt. "It is not. It is only a way of falling faster."

Dan flushed red to the ears. Drago's tail cracked once against the marble like a whip, and five figures around the dais turned their heads toward the sound at precisely the same moment, at precisely the same angle, the way a field of grass turns before a wind. Nobody else in the hall moved. The Tall One, at the emperor's right hand, did not even look; he simply let his long pale fingers uncurl at his side, and that was somehow worse.

Kage stepped forward before anybody could decide to find out what the uncurled fingers were for.

He found the priest's smile where he had left it — serene, generous, forgiving every sin in the village except yours — and put it on. His cheeks, still stiff from Iceland, objected. He overruled them.

"That," he said warmly, "was a good brawl. Seriously. You two pushed us harder than half the clan does on a good day." He spread his hands, the leather of his jacket creaking. "Challenge us whenever you want. Any day, any arena. We'll be here." The smile widened by exactly the right degree. "And — welcome to the Dragon Clan."

Dan's grin came back the way a fire comes back when you blow on the coals: not all at once, but stubbornly. "Yeah," he said, getting to his feet, hauling Drago up with him. "Yeah. Next time, we're gonna win."

"Next time," Kage agreed, with the sincerity of a man who had already planned next time down to the footing.

Drago didn't smile. Drago looked at Kage — the same look he had given him on the wet grass outside the station, three hours and a whole climate ago. Nostrils flared. Head tilted, a fraction too far for a human neck. As if he were trying to read a word written underneath another word, and almost had it.

Kage held the look, pleasantly, until Drago's gaze dropped to his collar.

Then something curled around his ankle, warm and heavy and absolutely certain, and Kage stopped thinking about Drago at all.

* * *

The emperor was not finished.

Kage had known he wouldn't be. Kage had known it from the instant the tail found him, from the slow luxurious stretch of it across the floor, the black scales sliding past the hem of his trousers, the gold edging brushing his boot like a cat that weighed as much as a car. That was not the tail of a creature preparing to stand up. That was the tail of a creature settling in.

"Fire Wall," Nillious said, contemplatively. He turned one clawed hand over on the armrest and examined the nails as if Drago's defeat were written on them. "You always loved that technique. It was beautiful in Vestroia, too. On the day it did not hold."

"Don't," Drago said.

"You still drop your left wing before you breathe." The emperor's voice was almost gentle. Almost. "Your sire did the same. I told him once. He did not listen either."

*Please,* Kage thought, keeping his eyes fixed on the nearest column — such a column, really, look at the fluting, look at the gold leaf in the grooves, a masterpiece, an absolute triumph of load-bearing — *Your Radiant Majesty. Light of the Dragon Throne. Sun of Vestroia. Lord of Fire and Shadow and Every Single Spoon in This Building. I am begging you. You won. He knows you won. The walls know you won. End this. Let's go to the chambers. I want to lie down and never wake up again.*

He meant sleep. He meant twelve hours of it, uninterrupted, face down in a pillow that wasn't actively being shredded. That was all he meant.

"You took the throne," Drago snarled, "when there was nothing left to sit on."

"I took the throne," said Nillious, "because it was mine. Everything I want is mine. That is what *emperor* means, hatchling. You would know that if you had ever wanted anything with your whole body." The amber eyes moved, idly, the length of the hall. "Instead you want to be *good*. It is a small, sad wanting. It fits in one hand."

The tail tightened around Kage's ankle, briefly. Not a warning. Kage knew the difference by now — knew the grammar of that tail better than he knew the grammar of his own native language. That had been punctuation. A satisfied full stop at the end of a sentence the emperor had enjoyed saying.

Kage was vibrating. Not visibly. Visibly, he was a statue of polite attention; visibly, he had been carved out of the same marble as the floor and placed at the foot of the dais by a sculptor with a fondness for spikes. But somewhere under his ribs a small, exhausted version of himself was hopping from foot to foot like a child outside a locked bathroom, and it had been doing so for six minutes.

*He's going to bring up the Battle of the Crimson Ridge,* Kage thought. *He's going to bring up the Crimson Ridge, and then Drago is going to bring up the Moon Gate, and then they'll be at it until breakfast, and I will die standing up, and they'll leave me here as a decorative feature. Visitors will ask about me. "That's the strategist," the Tall One will say. "He was very thorough."*

"Drago," Dan said, quietly, and put a hand on his partner's arm.

That was all. Just a name and a hand. But Drago's tail went still, and the snarl folded back into his mouth, and he lowered his eyes.

Nillious watched this with an expression Kage had never been able to name. Not contempt. Something colder and more interested — the look of a collector who has noticed, in someone else's cabinet, a piece he does not yet own.

"Enough," the emperor said.

It was not loud. It did not need to be. The word went through the hall like a blade drawn slowly from a sheath, and the braziers leaned toward it.

"Take them to the guest wing." He did not look at the Shadows when he spoke; the Shadows did not need to be looked at. "Feed them. The boy smells of fear and ash. Have it washed off him." A slow blink — the first Kage had seen from him all evening. "And the hatchling's wounds. See to them. I want him whole the next time I break him."

Smile was there before the sentence ended. He was always there before the sentence ended. One moment the space at Dan's elbow was empty and lit by brazier-glow; the next it held a tall dark-skinned figure with small curled horns and white-gold eyes, bowing courteously, a hand extended toward the doors, his face arranged in that gentle, patient, endlessly kind smile that Kage had privately decided was the single most frightening expression in the known universe, and possibly in Vestroia as well.

Doesn't-Blink took Drago's other side. He didn't touch him. He didn't have to. Drago walked.

"See you tomorrow, Kage!" Dan called over his shoulder, already halfway to the doors, already talking to Smile about whether the guest wing had a TV, whether there was food, what kind of food dragons even *ate*, and did they have pizza, because if they didn't have pizza he wasn't sure this was going to work out—

Smile listened. Smile nodded. Smile tilted his head — not the courteous tilt of a host but further, too far, the angle of something that has a longer neck than it is currently wearing — and his nostrils flared, once, delicately, at the air around the boy. Then at the air around Drago. Then, thoughtfully, at the boy again.

Kage knew that look.

He had seen it this afternoon in the sparring corner, on the faces of the dragons who leaned against the wall and watched their humans fight; he had seen it in the lab, on the ash-grey dragon whose tail curled round the leg of Toma's chair; he had seen it in a hundred corridors over a hundred days and filed every single instance under *normal*, under *dragons*, under *not my department*. It was not hunger, exactly. It was the look of a customs officer reading a seal. *Whose is this. How strongly. Is it marked.* Dan smelled of Drago, Kage supposed — of Drago and of nothing else, a claim written in scent and nowhere deeper — and every dragon in the palace would know it by morning, the way a city knows which houses have no locks.

*I should tell Dan—*

—that breakfast was at seven, and the guest wing had heated floors, and the showers took a full minute to run hot but were worth the wait, and that if he wanted pizza he should ask the kitchens directly, because the kitchens were run by an elderly Dragonoid named Ashka who took requests very seriously and had once, on Kage's offhand mention of tiramisu, produced a tiramisu the size of a coffee table. Yes. He'd tell him tomorrow. Tomorrow was good. Dan would need someone to explain the floors.

The doors closed behind them. The Tall One drew the bolt with one long finger, and the sound it made was very small and very final, like a full stop at the end of a long, long sentence.

* * *

Then it was only the two of them, and the twins.

Left and Also Left had taken up position by the far columns, one on each side of the dais, matching from the tips of their braids to the angle of their silence. Kage looked away from them for exactly one heartbeat, and when he looked back they had swapped. He had stopped finding this upsetting several weeks ago. Now he just found it rude.

Nillious rose.

He did it slowly, as he did everything, as though the world had been informed in advance and would wait. The robe slid back across his shoulders. The horns caught the light — black, ridged, curving back from his brow like a crown that had been broken and then grown back worse. He came down the steps of the dais one at a time, and the tail did not uncoil from Kage's ankle as he came; it simply shortened, drawing in like a rope being reeled, so that by the time the emperor reached the bottom step he was standing close enough for Kage to feel the heat coming off him in a wall.

He bent his head.

He breathed in.

It was not subtle. It was never subtle. A long, deep, unhurried inhale through the nose, his face an inch from Kage's hair, the breath going in and in as though he intended to keep it — and then, low in that enormous chest, a sound began. Not a purr. Kage had spent his first week in the palace calling it a purr in his head, and then he had heard what an actual Dragonoid sounded like when it was pleased, and he had retired the word forever out of respect for cats. It was a rumble. A deep, rolling, seismic vibration that he felt in his own sternum, in his teeth, in the soles of his feet through the marble. The sound a mountain might make if it were very satisfied with where it was standing.

"You were gone," said Nillious, into his hair, "nineteen days."

"Twenty-one, Your Majesty." Kage kept his eyes on the column. It was still a very good column.

"Nineteen in which I could not smell you at all." The rumble deepened, roughened, gained an edge like a blade drawn across stone. "Two in which you smelled of the boy. And of the cold. And of *Drago*." The last word came out with a curl of smoke behind it.

"I brought them," Kage said. "As you ordered."

"You did."

A hand found the back of his neck. It covered the whole of his nape easily, fingers curving round to the side of his throat; the claws rested on his skin without pressing, five points of warning so familiar they had stopped feeling like warning and started feeling like weather. The thumb settled, unerringly, over the mark under his collar.

Heat went through him.

Not pain. It had never been pain, not since the first night, and he did not think about the first night — his thoughts went toward it and slid off, smooth as a palm over silk, and found something softer to rest on instead. Warmth. That was all. The warmth spread from the place under the emperor's thumb, down through his shoulders, into his arms, out to the fingers that had been numb since the portal spat him out in Iceland. It unknotted the cold that had lodged in his spine somewhere between the burned grass and the station doors. It went into his head and made everything there quiet and orderly and simple, a desk swept clean.

*Good,* said the warmth. *Good. Done. Home.*

"Come," said the emperor.

* * *

The way to the imperial wing ran through the moon gallery.

Kage had walked it a hundred times and it still stopped his breath, which annoyed him on principle. A long colonnade of carved sandstone, warm even in moonlight, every pillar worked from base to capital in coils and petals and patient geometries no human hand had cut. Overhead, the ceiling rose into a lattice so fine it looked like frost. To the right, a balustrade of pierced stone, and on every post a candle burning in glass, small steady flames in a long receding line like a procession of very polite ghosts. And beyond the balustrade, framed by scalloped arches, a full moon hung over a dark river and a hill of black trees — a moon that had no business being there, over a river that did not exist in Iceland, in a night that the real sky, on the other side of the front doors, had not yet got round to.

He had stopped asking which sky was real. The windows lied, and they lied beautifully, and he had decided that was their right.

The moonlight fell through the lattice and lay across the floor in a net of shadows — thousands of small dark shapes, petals and stars and interlocking curves, a pattern that moved, very slightly, as clouds crossed the false moon. Kage walked across it. The emperor's hand stayed on his nape. The tail trailed behind them, heavy and slow, and the shadows of the lattice slid over its scales and over Kage's boots and over the hem of the black-and-gold robe, and to Kage they looked like lace.

Behind them, at a distance measured to the inch, came Left and Also Left. Their footsteps made no sound at all.

*So,* Kage thought, because he was a strategist and strategists did their accounts at the end of the day. *Let's review.*

He had left this palace twenty-one days ago with a direct order and a black card. He had crossed four countries and one very unpleasant ferry. He had found a boy with the attention span of a firework and a dragon with the trust issues of a fortress, and he had smiled at both of them until his face hurt, and he had brought them home through a door in a ruin in the cold. He had sat them on cushions under a sky that wasn't there. He had poured them tea. He had told them about clans and freedom and how the authorities were wrong, and every word of it had been true, which was the best kind of lie. He had walked them into this hall and let them see the throne, and he had let Drago say the thing about the brawl, because Drago was always going to say the thing about the brawl. And then he had let Dan run toward the bright spot on the ground, because the ground before the bright spot was a trap, and the trap was a lesson, and the lesson was *you need us*.

The Dragon Clan was larger tonight by one boy and one Dragonoid. The other clans would hear of it by morning. The Insects would sulk. The Avians would send a congratulatory delegation with a gift that was secretly an insult. The Dinos wouldn't notice for a week. Kage had a meeting at nine with the lab about B.A.M. calibration, a report due on the Aquatic Clan's water-arena proposal, a stack of tribute ledgers he had been ignoring since before he left, and a first training session to plan for a boy who believed courage was a map.

It had been, by any measure, a good day.

A very good day. A productive day. He had been useful.

And it was strange — it was genuinely, faintly funny — how long it had taken him to notice any of it. The way the dragons had changed. The way they had started standing closer. The way they watched. A year ago he had been so busy being clever, so busy running the clan and drafting strategies and arguing with the lab about ability-card latency, that he had not noticed the emperor's eyes following him across rooms. He had not noticed the Shadows at the edge of every corridor he walked down. He had not noticed Smile at all, for three entire months, which in retrospect was either a tribute to Smile's professionalism or a damning indictment of Kage's peripheral vision. In retrospect the signs had been there. In retrospect the signs had been the size of a building, with an emperor sitting on top of it, waving.

And now — now he lived here. Now the emperor's wing was his wing. Now he had a black card with no limit — *what is a limit?* — and that had not been a joke, because the emperor did not make jokes; it had been a genuine question about a genuinely foreign concept, like asking a human what the word for the colour of ultraviolet was. Now he had money. Real money. Rent-was-never-going-to-be-a-problem-again money. Now he could buy anything, anywhere, he could take that card and walk out through the fire station and buy a ticket to—

—a better coat. That was the thing. A proper coat for Iceland, a long one with a hood, something with down filling and a fur collar, so that the next time the emperor sent him out to fetch something from the cold he wouldn't arrive back with ears like frozen shrimp. He'd look into it tomorrow. After the lab meeting. Before the ledgers.

The lattice shadows slid over his boots. Lace. Lovely, really.

At the end of the gallery the stone changed to wood: dark, polished, old as a forest, inlaid with gold that had been worn soft under centuries of claws. The air changed too. Warmer. Heavier. Cedar and resin and the faint, persistent, mineral scent of smoke that lived in the walls of the emperor's wing, a smell Kage could no longer separate from the smell of sleep. The corridor narrowed and rose, lantern-lit, dark red silk on the walls, and the doors at the end of it were twice his height, carved from a single slab of black wood and banded in gold, the handles shaped as two dragons biting each other's tails.

Kage had once, in his first week, tried to estimate how much one of those handles would fetch at auction. He had come up with a number so large he had needed to go and lie down.

The Tall One was standing beside the doors.

Kage had not seen him leave the throne room. Kage had not seen him pass them in the gallery. There was no other route. The Tall One simply stood there, silver hair falling to his waist, long pale horns brushing the lantern-light, eyes lowered in perfect deference to his emperor — and then, briefly, lifted, to rest on Kage.

There was nothing in them. No warmth, no malice, no curiosity. They were the eyes of a creature confirming that an item was where it had been left.

He bowed. He opened the doors.

Smoke and cedar and the scent of torn silk rolled out to meet them, and the low gold light of the chambers, and beyond it — past the carved screens, past the braziers burning down to embers, past the table where Kage's laptop still sat open on a page of ledgers he had abandoned three weeks ago — the nest. A dark sea of pillows and blankets, crimson and black and gold, heaped higher than a bed had any right to be, and half of them scored with long pale lines where claws had raked through velvet in sleep. A few feathers still drifted in the warm air, stirred by the opening doors, turning slowly, settling.

Kage felt his whole body go heavy at the sight of it, as though someone had cut a string.

*Twelve hours,* he thought. *Face down. No dreams. No Drago. No columns.*

The hand on his nape tightened, just slightly, and steered him forward. He went. He was already reaching up to unbuckle the first spiked strap of his jacket, already thinking about the lab at nine and Dan's training and the Avians' inevitable insult-gift and whether Ashka could be persuaded to make the tiramisu again, as the doors began to swing shut behind them and the emperor's tail slid up from his ankle to wind, slow and warm and certain, around his calf, and drew him over the threshold into the heat—
